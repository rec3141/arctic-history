The Breadalbane was not an exploring ship and nobody died in her. She matters
because she is still there, and because she is in better condition than any
other wooden wreck anyone has found in any ocean.

## A wine trader in the ice

She was built in Scotland in 1843: a three-masted barque of about forty metres,
square-rigged, generously built, of the sort that carried wine, wool and grain
between European ports by the hundred. In the spring of 1853 the Admiralty
chartered her as a transport, to carry stores north to the five-ship squadron
[[person:Edward Belcher]] had in the archipelago looking for Franklin. She
sailed in company with HMS Phoenix, [[person:Edward Augustus Inglefield]]
commanding, and reached the depot at Beechey Island in August. The
[[artifact:wrecks-disasters-035|only picture of her]] is a wood engraving in
the Illustrated London News of 22 October 1853, made after she was lost: the
two ships lie off [[place:Cape Riley]], boats going in with stores.

## Fifteen minutes

Both ships were made fast to a driving floe about half a mile south of
[[place:Beechey Island]] when the pack came in from the offing. Belcher, who
was at Beechey and wrote it down, is exact:

> Early on Sunday morning, the 21st of August, a little after four, the first
> warning of 'nip' was noticed on board the 'Breadalbane,' by the groaning of
> beams, creaking of bulkheads, etc., which drove the crew on the ice in rather
> unprepared garb. No time was afforded to save anything. In about fifteen
> minutes the vessel was destroyed.

([[artifact:wrecks-disasters-029|the passage in full]].) The agent scrambled
onto the floe more or less naked. All twenty-one of her people were taken up by
the Phoenix, which was badly nipped herself and survived it. The coal the
Breadalbane had landed at Cape Riley stayed on the shingle and could not be
recovered. A year later a shore party found a piece of her teak on a beach
fifty-five miles to the north.

The hour is disputed. Belcher was present and says a little after four in the
morning of Sunday 21 August. George McDougall, writing at second hand in the
Resolute far to the west, describes the same event as happening on the night of
the 20th. [[person:Joseph Beverley MacInnis]], who found the wreck, says just
after midnight. Belcher's is the eyewitness account and is the one used here.

## Three seasons of sonar

MacInnis began looking for her in August 1978, prompted by the research of
Stuart Hodgson and Maurice Haycock and working from the eyewitness accounts
held at the Scott Polar Research Institute. Beechey Island is a small place and
the search area was still large: a ship secured to a *driving* floe is not
necessarily lost where she was last seen anchored. It took three seasons. On
[[artifact:wrecks-disasters-002|13 August 1980]] she came onto the side-scan
sonar record with her hull intact and two of her masts still standing.

She lies upright in about a hundred metres of water. In 1981, with the Canadian
Coast Guard and the National Geographic Society, a remotely operated vehicle
took the first colour photographs: the bow, the masts, the rudder and anchor,
the big wheel, and a small cabinet on the deckhouse still holding her compass
and a signal light. The copper sheathing on her hull looked new. In 1983 divers
worked on her in the WASP atmospheric suit, a piece of equipment developed
because a hundred metres under moving sea ice is beyond ordinary diving.

## Why a wreck is a scientific instrument

Cold water, darkness, low oxygen and no shipworm are why she looks like that.
For the people aboard this ship the Breadalbane is a benchmark of a rare kind:
a wooden structure of known construction and known date, sitting in known water
under known ice since 21 August 1853. What has happened to her timber, her
copper and the organisms living on her is a controlled measurement of a hundred
and seventy years of Arctic seabed conditions, and one that can be repeated.
She is also a marker for the ice itself: the floes that ground and scour in
Barrow Strait pass above her masts.

She is a Territorial Historic Site and access to her is controlled. That is not
bureaucratic caution: an intact 1843 hull with her fittings in place is a
single, unrepeatable object, and the first thing that damages such a site is
usually enthusiasm.
