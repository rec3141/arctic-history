The British whale fishery lost more ships in [[place:Melville Bay]] than in all
other Arctic waters put together. This page is about why, and about the four
worst years.

## The geography of the trap

The bowhead grounds the fleet wanted were on the west side of Baffin Bay, and
the way to them was up the Greenland coast, through Melville Bay, into the
[[place:The North Water|North Water]] and then across. Melville Bay is a great
open bight between about 74 and 76 degrees north where the land-fast ice runs
out from the shore and the moving pack of the bay presses against it. A ship
worked north in the lane between the two, warping and tracking, sometimes sawing
a dock in the fast ice to lie in. If the pack came onshore while she was in that
lane there was nowhere at all to go. The ice did not need to be thick. It needed
only to move.

Ships went in because the season was short and the alternative was to go home
empty. The fleets were large: about 150 sail from Britain alone in some of these
years.

## The bad years

Robert Goodsir, surgeon, sailing in 1849 in search of his own brother in the
Franklin expedition, sets the tally out plainly:

> In 1819, fourteen ships were thus entirely lost; in 1821, eleven; in 1822,
> seven; but the year 1830 was peculiarly disastrous.

[[artifact:wrecks-disasters-019|1819]] and the years after it were bad enough to
change insurance practice. Nothing matched 1830.

## The Baffin Fair, June 1830

The fleet was beset from 19 June 1830 at about 75 degrees 10 minutes north, 60
degrees 30 minutes west, some forty miles south of [[place:Cape York]]. From the
evening of the 24th the floes began to close. Goodsir names the Baffin, the
Achilles, the Ville de Dieppe and the Rattler as broken up inside a quarter of an
hour; the Progress of Hull went under an iceberg; the Oenhope was a total wreck.
Nineteen ships were destroyed and twelve seriously damaged, a loss put at
142,600 pounds.

Not one man was killed.

About a thousand seamen were suddenly homeless on the ice with nothing to do and
nobody's ship's discipline to answer to. They broached the wrecks' spirit rooms,
built bonfires of wreck timber, and held what the fleet ever afterwards called
the Baffin Fair:

> The various scenes of ludicrous frolic, the joyous shoutings of the British
> sailors, and the dances and songs of the French suggested the idea of a large
> fair; some even gave it the name of Baffin Fair.

([[artifact:wrecks-disasters-018|the passage]].) A postal service, the "North
Mail", ran between the northern and southern parties of wrecked men.

The sources disagree about the count and about what to call it. Lindsay says
nineteen ships and 140,000 pounds, on 19 June, and explains the name. Goodsir,
quoting the *Edinburgh Cabinet Library*, says nineteen lost and twelve damaged,
142,600 pounds, beset from the 19th and crushed from the 24th. Markham says
twenty were lost and gives more names — the Princess of Wales and the Letitia
cut in two, the Resolution of Peterhead holed through the counter, the Laurel
and the Hope squeezed flat, the brig Commerce sunk. Nineteen out of a fleet of
ninety-one is the usual modern figure.
[[artifact:wrecks-disasters-017|The gazetteer entry]] carries all three versions.

## The winter of 1835-36: the disaster with the deaths

The 1830 loss was expensive and nearly bloodless. Five years later the opposite
happened.

Winter came a month early to Davis Strait in 1835. On
[[artifact:wrecks-disasters-020|11 October]] the Alfred of Hull, getting clear
to the south, left eleven ships fast in the ice between 69 and 70 degrees north
and 60 and 65 degrees west, with upwards of six hundred men aboard: the Jane,
William Torr, Abram, Dordon, Duncombe, Harmony, Viewforth, Middleton, Lady Jane,
Grenville Bay and Norfolk. None of them was provisioned for a winter, and none
of them had lime juice enough.

What followed was slow. The ships drifted south in the pack through the winter
while their people ate short and got scurvy. The Middleton was lost on 15
November on the west side of the strait. Twenty-two of the Lady Jane's crew died
before she was released; of her survivors and the Mary Francis's, only ten or
twelve could still walk. The Viewforth came out at the end of January in 59
degrees 40 minutes north with seven men fit for duty and landed sixteen sick at
Stromness. The Grenville Bay was carried through Hudson Strait and out again
before she got free off Labrador on 16 December. Three more ships had already
been lost earlier that season: the Isabella off the Whalefish Islands on 18 May,
the William Lee off Dark Head on 4 July, the Mary Francis crushed on 26 August.

The worst of it was the [[artifact:wrecks-disasters-021|William Torr]]. She was
last seen on 16 October forty-five miles south-east of [[place:Cape Searle]],
drifting south, and was crushed off Cape Fry some time in December. Twenty-two
men too ill to travel were put ashore into the care of Inuit families, who fed
and sheltered them; all twenty-two died anyway, of scurvy and starvation nobody
on that coast could have cured. A party under Captain Smith set off over the ice
to reach the Jane and was never heard of again. Lloyd's posted her lost in
October 1836. Everything known about her end came back second-hand, through Inuit
testimony passed to later whalers — which is worth stating plainly: the only
witnesses to the largest loss of life in the Baffin Bay fishery were Inuit, and
the record we have exists because they told it.

## HMS Cove: the first relief expedition

Public alarm was enough that the Admiralty did something it had never done for
merchant seamen. [[person:James Clark Ross]] took HMS Cove out of Hull on
[[artifact:wrecks-disasters-022|6 January 1836]] to find the beset whalers — in
January, into the North Atlantic. A storm on the 28th carried away his bowsprit
and he put back into Stromness on 5 February. He sailed again on the 24th,
reached the pack edge at 54 north on 7 April, worked the ice all spring, and
searched into July for the William Torr without finding a trace of her. He came
into Hull on 31 August 1836. He rescued nobody: every ship that was going to get
free had done so before he arrived, and the rest were gone. HMS Terror and HMS
Erebus were fitted out as a second relief force and never sailed — the same two
ships that would be abandoned off King William Island twelve years later.

## What it cost, and who paid

The fishery ran on this arithmetic for a century: a full ship paid for several
lost ones. The men who drowned or starved were not insured. The Inuit of the
Greenland and Baffin coasts absorbed the wrecked crews, fed them, and in the
William Torr's case buried them.
