The people who wrote the accounts in this gazetteer were not engineers, but
they were exact observers of one thing, because their lives turned on it: what
moving ice does to a wooden hull. Read together their descriptions are
consistent enough to be useful, and they explain why the losses in this region
look the way they do.

## The nip

A nip is not a collision. It is the closing of two ice masses with a ship
between them, and the force comes from wind and tide acting on floes measured
in square kilometres. The ship is the softest thing in the system.

Every account describes the same sequence and in the same order. First noise:

> Early on Sunday morning, the 21st of August, a little after four, the first
> warning of 'nip' was noticed on board the 'Breadalbane,' by the groaning of
> beams, creaking of bulkheads, etc.

That is Belcher on the [[artifact:wrecks-disasters-029|Breadalbane]]. The
timbers work before anything breaks, which is why crews so often get off. Then
the ice climbs rather than crushes. Lieutenant Garlington, watching the
[[artifact:wrecks-disasters-039|Proteus]] die off Cape Sabine thirty years
later:

> The pressure against the ship's sides was incalculable; the heavy ice, from 5
> to 7 feet in thickness, as it came against her sides under this powerful
> strain, broke and rafted up on the floe amidships and astern, but still there
> were no signs of giving way. At 4.30 p.m. the starboard rail gave way with a
> crash.

Then failure, and it is fast and local: the bulwarks go, then the ice comes
through the side at one point — in the Proteus's case into the starboard coal
bunker — and the planking begins to lift and the seams to open. The Breadalbane
was destroyed in about fifteen minutes; the Proteus took from 2.45 in the
afternoon, when she stopped moving, until 6.50, when the last man left her, and
sank at 7.15 on an even keel.

Note what the interval is for. In both cases the ship stayed up long enough for
her people to get onto the ice and, in the Proteus, to throw stores overboard —
though a third of those were lost because they were pitched too near the hull
and were drawn under as she went.

## The three shapes of a besetment

**Held and released.** The ordinary case. A ship is frozen in, drifts with the
floe, and comes out when the floe opens. The eleven whalers caught in Davis
Strait on [[artifact:wrecks-disasters-020|11 October 1835]] mostly came out; the
[[artifact:wrecks-disasters-038|Grenville Bay]] was carried the length of the
strait, into Hudson Strait and out again, and was free off Labrador on 16
December. The ship survives. The crew may not: what kills in a long besetment is
scurvy, not pressure.

**Held and crushed.** The floe does not open, and closes instead. This is the
Breadalbane, the Proteus, and nineteen ships in Melville Bay in
[[artifact:wrecks-disasters-017|June 1830]].

**Held and abandoned.** The hull is sound and the people are not. This is the
whole of [[page:the-ships-left-in-the-ice]] — five ships left in the
archipelago in 1853 and 1854 because their crews could not stand another
winter, and one of them, the [[artifact:wrecks-disasters-030|Resolute]], sailed
out of the pack by herself and was found twelve hundred miles away, dry below.

The third case is why a beset ship is never written off lightly. It is also why
[[artifact:wrecks-disasters-003|HMS Investigator]] is still recognisably a ship
on the floor of Mercy Bay, and why the
[[artifact:wrecks-disasters-001|Breadalbane]] is intact under a hundred metres
of cold water off Beechey Island.

## Where the losses cluster, and why

Look at the map for this topic and the dots are not scattered evenly. They sit
in four kinds of place:

1. **Where a moving pack meets fast ice** — [[place:Melville Bay]] above all.
   The lane between the two is the only road north on that coast and there is no
   sea room in it.
2. **Where a tide runs through a constriction** — [[place:Smith Sound]] and
   Kane Basin, which between them account for the Proteus, the Polaris, and
   the deaths at Camp Clay that followed from the first of those.
3. **Where the water shoals without warning** — Simpson Strait, and the reef
   off [[place:Kinngait]]. Both modern losses in this gazetteer are of this
   kind, and neither had anything to do with ice.
4. **Where an aircraft must get down at a place with no alternate** — an
   approach through weather onto high ground at [[place:CFS Alert]], twice; a
   whiteout approach at [[place:Cape Dyer]] in 1967; two Twin Otters lost on
   the same runway at [[place:Iqaluit]] a year apart in 1978 and 1979; and the
   thawing mud strips at [[place:Isachsen]] and [[place:Qausuittuq]] that wrote
   off two aircraft in the spring of 1949. Most of the aviation entries in this
   gazetteer happened on an approach or a landing rather than in flight, and in
   most of those the crew could not see the ground they were descending
   towards.

## What this is worth to a ship working here now

Three things, none of them romantic.

The first is that the historical loss positions are a real dataset. They record
where ice actually closed on a hull, in what month, in years whose ice
conditions can be reconstructed from the same journals. They are a
century-and-a-half baseline against which present ice behaviour can be compared,
and they were collected by people with no reason to exaggerate.

The second is that the failure mode has not changed. A modern ice-strengthened
hull resists the nip far better than the Breadalbane's oak, but the pressure a
closing floe can exert is unchanged, and the sequence — noise, rafting, local
failure, water — would be recognisable to Belcher.

The third is the boring one, and it is the one that has actually caused the
losses of the last eighty years. The Nascopie hit a rock nobody had charted. The
Hanseatic hit a rock everybody had charted, because a buoy had moved 200 metres
over the winter and the bridge team trusted it instead of their own plan. Boxtop
22 flew into a hill on an approach the squadron flew twice a year. Ice is not
what most often goes wrong up here. Assumptions are.
