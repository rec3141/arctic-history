[[place:CFS Alert]] sits at 82 degrees 30 minutes north on the northern tip of
Ellesmere Island, 817 kilometres from the Pole. Everything there has been flown
in since 1950, and the two worst days in its history are both aviation
accidents on the supply run, forty-one years apart.

## 31 July 1950: the Lancaster

The station was being built by twelve men working from an ice runway on Dumbbell
Bay. Aircraft could not land on the site, so stores were parachuted. Shortly
after noon on 31 July 1950 a Lancaster of 405 Squadron came up from Thule with
spark plugs for a broken bulldozer and made a run over the half-built camp. The
supply parachute fouled the aircraft's elevator. The Lancaster dived into the
ground about 450 metres west of the camp and exploded. The men on the ground
ran to the smoke and found no one alive.

Nine died. Among them was Charles J. Hubbard of the United States Weather
Bureau, the man who had conceived the network of Arctic weather stations and
personally chosen every site in it, including this one; and Dr D. W. Kirk, a
Canadian ice observer. The other seven were RCAF aircrew. They were buried at
the north end of the airstrip, looking over Cape Belknap and the Arctic Ocean,
because Hubbard's family asked that he be buried where he died. It is the
northernmost cemetery in the world.

## 30 October 1991: Boxtop 22

Operation Boxtop is the twice-yearly airlift that keeps Alert fuelled. On 30
October 1991 flight 22, a CC-130 Hercules of 435 Transport and Rescue Squadron,
tail number 130322, came up from Thule with eighteen people aboard and 3,400
litres of diesel in the cargo bay. It was the polar night.

On final approach at about 1640 the pilot flying lost sight of the runway.
Radar contact and radio went at the same moment. The aircraft struck a rocky
slope about sixteen kilometres south of the station. The crew of another
Hercules also inbound for Alert saw the fires and fixed the position.

Four people were killed outright: Captain Judy Trepanier, Master Warrant
Officer Tom Jardine, Warrant Officer Robert Grimsley and Master Corporal Roland
Pitre. Fourteen were alive on the hillside.

What followed is why this crash is remembered. Twelve of the survivors crowded
into the broken tail section, some of them soaked in diesel; two were too badly
injured to be moved and a shelter was built around them where they lay. The
temperature was between minus 20 and minus 30 with a high wind, in the dark.
[[person:Wilma de Groot]], an army doctor travelling as a passenger and injured
herself, treated the wounded and then called the survivors' names aloud every
hour or two through the night so that nobody fell asleep in the cold.

The first two overland rescue parties turned back — weather, and ground that no
vehicle could cross. A search and rescue Hercules from 413 Squadron at Greenwood
was overhead within about eight hours and could not jump: high wind, cloud, no
visibility. When the SAR techs finally did jump, most of their equipment and
medical stores were blown away and lost on landing. They worked with what they
had. A second stick jumped after them and a third overland party got through
with supplies.

[[person:John Couch]], the Hercules's pilot, answered the roll for several hours
and then died of hypothermia, having spent the time working to keep the others
alive. The survivors were lifted out by a CH-135 Twin Huey that had been flown
north inside another Hercules; the dead, the overland rescuers and the remaining
SAR techs came out on United States Pave Hawks from Elmendorf. It had been more
than thirty hours.

Thirteen of the eighteen came out alive. They were together in the station
dining room, turned into a field hospital, when de Groot called the names one
last time; it was the only time all thirteen were in one room, and they were
flown south the same day.

A cairn of local slate, seven feet high, was built by the station's construction
engineering section near the runway, about twenty kilometres from the crash
site, and dedicated on 24 June 1993. Its bronze plaque carries the five names in
English and French. An identical plaque was dedicated on a second cairn on 30
October 2016.

## What the two have in common

Both were routine supply flights to a place that cannot be supplied any other
way, and in both the aircraft was destroyed within sight of the station by
people doing an ordinary job. Alert has never had a road, a harbour usable for
more than a few weeks, or an alternative. That dependence is the hazard, and it
has not changed: Operation Boxtop still flies twice a year.
