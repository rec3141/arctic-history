In the summer of 1854 the Royal Navy walked away from four ships in good order
in one season, and a fifth had already been left the year before. It is the
largest deliberate abandonment in the history of Arctic navigation, and it is
worth understanding what abandonment meant, because the word suggests a
shipwreck and this was nothing of the kind.

## What abandonment meant

A beset ship is not a sinking ship. She is a sound hull held fast in a floe
that is itself moving, and she may be held for a season or for three. The
decision to leave her is a decision about people: how much coal and food is
aboard, how many winters the crew can stand, and whether a relief ship can
reach a depot they can walk to. When a captain abandoned a ship he did not
scuttle her. He secured the hatches, left the colours flying, banked what
stores could not be carried, and marched his people over the ice to a depot.
The ship was left afloat, or aground, in the expectation that she would be
crushed. That expectation was often wrong.

The five ships of [[person:Edward Belcher]]'s squadron were photographed
together in 1852 by the Assistance's surgeon, William Domville — one of the
earliest photographs made in the Canadian Arctic
([[artifact:wrecks-disasters-036|the calotype]]).

## Kellett's two, 13 May 1854

[[person:Henry Kellett]] took HMS Resolute and the steam tender Intrepid west
in 1852 and wintered at [[place:Dealy Island]] off Melville Island, in latitude
74 degrees 56 minutes 25 seconds north, longitude 108 degrees 48 minutes 33
seconds west. It was from there that Bedford Pim's sledge party crossed to
[[place:Mercy Bay]] in April 1853 and found [[person:Robert McClure]]'s people
alive — the one unambiguous success of the whole search.

Working east in September 1853 the ships were caught in young ice five inches
thick at eight in the evening of the 10th and spent a second winter beset in
[[place:Barrow Strait]] at 74 degrees 41 minutes 34 seconds north, 101 degrees
22 minutes 06 seconds west, with [[place:Cape Cockburn]] bearing north 34 east,
twenty-eight miles. On 28 April 1854 [[person:Francis Leopold McClintock]] came
back over the ice from Beechey with Belcher's decided orders to abandon. Kellett
believed both his ships would swim out that summer and said so; he obeyed. On
the evening of Saturday 13 May 1854:

> At 6.15 p.m., the Captain Kellett inspected the lower deck, holds, &c., and
> after drinking a glass of wine to the old 'Resolute' and her crew, the lower
> deck was cleared for the last time, and the main hatchway secured.

([[artifact:wrecks-disasters-025|the passage]].) The crews marched at seven and
reached the North Star at Beechey Island on 18 June.
[[artifact:wrecks-disasters-023|The abandonment point]] is on the map. There is
a small discrepancy in McDougall's own book: the plate is captioned 15 May, two
days after the date in his journal.

## Belcher's two, 25 August 1854

Belcher took the Assistance and her tender Pioneer up
[[place:Wellington Channel]]. They wintered in 1852-53 in Northumberland Sound
at about 76 degrees 52 minutes north, and in 1853-54 in the channel at 75
degrees 31 minutes 14 seconds north, 92 degrees 21 minutes 55 seconds west,
some fifty-two miles north of the depot ship. Men died there that winter and
are buried on that shore. At six in the morning of 25 August 1854 the crews
formed up on the floe in travelling order and left:

> The colours, pendant, and Jack were so secured that they might be deemed
> 'nailed to the mast,' and the last tapping of the caulker's mallet at my
> companion hatch found an echo on many a heart, as if we had encoffined some
> cherished object.

([[artifact:wrecks-disasters-026|Belcher's own words]].) They reached Beechey
at five the following afternoon, the same day Inglefield's Phoenix and Jenkins's
Talbot came in from England, and on 27 August the whole party — five ships'
companies in four hulls — sailed for home. A marble tablet to
[[person:Joseph Rene Bellot]] had gone up on the beach that morning.

Belcher was court-martialled for leaving his ships in the Arctic and was
acquitted, on the ground that his orders had given him complete discretion. The
acquittal settled nothing: he was criticised severely in England, more so once
the Resolute came back on her own, and the Admiralty never gave him another
command. He rose to admiral by seniority and age alone
([[source:stuartstubbs1972belcher]]).

## The fifth ship, and the one that came back

[[artifact:wrecks-disasters-003|HMS Investigator]] had already been left in
Mercy Bay on 3 June 1853, which makes five. Her hull was found by Parks Canada
in [[artifact:wrecks-disasters-004|2010]]. Of the five, four were never seen
again by anyone who left them.

The exception is the reason this page exists. On
[[artifact:wrecks-disasters-030|10 September 1855]] the New London whaler George
Henry, [[person:James M. Buddington]] master, sighted a ship in the pack about
twenty miles off [[place:Cape Mercy]] in Davis Strait. The two drifted towards
each other for five days. Buddington sent four men across the ice and they came
back to report that the stranger was Her Britannic Majesty's ship Resolute, dry
below, sound, and empty. He put eleven of his own crew aboard, set up her
rigging, and brought her into New London on Christmas Eve with a lever watch, a
quadrant, an untrustworthy compass and an outline of the American coast drawn on
a sheet of foolscap.

She had drifted about twelve hundred miles, unmanned, through Barrow Strait,
Lancaster Sound and Baffin Bay, in sixteen months. Congress bought her from her
salvors, refitted her, and presented her to Queen Victoria in December 1856 as
a gift from the American people. When she was broken up her timbers were made into a desk,
which sits in the Oval Office.

## What the episode is worth

Two things. First, an abandoned ship in the archipelago is not necessarily a
wreck: the Resolute proved that a hull can survive both the besetment and the
release. Second, five ships' companies came out of that summer without a single
man lost in the withdrawal itself, which was good seamanship, and the Admiralty
still treated it as a humiliation and closed the naval search. The search was
already over; [[person:John Rae]] had brought the Inuit testimony to London
that same year.
