Most of this gazetteer is losses. This page is the other column: five parties
who lived through conditions that killed comparable parties elsewhere. They have
almost nothing in common as expeditions, and one thing in common as survivals.

## The Polaris floe party: 196 days, nobody lost

On the night of 15 October 1872 the Polaris was nipped in Smith Sound and
nineteen people — nine of the ship's company and ten Inuit, including
[[person:Ebierbing]], [[person:Tookoolito]], [[person:Hans Hendrik]] and their
families, two of the children infants — were on the ice putting stores off when
the floe parted from the ship in the dark. They were not picked up.

They drifted the whole length of Baffin Bay and down the Labrador coast, about
1,800 nautical miles, on a floe that broke up under them repeatedly, and were
taken off by the sealer Tigress on 30 April 1873, 196 days later. Every one of
them was alive, including the babies.

The reason is not in dispute and was not in dispute at the time. The naval
provisions ran out early. Ebierbing and Hans Hendrik hunted seal from the floe
edge through the winter with the two kayaks they had saved, and Tookoolito kept
the children fed and dry. [[person:George Emory Tyson]], the officer in charge,
wrote it down. The party lived because ten of them were Inuit hunters doing what
they did at home, and because the others could be persuaded to eat seal.
[[artifact:wrecks-disasters-014|The drift]] is on the map; the story is at
[[topic:kane-hall-polaris]] and [[page:the-polaris-and-the-floe-drift]].

## Greely's six at Cape Sabine: the one that mostly failed

The counter-example belongs on this page for honesty's sake. Twenty-five men
came south from Fort Conger in the autumn of 1883 to a depot at
[[place:Cape Sabine]] that did not exist, because the ship carrying it, the
[[artifact:wrecks-disasters-005|Proteus]], had been crushed off that same cape on
23 July. They built a hut of stone and an upturned boat and waited.

Eighteen starved between January and June 1884. One was shot on
[[person:Adolphus Washington Greely]]'s written order for repeatedly stealing food. Six
were taken off by Commander Schley's Thetis and Bear on
[[artifact:wrecks-disasters-015|22 June 1884]], and one of those died on the
passage south. They had been eating lichen, sealskin lashings and the shrimp
they could net through a hole.

What separates this from the Polaris floe is not courage or discipline. It is
that Greely's people were on a barren shore in winter with no hunters and no
boats, waiting for a depot, while Tyson's were on moving ice full of seals with
men who could take them. Told at [[topic:nares-greely]].

## Bartlett's walk, 1914

When the Karluk was crushed in the Chukchi ice in January 1914,
[[person:Robert Bartlett]] got his people onto the ice and to Wrangel Island,
and then walked. He and the young Inupiaq hunter [[person:Claude Kataktovik]] crossed some 200
kilometres of moving ice to the Siberian shore in March and April, then went on
by dog team along the coast until Bartlett could get a message out. Eleven of the
twenty-five died; the fourteen who lived on Wrangel Island did so because
Bartlett went for help himself rather than waiting. The story belongs to
[[topic:karluk-cae]].

## Storkerson's drift, 1918: survival as an experiment

[[person:Storker Storkerson]] did on purpose, and comfortably, what the Polaris
party did by accident and nearly died of.

In March 1918 he took five men and sixteen dogs north from Cross Island, Alaska,
sent his last support party home on 14 April from 73 degrees 3 minutes north,
and then camped on a floe and let it carry him, to test whether the Beaufort Sea
had a current running west. It did not do what he expected: they drifted east
for three months, then northwest, reaching 74 north on 8 September, 184 days and
440 miles in all. They brought 101 days of rations and hardly touched them,
living on seal and bear. On 9 October they started for land from 200 miles out
and reached it on 8 November in the early winter dark, which Storkerson
described as "without accident or hardship".
[[artifact:wrecks-disasters-016|The drift]] is on the map.

[[person:Vilhjalmur Stefansson]]'s crossing from Martin Point to Banks Island in
1914 had made the same argument first, and made it noisily: a party that can
hunt is not a party in distress. The argument was overstated — Stefansson lost
men on other journeys, and his method depended on ice with seals under it — but
in the specific case it was right, and it is the reason a Norwegian and four
companions spent six months on a floe as a matter of routine.

## Boxtop 22, 1991: thirty hours

The most recent survival in this gazetteer is measured in hours rather than
months. Fourteen people lived through the crash of a Hercules sixteen kilometres
from [[place:CFS Alert]] on 30 October 1991; thirteen were alive when the last
of them was lifted out more than thirty hours later, in the polar night at minus
20 to minus 30 with a high wind, after the first two ground parties turned back
and the parachuted rescuers lost most of their equipment to the wind on landing.
See [[page:alerts-two-air-disasters]].

## What the five have in common

Not equipment, and not planning. In each case the survivors had someone among
them who could feed the party or keep it awake, and something to be done rather
than a rescue to be waited for. The parties that died — Greely's eighteen, the
William Torr's twenty-two, Franklin's hundred and five — were, in every case,
waiting.
