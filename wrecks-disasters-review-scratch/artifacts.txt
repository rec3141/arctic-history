  local_file:  | source_url: https://archive.org/details/furtherpapersrel00grea
  DESC: The Breadalbane was a three-masted barque built in Scotland in 1843, a wine and grain trader chartered by the Admiralty in the spring of 1853 to carry stores north to Sir Edward Belcher's squadron. She and HMS Phoenix were made fast to a driving floe about half a mile south of Beechey Island when the pack came in from the offing. Belcher, who was at Beechey, sets the first warning of the nip at a little after four on the morning of Sunday 21 August 1853, the groaning of beams and creaking of bulkheads driving the crew onto the ice half dressed; in about fifteen minutes the vessel was destroyed. Nobody was lost; the Phoenix, badly nipped herself, took the twenty-one men off. Sources disagree on the hour: McDougall, writing at second hand from Barrow Strait, puts it on the night of the 20th, and MacInnis says just after midnight. Belcher's is the eyewitness account. A piece of her teak was picked up on a beach fifty-five miles to the north the following year.

  date_text: 1980-08-13  start: 1980-08-13  end: 
  lat/lon: 74.6833 -91.85  bibkey: macinnis2015breadalbane  pages: The Canadian Encyclopedia, s.v. Breadalbane
  people: ["Joseph Beverley MacInnis"] | tags: ["wreck,discovery,1980,archaeology"]
  credit: after MacInnis, The Canadian Encyclopedia
  local_file:  | source_url: https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/breadalbane
  DESC: Joseph MacInnis began looking for the Breadalbane in August 1978, prompted by the research of Stuart Hodgson and Maurice Haycock and working from eyewitness accounts in the Scott Polar Research Institute. After three seasons of towing side-scan sonar through the water south of Beechey Island, on 13 August 1980 the ship came up on the screen with her hull intact and two masts still standing. She lies upright in about 100 metres of water. In 1981 a remotely operated vehicle photographed her bow, masts, rudder, anchor, the wheel and a deckhouse cabinet still holding compass and signal light, the copper sheathing on her hull looking new; in 1983 divers in the WASP atmospheric suit worked on her. Cold and darkness have made her the best-preserved wooden wreck yet found in any ocean, and for the ship's scientists she is a benchmark: a dated wooden structure sitting under Arctic sea ice for 127 years.
