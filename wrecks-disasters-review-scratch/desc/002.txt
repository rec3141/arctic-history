Joseph MacInnis began looking for the Breadalbane in August 1978, prompted by the research of Stuart Hodgson and Maurice Haycock and working from eyewitness accounts in the Scott Polar Research Institute. After three seasons of towing side-scan sonar through the water south of Beechey Island, on 13 August 1980 the ship came up on the screen with her hull intact and two masts still standing. She lies upright in about 100 metres of water. In 1981 a remotely operated vehicle photographed her bow, masts, rudder, anchor, the wheel and a deckhouse cabinet still holding compass and signal light, the copper sheathing on her hull looking new; in 1983 divers in the WASP atmospheric suit worked on her. Cold and darkness have made her the best-preserved wooden wreck yet found in any ocean, and for the ship's scientists she is a benchmark: a dated wooden structure sitting under Arctic sea ice for 127 years. The point plotted here, 74 degrees 41 minutes north, 91 degrees 51 minutes west, is the rounded position published for the protected site, not a surveyed wreck position; no surveyed position has been published.
