# Over Ellesmere to Axel Heiberg, February and March 1908
[page · cook-1908-axel-heiberg · draft]

The outward journey of 1908 took twenty-eight days and covered about five hundred miles of ground, most of it across an island nobody had crossed at that latitude. It is the part of Cook's expedition that stands on its own feet, because the country he describes is there and matches.

## Getting ready

The work was done by the Inughuit, in the dark. Through the winter of 1907-08 at [[place:Anoatok]] the sledges were built, the fur clothing sewn, the walrus and narwhal hunted and cached. Cook is unusually good on this. He names [[person:Cla-you]], who was making hare-skin stockings for the polar outfit and left the work before Christmas to go into a snow-house alone and give birth, with a piece of glass for a knife; [[person:E-ve-lue]] finished the stockings. He names her husband [[person:Ac-po-di-soa]], a sledge-builder, whom the party called Bismark, and who left his bench to cut snow blocks and dig for grass to floor the birth house.

On 5 February 1908 [[person:Es-se-you]], [[person:Kud-la]] and [[person:Me-tek]] took eight sledges across to lay a depot at the head of [[place:Flagler Bay]] and to hunt musk ox there. [[person:Rudolph Franke]] had already been out toward [[place:Cape Sabine]] in January with two men, caching.

## Across Smith Sound

Cook left on the morning of 19 February with eleven sledges and 103 dogs. They saw [[place:Cape Sabine]] at three in the afternoon of the 20th and camped in rafted ice under it; two men would go no further and turned home. On 22 February they passed [[place:Cape Rutherford]] and picked up the cache igloo near Cape Viele in [[place:Buchanan Bay]]. Temperatures in these marches run from fifty to eighty below zero Fahrenheit in Cook's pages.

## The pass

On 25 February they made the head of Flagler Bay, twenty-five miles in a day, and turned west into a valley that no ship's survey had followed. It runs about eighty kilometres clean across [[place:Ellesmere Island]] between the ice caps, from the Smith Sound side to [[place:Bay Fiord]] and [[place:Eureka Sound]]; Cook's chapter title is "Exploring a New Pass over Acpohon" and his subtitle is "From the Atlantic Waters at Flagler Bay to the Pacific Waters at Bay Fiord". He named the two rivers for Schley and for Greely. The pass carries [[person:Otto Sverdrup]]'s name today, and it is now one of the most worked-over pieces of ground in the High Arctic: a relict musk-ox range, a glacier transect, and Palaeoeskimo and Thule sites.

The valley was full of game — "the Mecca of the Musk Ox" — and the party hunted it hard, which is why they could travel light. A blizzard caught them in a canyon and Cook records what saved them: [[artifact:cook-1908-axel-heiberg-023|"Dig a hole," said Koo-loo-ting-wah]].

## North to the black wall

They came down to [[place:Irene Bay]] and [[place:Bay Fiord]], saw the sun again, and on 2 March went out into [[place:Eureka Sound]] past the last point of land, counting four herds of musk ox, in a night of eighty-three below — the coldest of the season. On 7 March, near Schei Island at the north end of the sound, they began what Cook calls a straight run of a hundred and seventy miles to the polar sea, up [[place:Nansen Sound]] between Ellesmere and [[place:Axel Heiberg Island]].

They reached [[place:Cape Stallworthy]] — [[artifact:cook-1908-axel-heiberg-011|Svartevoeg, the black wall]] — in mid-March, and took seven musk oxen and eighty-five hares in the lowlands just south of it. Cook searched the cape carefully for the cache [[person:Robert Edwin Peary]] said he had left there in 1906 and reported [[artifact:cook-1908-axel-heiberg-012|that there was none]]. The neighbouring cape to the east is Peary's [[place:Cape Thomas Hubbard]]; the bay between the two is now [[place:Eetookashoo Bay]], for one of Cook's two young companions.

## The parting

On 18 March 1908 the supporting party turned for home. Cook kept twenty-six dogs, two sledges of fifty-two pounds each, and about fourteen hundred pounds of food, most of it beef and walrus pemmican, reckoned for eighty days. [[person:Koolootingwah]] and [[person:In-u-gi-to]] stayed on the sea ice another sixteen miles to help over the rough shore pack, then were sent back with empty sledges and hungry dogs to make the five hundred miles home alone. In-u-gi-to had volunteered to push on one more day without dog food.

The men shouted after him *Tigishi ah yaung-uluk* — the big nail. Cook: [[artifact:cook-1908-axel-heiberg-010|"Even after they were out of sight in the drifting snowstorm their voices came cheerily back to me."]]

Koolootingwah carried orders back to Franke at Annoatok: hold the station until 5 June 1908, then hand it to Koolootingwah and go home.

What happens next on the map is nothing, deliberately. The track [[artifact:cook-1908-axel-heiberg-001]] ends at Cape Stallworthy and the next one, [[artifact:cook-1908-axel-heiberg-002]], picks the men up three months later in [[place:Hassel Sound]]. Between those two points is a claim, and it belongs to [[page:the-pole-controversy-in-one-page]], not to a map layer.
