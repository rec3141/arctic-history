# The winter at Cape Sparbo, 1908-1909
[page · cook-1908-axel-heiberg · draft]

Three men reached [[place:Cape Sparbo]] on the north coast of [[place:Devon Island]] early in September 1908. They had a half sledge, a punctured canvas boat, a torn silk tent, some kettles, knives and matches, four cartridges Cook had hidden for a last emergency, and clothing splitting to shreds. No dogs. The nearest people were more than three hundred miles away at [[place:Anoatok]], across [[place:Jones Sound]] and [[place:Smith Sound]], and the ice was making.

They stayed until 18 February 1909.

## Somebody else's house

The place they landed on was not empty ground. Cook: [[artifact:cook-1908-axel-heiberg-016|"we found to our surprise that it was the site of an old Eskimo village. There was a line of old igloos partly below water..."]]

They picked one of the ruins, a pit with a raised sleeping platform about six by eight feet, walls two feet high, and lamp wings at the sides, and rebuilt it — another foot of wall, sod cut and dried in the sun, and a frame of whale ribs gathered from the beach for a roof. Digging it out, Cook [[artifact:cook-1908-axel-heiberg-017|turned up a human skull]] in the floor. He wrote that the omen was bad but the company was welcome.

This matters beyond the anecdote. A three-man occupation of a Thule winter house on the north Devon coast in 1908-09, described in print, is a datable disturbance of an archaeological site, and Cook's own notes on the drowned house rings and the whale-bone are an early observation of the raised beaches of Jones Sound. The site has been located and studied since; the modern literature on it is not yet summarised here (see the worklog).

## Living on musk ox

They had no ammunition worth the name, so they built weapons out of the sledge. Bows and arrows from the hickory; harpoon and lance shafts from the same wood, tipped with musk-ox horn and whale bone; a sledge shoe cut up for metal points; nails from the cooking box as rivets; a sling for stones; looped lines for hares and gulls. Two arrows were eventually tipped with the side-blades of [[person:Etukishook]]'s pocket knife, set in bone.

Musk ox were killed by stalking with stones until the herd ringed up, then dropping a slip loop over a bull's horns and making the line fast to a rock, so the animal strangled itself pulling. A walrus of about three thousand pounds was harpooned after a fifteen-hour fight. Seals were taken at blow holes found by following bear tracks. Foxes went into bone traps: fourteen white and two blue. [[person:Ahwelah]] became good enough with the bow to take ptarmigan.

They ate musk ox for seven months without variation.

## The dark

The sun went behind the southern cliffs on 3 November 1908 and did not come back until 11 February 1909 — about a hundred double nights, which is the title of Cook's chapter. Two half tin plates of musk-ox fat with moss wicks burned day and night in the den; the top of the shelter was tolerable, the floor was below zero, and [[artifact:cook-1908-axel-heiberg-018|except close to a lamp you could not see the other men's faces]].

Cook is better on this than on almost anything else in the book. [[artifact:cook-1908-axel-heiberg-019|"We were all alone - alone in a vast, white dead world."]] Lemmings shared the den and hibernated for a month. Five ravens were fed and kept as company, and the two young men sent messages home by them — Etukishook to An-na-do-a and to his father [[person:Panikpah]]; Ahwelah to Ser-wah, telling her not to marry a man he called a lazy gull.

That detail is worth pausing on. Two twenty-year-olds spent a hundred nights in a hole in the ground on an uninhabited island, hundreds of miles from anyone, with a foreigner whose reason for being there they had no stake in, and what they talked about was who was going to marry whom at home.

## Out

Three weeks before sunrise the equipment was ready. On 18 February 1909 they hauled the rebuilt sledge out past the ice fort and started north (the printed book says 1908, which cannot be right). Eight days of man-hauling at about seven miles a day brought them to [[place:Cape Tennyson]], where Cook sighted two small islands and [[artifact:cook-1908-axel-heiberg-022|named them for Ahwelah and Etukishook]].

Then it got worse. A month without seeing a living thing on the Ellesmere coast; boiled walrus line and scraps of skin; [[artifact:cook-1908-axel-heiberg-020|Cook broke his teeth on walrus hide]]. A bear taken at [[place:Cape Faraday]] on the evening of 20 March saved them. At [[place:Cape Sabine]] they found a seal Panikpah had cached a year before, with a drawing beside it, spotted with lamp soot, of a father searching for his son.

They crossed Smith Sound and [[artifact:cook-1908-axel-heiberg-021|climbed an iceberg on hands and knees to see Annoatok]].
