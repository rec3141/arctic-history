# The Karluk, the Canadian Arctic Expedition and the Fifth Thule, 1913-1924

Three things happened in the North between 1913 and 1924 that are usually told
separately and belong together. A Canadian government expedition lost its ship
and eleven men in the Chukchi ice, and then, with the survivors of its own
reputation, walked the last large islands of the Queen Elizabeth group onto the
map. Its southern half quietly did the science and produced one of the great
ethnographies. And a Danish-Greenlandic expedition crossed the whole of Arctic
America by dog sledge, asking a question none of the others thought to ask.

## The Canadian Arctic Expedition, 1913-1918

It was the largest scientific expedition Canada had ever sent north, and it was
put together in about four months. [[person:Vilhjalmur Stefansson]] proposed it,
the American Museum of Natural History was to pay for it, and in February 1913
the Canadian government took it over entire - partly for the science, mostly
because a Canadian expedition finding land north of the archipelago would settle
sovereignty questions that Sverdrup's Norwegians had raised (see
[[topic:sverdrup-bernier-peary]]).

It sailed as two parties. The Northern Party under Stefansson was to go out over
the ice of the Beaufort Sea looking for land. The Southern Party under
[[person:Rudolph Martin Anderson]] was to survey and collect along the mainland
coast. The flagship was the [[vessel:Karluk]], an elderly brigantine, and her
master was [[person:Robert Bartlett]].

Almost everything went wrong at once. The Karluk was beset near Camden Bay on 13
August 1913 and never got free. Stefansson left her with five others on 20
September to hunt caribou; the ice took her west that night. She was crushed on
11 January 1914 north of [[place:Herald Island]], and of the twenty-five people
left on the ice, eleven died - four who set off for Herald Island in January,
four who left on their own in February, and three on [[place:Wrangel Island]]
during the summer. Bartlett and the young Inupiaq hunter
[[person:Claude Kataktovik]] walked to Siberia to fetch help. That story is
[[page:karluk-cae-the-karluk]].

The two parties then spent five years working, and mostly not speaking.

**The Northern Party.** In March 1914 Stefansson,
[[person:Storker Storkerson]] and [[person:Ole Andreasen]] walked north from
[[place:Martin Point]] in Alaska onto the moving pack with no depot and no relief
behind them, living on the seals they shot, and came ashore on Banks Island
ninety-six days later: [[page:karluk-cae-ninety-six-days]]. From the base at
[[place:Cape Kellett]] they then made two long ice journeys, in 1915 and 1916,
which put [[place:Brock Island]], [[place:Borden Island]],
[[place:Mackenzie King Island]], [[place:Meighen Island]] and
[[place:Lougheed Island]] on the chart, wintered on [[place:Melville Island]] and
disproved Sverdrup's Crown Prince Gustav Sea. This is the part of the expedition
that happened where the ship is now: [[page:karluk-cae-new-islands]]. The last
journey was [[person:Storker Storkerson]]'s six-month drift on a Beaufort Sea ice
floe in 1918: [[page:karluk-cae-storkerson-drift]].

**The Southern Party.** Anderson, [[person:Kenneth Chipman]],
[[person:John R. Cox]], [[person:John Johnston O'Neill]] and
[[person:Diamond Jenness]] mapped and collected the coast from the Mackenzie
delta to Coronation Gulf out of [[place:Bernard Harbour]], and Jenness lived two
years in a Copper Inuit household: [[page:karluk-cae-southern-party]].

## The Fifth Thule Expedition, 1921-1924

At the other end of the archipelago, and with entirely different intentions,
[[person:Knud Rasmussen]] built a house on [[place:Danish Island]] in
[[place:Foxe Basin]] in September 1921. [[person:Therkel Mathiassen]] dug the
Thule-culture village at [[place:Naujan]] and at [[place:Iglulik]] and defined
the archaeology of the eastern Arctic; [[person:Kaj Birket-Smith]] went inland to
the Caribou Inuit; [[person:Peter Freuchen]] mapped, and lost a foot doing it.
In March 1923 Rasmussen set out from Danish Island with two Inughuit from
Greenland, [[person:Arnarulunnguaq]] and [[person:Qaavigarsuaq]], and sledged to
Bering Strait, reaching Nome on 31 August 1924. See
[[page:karluk-cae-fifth-thule]].

## Who was actually there

The record of these years names a great many white men and very few of the people
without whom none of them would have come home.

[[person:Natkusiak]], an Inupiaq hunter who had travelled with Stefansson since
1908, laid the meat depots on Melville Island that the ice journeys of 1916 and
1917 ran on; he later settled on Banks Island and his descendants were among the
founders of Sachs Harbour. [[person:Emiu]] and [[person:Palaiyak]] drove sleds
and carried mail across hundreds of miles of ice. [[person:Kuraluk]] hunted for
the Karluk survivors on Wrangel Island and is the reason most of them lived, and
his wife [[person:Kiruk]] was the ship's only seamstress: every person who walked
off that ice did so in clothing she had made. Their daughters
[[person:Helen Kuraluk|Helen]], aged seven, and [[person:Mugpi]], not much over a
year old, survived the whole thing; Mugpi died at Barrow in 2008, the last of the
Karluk. [[person:Claude Kataktovik]] walked seven hundred miles to Siberia and
back into private life. [[person:Arnarulunnguaq]] became the first Inuit woman to
cross Arctic North America.

And there are the people whose names the sources withhold. Stefansson's fur
clothing - the thing that made a 229-day journey possible - was made by three
Inuvialuit women he calls only [[person:Elvina Storkerson]],
[[person:Jennie Thomsen]] and [[person:Mrs Lopez]]. He prints their husbands' names,
their daughters' names and the names of the dogs.

The animals are named too, when anyone bothered. The Karluk's dogs
[[animal:Molly]], [[animal:Nellie]] and [[animal:Whitey]] hauled from Shipwreck
Camp; [[animal:Colt]], bought from a Chukchi camp on the Siberian shore, was the
one still fresh when the rest were spent; [[animal:Mike]] was Natkusiak's big dog
on M'Clure Strait, named in a photograph caption and nowhere in the books; and
[[animal:The Karluk's black cat|the ship's black cat]] came off Wrangel Island
alive after eight months on the ice. They and their kind are gathered in
[[topic:animals]], as the expedition's ships are in [[topic:vessels]].

## Why it matters here

Three reasons a scientist on this ship might care.

The map. Meighen, Lougheed, Brock, Borden and Mackenzie King Islands were drawn
by men on foot between 1915 and 1916, and their outlines stood essentially
unchanged until aerial photography in 1947 split one of Stefansson's islands in
two. When a modern chart of the northwest Queen Elizabeth Islands disagrees with
the historical one, this is usually why.

The data. Storkerson's soundings and drift positions of 1918 are among the first
physical measurements ever made in the Beaufort Sea, and they killed a theory
(the permanent current) and a phantom island (Keenan Land) in one season.
Stefansson's parties took tidal observations on Lougheed Island and in Hassel
Sound, and soundings across the mouth of what is now Peary Channel, at points
where nobody has been since.

The people. The Copper Inuit ethnography of [[person:Diamond Jenness]] and the
Iglulingmiut and Netsilingmiut material of [[person:Knud Rasmussen]] were both
collected in these years, and both are read today in Kitikmeot and Qikiqtaaluk
communities as records of named ancestors, camps and stories. See
[[topic:thule-dorset]] and [[topic:inuit-oral-history]].

## A note on the sources

The official narrative volume of the expedition - Report of the Canadian Arctic
Expedition 1913-18, Volume I, General Introduction and Narrative, Part A
Northern Party by Stefansson and Part B Southern Party by Anderson - was never
published. Every other volume in the series, from 1919 to 1946, lists it as "in
preparation." The consequence is that the only continuous account of the Northern
Party's five years is [[artifact:karluk-cae-042|Stefansson's own book]], written
while he was defending himself in public, and the only continuous account of the
Karluk is [[artifact:karluk-cae-043|Bartlett's]], dictated within two years of
the events while the loss of the ship was still being argued over. Both are used
here, and both should be read knowing what they are.
