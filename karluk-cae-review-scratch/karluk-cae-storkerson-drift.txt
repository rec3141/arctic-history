# Storkerson's drift and the end of the expedition, 1917-1918

The Canadian Arctic Expedition's last journey was the strangest of them: five men
who went out onto the Beaufort Sea, found a floe they liked, and stayed on it for
six months on purpose.

## Why drift

By 1917 [[person:Vilhjalmur Stefansson]] had established that a party could feed
itself on the polar pack. The next question was oceanographic. Was there a
permanent current in the Beaufort Sea, as almost everyone assumed - the current
that had taken Nansen's Fram across, and that had carried the
[[page:karluk-cae-the-karluk|Karluk]] west to her death? And did Keenan Land
exist, the land marked on a 1912 map published by the American Geographical
Society and the American Museum of Natural History in the unsounded water north
of Alaska?

The way to answer both was to sit on an ice floe with a sextant, a chronometer
and a sounding line and let it take you where it would. The plan was to drift for
a year.

## The floe

[[person:Storker Storkerson]] left Cross Island on the Alaskan coast on 15 March
1918. The route out and back is [[artifact:karluk-cae-005]]. By 3 April the party
was 105 miles out; on 14 April observations put them at 73 degrees 3 minutes
north, 148 degrees 32 minutes west, about 190 miles north of the Colville delta,
and [[person:Aarnout Castel]] took the last support party home with the mail.

Five men stayed: Storkerson, [[person:August Masik]],
[[person:Adelbert Gumaer]], [[person:E. Lorne Knight]] and
[[person:Martin Kilian]], with sixteen dogs and exactly 101 days' full rations.
[[person:Fred Volki]], a boy, had volunteered and was sent home because five men
were enough.

On 16 April they moved camp three miles east onto the young ice of a refrozen
lead, where the seals were, and did not move again for six months. Kilian kept
the meteorological record. Storkerson took the astronomical observations and the
soundings. By the middle of June they had killed forty-two seals and four polar
bears, about three tons of meat, and the food brought from shore was still
untouched: it was being saved for the walk home.

## What they found

Two negatives, both of them worth having.

There is no permanent current in the Beaufort Sea between about 72.5 and 74
degrees north. The floe's drift, tracked by observation for six months, followed
the wind and nothing else. (Stefansson, to his credit, footnoted his own man's
conclusion in print: "It is possible that further study may show that the ice
movement was not entirely controlled by the local winds and that Storkerson's
statement is here too emphatic." Modern work on the Beaufort Gyre says
Stefansson's caution was the better guess, but Storkerson's six months of
positions were real data where there had been none.)

And Keenan Land does not exist. The floe drifted straight through where it was
drawn on the map, and the sounding line found no bottom at 1,600 fathoms.

## Coming off

They came off early. Storkerson fell ill with asthma, and on 9 October 1918,
after 184 days on the floe, the party started for land from latitude 73.9 north,
a little over 200 miles out, with fifty-five days' rations left.

October is the worst month of the year to travel on sea ice: the light is going,
the fog and snow are continuous, and new ice under snow looks exactly like ice
that will hold. They crossed it in a month, some of the leads in sled-boats, and
camped on dry ground in the Colville delta on 8 November 1918, after 238 days
living on sea ice. Storkerson's whole account of the crossing is one sentence:
[[artifact:karluk-cae-005|"We started from a point a little over 200 miles from
shore on October 9th and reached land November 8th without accident or
hardship."]] Asked about it later he added: "We took every ordinary precaution
and no extraordinary circumstance came up."

(A note for anyone checking the dates: the version of Storkerson's narrative
printed in [[source:stefansson1921friendly]] gives the landfall as 5 November
1919. That is a misprint. He started on 15 March 1918 and counted 238 days on the
ice, which ends on 8 November 1918.)

## The end

That was the fifth and last exploring trip of the Canadian Arctic Expedition.
Storkerson wintered at [[place:Herschel Island]] and went out the next spring up
the Mackenzie with an inspector of the Royal North-West Mounted Police, and was
ordered by telegram to report to his commander at Banff, Alberta.

Stefansson himself had come out sick. He caught typhoid in 1918 and lay ill for
months at Herschel Island; he did not go north again. The expedition had run five
years, cost eleven lives on the Karluk and two more on the ice of M'Clure Strait,
and produced five new islands, a great deal of coastal survey, the first
soundings of the Beaufort Sea, and fourteen thick volumes of natural history and
ethnology - but not, in the end, the volume that mattered most. Volume I of the
Report of the Canadian Arctic Expedition was to have carried the narrative of
both parties. Every other volume published from 1919 onward lists it as "in
preparation." It never appeared. That is why the only continuous account of the
Northern Party is Stefansson's own [[artifact:karluk-cae-042|The Friendly
Arctic]] - a partisan book, written by a man defending himself, and the only one
there is.

## Afterwards

Stefansson's last Arctic venture was the worst of them. In 1921 he sent five
people to occupy [[place:Wrangel Island]] and claim it for the British Empire -
a claim Britain did not want, Canada disavowed and the Soviet Union enforced
against. Four of the five died: [[person:E. Lorne Knight]], who had spent six
months on Storkerson's floe, and [[person:Fred Maurer]], who had already survived
Wrangel Island once as a Karluk castaway. The only survivor was Ada Blackjack,
the Inupiaq seamstress hired at Nome, who kept herself alive alone on the island
for two years and was fetched off by [[person:Harold Noice]] - the man who had
stepped ashore first on [[place:Meighen Island]] in 1916.
