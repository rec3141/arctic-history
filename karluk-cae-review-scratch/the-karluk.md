# The Karluk, 1913-1914

The Canadian Arctic Expedition's flagship was a thirty-nine-year-old brigantine
built for the Aleutian cod fishery and later used for whaling. She was bought in
a hurry in 1913, and [[person:Robert Bartlett]] - who had taken Peary's
[[vessel:Roosevelt]] north twice and knew what ice does to a hull - said so
plainly. She was not an ice ship. The loss of the [[vessel:Karluk]] happened in
the Chukchi Sea, well west of the archipelago the ship is working in now, but it
is the story this topic is named for and it shaped everything after it.

The whole voyage is [[artifact:karluk-cae-001]]; Bartlett's own narrative,
[[source:bartlett1916last]], is held here in full as
[[artifact:karluk-cae-043]].

## Beset

The Karluk left Esquimalt on 17 June 1913, called at [[place:Nome]] and Port
Clarence, took on [[person:Claude Kataktovik]], [[person:John Hadley]] and the
family of [[person:Kuraluk]] - his wife [[person:Kiruk]] and their daughters
[[person:Helen Kuraluk|Helen]], aged seven, and [[person:Mugpi]], not much over a
year old - off Cape Smythe in early August, and pushed east along the Alaskan
coast. On 13 August 1913 she was beset in the ice near Camden Bay, about 225
miles east of [[place:Point Barrow]]. She never moved under her own power again.

On 20 September [[person:Vilhjalmur Stefansson]] left the ship with five others -
[[person:Burt McConnell]], [[person:George Hubert Wilkins]],
[[person:Diamond Jenness]], [[person:Panyurak]] and [[person:Asatshak]] - to hunt
caribou ashore for ten days. A gale came up that night and took the ship west.
They never saw her again. Whether that departure was a hunting trip or an
abandonment has been argued for a century: Bartlett's book does not accuse,
[[person:William Laird McKinlay]] in [[source:mckinlay1976karluk]] does, and the
evidence is genuinely ambiguous. It is worth saying that Stefansson could not
have known the ice would move that night, and equally that he was the only man
who got off the ship before it sank. The photograph taken from the deck as the
party pulled away is [[artifact:karluk-cae-058]].

The drift ran northwest, reached latitude 73 north on 15 November, then turned
southwest toward [[place:Herald Island]]. The last observation taken aboard, on 7
January 1914, was 72 degrees 11 minutes north, 174 degrees 36 minutes west.

## The crushing

On the evening of 10 January 1914 the ice opened the ship's side.
[[artifact:karluk-cae-021|"I went on deck again and gave the order, 'All hands
abandon ship.'"]] Everything movable had already been carried onto the floe.
The next afternoon she went down: [[artifact:karluk-cae-020|at four o'clock, with
the blue Canadian ensign at her main-topmast-head and the Victrola in the galley
playing Chopin's Funeral March]], in thirty-eight fathoms about sixty miles north
of Herald Island. Bartlett bared his head and said, "Adios, Karluk!"

Twenty-five people and a cat were left on the ice at what they called
[[place:Shipwreck Camp]]. The cat, given to the ship at Esquimalt and "as black
as the ace of spades," was carried off in a basket, kept the box-house all winter
and went to Wrangel Island in a deerskin bag on a sledge:
[[animal:The Karluk's black cat]]. There were also the dogs, of whom [[animal:Molly]], [[animal:Nellie]] and
[[animal:Whitey]] have names in the book.

## The eight who were never found

On 21 January Bartlett sent the first mate [[person:Alexander Anderson]] with
[[person:Charles Barker]], [[person:A. King]] and [[person:John Brady]] toward
Wrangel Island by way of Herald Island. [[person:Bjarne Mamen]]'s supporting
party left them at open water near the island and turned back. Nothing was ever
found of the four in Bartlett's time; a landing party on Herald Island in 1924
found a camp and human remains ([[source:niven2000ice]]).

On 5 February [[person:Alister Forbes Mackay]], [[person:James Murray]],
[[person:Henri Beuchat]] and [[person:Stanley Morris]] left on their own account,
man-hauling their own sledge, having signed a letter absolving Bartlett of
responsibility: the letter survives in facsimile as [[artifact:karluk-cae-083]].
Mackay and Murray were Shackleton veterans and thought they knew better.
[[person:Ernest Chafe]]'s returning party was the last to see them, about twenty
miles from Herald Island: three men on the traces, Beuchat a mile and a half
behind with his hands and feet frozen and partly delirious, Morris with
blood-poisoning already set in from a cut hand. None of the four was seen again.

## Wrangel Island

Bartlett brought the rest to [[place:Wrangel Island]] on 12 March 1914, landing
at one in the afternoon on Icy Spit, on the northeast side - twelve people,
including the two children. Helen, at seven, walked the whole way and sometimes
helped her father with the sledge; Kiruk carried Mugpi on her back from Shipwreck
Camp.

On 18 March Bartlett and Kataktovik left for Siberia with one sledge and seven
dogs. His written order to [[person:John Munro]], who was left in charge, ends
[[artifact:karluk-cae-024|"You will assemble at Rodgers Harbor about the middle of
July where I hope to meet you with a ship."]] The same letter says what everything
turned on: "The great thing of course is the procuring of game. In this Kerdrillo
will be of great assistance." Kerdrillo is Bartlett's spelling of
[[person:Kuraluk]], and the hunting was his.

Three of those left died. [[person:George Malloch]] died of nephritis at
[[place:Rodgers Harbour]] towards the end of May 1914 and
[[person:Bjarne Mamen]] a few days later. [[person:George Breddy]] died of a
gunshot at Waring Point in the summer. Bartlett's book gives the whole of it in
thirteen words - [[artifact:karluk-cae-026|"Breddy accidentally shot himself
later on, making the third death on the island."]] - with no date, weapon or
circumstance. The survivors' diaries record suspicion among themselves. It has
never been settled.

And every person who walked off that ice did so in clothing made or mended by
[[person:Kiruk]], the party's only seamstress.

## The walk to Siberia

[[artifact:karluk-cae-006]] is the route. Two men crossed the moving ice of the
Chukchi Sea in seventeen days and came ashore near Cape Yakan on 4 April 1914,
about sixty miles west of the cape the charts called Cape North - the Chukchi
Irkaipij, the modern Cape Schmidt. Kataktovik, who was between eighteen and
twenty and a widower with a baby daughter at home, expected to die twice over: on
the ice - [[artifact:karluk-cae-022|"my mother, my father, tell me long time ago
Eskimo get out on ice and drive away from Point Barrow never come back"]] - and
then at the hands of the Chukchi, who he had been told were enemies. They were
not; the Chukchi villages fed and sheltered the two men the whole way east, and one
old man on the coast let them have [[animal:Colt]], the dog that was still fresh
when all the others were spent.

It was Kataktovik who [[artifact:karluk-cae-023|saw the land first]]. They reached
Emma Town, a few miles southwest of East Cape, at six in the evening on 24 April,
thirty-seven days on the march and about seven hundred miles, almost all of it on
foot. From Emma Harbour on Providence Bay, reached on 16 May, word could finally
be telegraphed. Bartlett crossed to St Michael, Alaska, at the end of May in the
whaler Herman and joined the revenue cutter [[vessel:Bear]].

## The rescue, and the count

The walrus schooner [[vessel:King and Winge]], commanded by
[[person:Jochimsen|Captain Jochimsen]] with the trader
[[person:Olaf Swenson]] aboard, reached [[place:Rodgers Harbour]] on 7 September
1914 and Waring Point the next day. Bartlett, coming up in the Bear, met her in
the ice on 8 September and went across in a boat.
[[artifact:karluk-cae-025|His first question, and the answer]], are the whole
story in fourteen words. The Bear landed the survivors at Nome on 13 September:
[[artifact:karluk-cae-055]].

Of the twenty-five people aboard when Stefansson left the ship, eleven died:
Mackay, Murray, Beuchat, Morris, Anderson, Barker, King, Brady, Malloch, Mamen
and Breddy. Fourteen lived, among them the whole of Kuraluk's family. Mugpi,
carried across the ice as a baby, died at Barrow in 2008 as Ruth Makpii Ipalook,
the last survivor of the Karluk.

Kataktovik went home to Point Hope in August 1914. Bartlett went on to another
thirty years of Arctic work. [[person:Fred Maurer]], who had survived Wrangel
Island once, went back to it in 1921 with four others on Stefansson's scheme to
claim the island for the Empire, and died there; only
[[person:Ada Blackjack]], the Inupiaq seamstress hired at Nome, came out alive.
[[person:E. Lorne Knight]], who had drifted with Storkerson in 1918, died with
him. That story is picked up at the end of
[[page:karluk-cae-storkerson-drift]].

The fullest modern reconstruction, built from the diaries of McKinlay, Mamen,
Hadley and others, is [[source:niven2000ice]]. It and
[[source:mckinlay1976karluk]] are in copyright and are cited here, not reproduced.
