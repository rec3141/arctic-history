# Ninety-six days on the ice: the 1914 journey from Martin Point

The argument that made [[person:Vilhjalmur Stefansson]] famous, and that made the
long ice journeys of 1915 and 1916 possible, was tested once, in the spring of
1914, by three men with no way back.

## The quarrel first

By the winter of 1913-14 the Canadian Arctic Expedition had lost its flagship,
its commander had walked away from it a few days before it drifted off, and its
two halves were wintering three hundred miles apart on the Alaskan coast.
[[person:Rudolph Martin Anderson]]'s Southern Party was at
[[place:Collinson Point]] with most of the stores, the scientists and the ships;
Stefansson wanted men and equipment for a journey north over the ice into water
that no one had sounded. Anderson thought it a stunt, and said the expedition's
work was the coast and the science. The two men quarrelled in front of the
staff, and the quarrel never healed: Anderson's party went its own way for the
next two and a half years, and the two men were still writing letters about it
in the 1920s. See [[page:karluk-cae-southern-party]].

Stefansson went anyway, with the men who would come.

## Out from the land

On Sunday 22 March 1914 he started north from [[place:Martin Point]] on the
Alaskan coast with a support party and two companions who would go all the way:
[[person:Storker Storkerson]] and [[person:Ole Andreasen]]. It began badly.
On the second morning out, on ice the pressure had heaped against the shore
floe, [[person:Peter Bernard]] was pulled forward by a dropping sledge and cut
his forehead open in an arch from eye to eye, exposing the skull. Stefansson
called it the most nearly fatal accident he had ever seen in the North.
[[person:Burt McConnell]], who had caught the party up on the ice that morning
with the mail from Barrow, volunteered to put in the fourteen stitches the wound
needed, and Bernard was carried back ashore in an empty sled.

Around 6 April they crossed the edge of the continental shelf - the sounding line
dropped from a fathom a mile to 150 fathoms - and on the afternoon of 7 April,
at 70 degrees 13 minutes north, 140 degrees 30 minutes west, the support party
([[person:James Crawford]], [[person:Frits Johansen]] and McConnell) turned back
with thirty-one days' rations. The three who went on had no depot behind them,
no ship expecting them and no possibility of being found. From here they had to
feed themselves and their dogs off the sea.

The route is [[artifact:karluk-cae-002]]. It is a thin track, and honestly so:
Stefansson gives only three numeric positions for the entire crossing, and one of
the three - a stated latitude of 75 degrees 15 minutes on 23 April - is two
degrees north of the position he gives two days later, which cannot be right as
printed. The points between late April and late June are interpolated.

## Living on the sea

They killed seals. That was the whole method, and it worked: the party ate, the
dogs ate, the blubber burned for fuel and light. Storkerson later complained in
print that they killed more than they needed because the third man could not
believe a seal would ever appear again, and that hauling the surplus through the
spring slush cost them days. The complaint tells you how completely the method
had succeeded.

The ice carried them east as well as north. On 11 June a sounding found bottom at
668 metres, on 15 June at 350, and land birds began to appear - snow buntings,
jaeger gulls, then king eiders and old squaw ducks. On 22 June the soundings were
down to about fifty metres, and from a low hummock Stefansson saw through the
crags of a pressure ridge "something dark and uniform in outline which I felt
sure was land free from snow." Storkerson and Andreasen, who had been fooled
before by dirty ice and banks of fog, were not convinced, and by the time the
three of them got to the ridge the fog had come down. Next morning the diary was
certain: "The land is no longer problematic. It is in plain sight in the form of
three hills... The distance to the land is not less than ten miles and may be a
good deal more."

They came ashore at ten past eight on the evening of 25 June 1914 on
[[place:Norway Island]] off the west coast of [[place:Banks Island]].
[[artifact:karluk-cae-030|"Ninety-six days out from the Alaska coast... Our first
sleep on the land floe had a comfort and security about it that we had not known
for over ninety days."]] The chapter heading of his book says ninety-three days
and the text says ninety-six; the discrepancy is Stefansson's own.

A few days later they found that the larger island next inshore was on no chart
either, and named it Bernard Island for the captain whose head had been stitched
on the second day out. The [[artifact:karluk-cae-031|record they left in a beacon
there]] is worth reading for its tone: it tells any relief vessel exactly where
to winter, how far along the beach to gather driftwood, when to start hunting
caribou - and ends by telling them not to worry, because the rifles are in order.

## And then to Cape Kellett

They spent the summer hunting on Banks Island, and on 1 September started south
along the west coast. On 10 September Stefansson recognised the outline of
[[place:Cape Kellett]] from a hilltop and could see no ship and no beacon. The
next day he found fresh footprints and then the masts of the schooner
[[vessel:Mary Sachs]], hauled out on the beach: the base existed,
[[person:George Hubert Wilkins]] had got her there, and the Northern Party had
somewhere to winter after all. In the south they had been given up for dead.
[[artifact:karluk-cae-059|Unloading the schooner]] had gone on ten days before
they walked in.

Cape Kellett became the base for the next two years, and the ice journeys of 1915
and 1916 that reached [[place:Meighen Island]] and the rest started from it. See
[[page:karluk-cae-new-islands]].

## What it proved, and what it did not

It proved that a small party could live on the moving polar pack for three months
on what it shot. That is a real result, and it changed how the Arctic was
travelled: [[person:Storker Storkerson]]'s drift of 1918 and every later ice
journey took it as given.

It did not prove, as Stefansson liked to imply, that the Arctic was
straightforwardly friendly. The men who could do it were a handful, most of them
Inupiat and Inuvialuit hunters or men trained by them, and the same five years
killed eleven people on the [[vessel:Karluk]] and lost [[person:Peter Bernard]]
and [[person:Karl Thomsen]] on the ice of M'Clure Strait. The method was sound.
The margin was not wide.
