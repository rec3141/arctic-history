# The Fifth Thule Expedition, 1921-1924

The third strand of this topic has nothing to do with the Canadian Arctic
Expedition except that it was in the field at the same time, over some of the
same country, asking different questions. Where [[person:Vilhjalmur Stefansson]]
wanted new land, the Danes wanted to know where the Inuit had come from.

## The plan and the base

The Fifth Thule Expedition was [[person:Knud Rasmussen]]'s. He was born at
Ilulissat in Greenland to a Danish missionary father and a Greenlandic-Danish
mother, spoke Kalaallisut as a first language, and had already made four
expeditions out of the trading station he and [[person:Peter Freuchen]] founded
at Thule in 1910. The fifth was the big one: to travel from Greenland to the
Pacific and collect, from every Inuit group along the way, the material remains,
the language and the stories that would show whether all these people were one
people, and where they had come from.

The schooner left Copenhagen in June 1921, called at Thule on 3 August to engage
the Inughuit hunters and dog drivers on whom everything would depend, and went
on through Hudson Strait and round the north of Southampton Island. In September
1921 the party found a harbour at a small, uninhabited and unmapped island in
[[place:Foxe Basin]] and spent a month building a house there. They called the
house the Blow-hole, for the wind, and the island [[place:Danish Island]].
Rasmussen's own observation put it at 65 degrees 54 minutes north, 85 degrees 50
minutes west; the modern position is about two degrees further east, which is a
fair measure of how thin the charts of Foxe Basin still were in 1921. See
[[artifact:karluk-cae-007|the expedition's track]].

The scientific staff was small and each man had a region. Peter Freuchen mapped
the coasts of the Melville Peninsula and Baffin Island; in the winter of 1923 he
was caught out in a blizzard, froze his left foot, cut away the gangrenous toes
himself and later lost the leg below the knee. [[person:Kaj Birket-Smith]] and
[[person:Helge Bangsted]] went inland to the Caribou Inuit of the Barren Grounds.
[[person:Jacob Olsen]], a West Greenlandic catechist, worked as interpreter and
collector and wrote his own account of the expedition in Greenlandic.
[[person:Therkel Mathiassen]] dug.

## Mathiassen at Naujan and Iglulik

Mathiassen was twenty-nine and an archaeologist, and what he did in the summers
of 1922 and 1923 changed the subject. At [[place:Naujan]] on Repulse Bay he
excavated a village of winter houses built of stone, whalebone and turf, full of
harpoon heads, sledge shoes, snow knives and the bones of bowhead whales. He
found the same culture again at [[place:Iglulik]], at Ponds Inlet and at
Malerualik on King William Island. It was not the culture of the people living
there in 1922: the Iglulingmiut of the 1920s did not hunt bowhead whales and did
not build houses like these.

Mathiassen named it the Thule culture, after the station in Greenland, and argued
that it had spread eastward from Alaska across the whole Arctic, carrying the
whale hunt with it, and that the modern Inuit of the central Arctic were its
descendants, reduced to sealing and caribou hunting when the whales became
unreachable. His report, [[source:mathiassen1927archaeology]], is still the frame
of eastern Arctic archaeology; the topic [[topic:thule-dorset]] follows the
argument forward, through [[person:Diamond Jenness]]'s identification of an
older, un-Thule culture that he called Dorset, to the radiocarbon dates that have
since pushed the migration later than Mathiassen thought.

Birket-Smith drew the opposite conclusion from his own material. The Caribou
Inuit, he wrote in [[source:birketsmith1929caribou]], were a relic inland people,
proto-Eskimo, from whom the coastal Inuit had descended - the Arctic peopled from
the forest edge outward rather than from Alaska eastward. He and Mathiassen
argued it out in print for years. Mathiassen was right, and the excavated
evidence is why.

## The seasons of digging, dated

Mathiassen's own introduction to [[artifact:karluk-cae-063|volume IV of the
Report]] is the only place the archaeological work is set out season by season,
and it is what fixes the dates on [[artifact:karluk-cae-007|the expedition's
track]]. The schooner reached Danish Island on **18 September 1921**, so late
that nothing could be dug that autumn: [[artifact:karluk-cae-069|the passage is
quoted here]]. In the **summer of 1922** Mathiassen excavated for two months at
[[place:Naujan]] on Repulse Bay, joined for the last month by
[[person:Jacob Olsen]], with a small dig at Aivilik as well; in the middle of
August he crossed to Kuk in [[place:Duke of York Bay]] on Southampton Island and
worked there until **6 September 1922**, the day an attempt to get off the island
failed. In the **summer of 1923** he dug at [[place:Mittimatalik]] - the old
settlements of [[place:Qilalukan]] and Mitimatalik, and [[place:Button Point]] on
Bylot Island - while [[person:Knud Rasmussen]] dug on King William Island,
[[person:Peter Freuchen]] at Chesterfield Inlet, [[person:Kaj Birket-Smith]] on
Sentry Island and [[person:Helge Bangsted]] on Vansittart Island. Mathiassen and
Birket-Smith went home that autumn and Freuchen carried the Qilalukan excavation
on through the **summer of 1924**. Ten sites in all, and about 6,400 catalogued
specimens, dug with no proper tools into ground that thawed two or three
centimetres a day: Mathiassen says it took him more than a month to reach the
bottom of one levelled house ruin.

## The texts

Both of the volumes this page leans on are now held here in full.
[[artifact:karluk-cae-063]] is volume IV, Mathiassen's *Archaeology of the
Central Eskimos*, and [[artifact:karluk-cae-062]] is volume V, Birket-Smith's
*The Caribou Eskimos* - each written in Danish, published in English at
Copenhagen in W. E. Calvert's translation, and taken here from the Wikisource
transcription of the scanned original. Two chapters of Mathiassen's volume are
missing from that transcription, and they are the two a reader would want first:
chapter 1, the Naujan find, and chapter 2, the archaeology of northern Baffin
Land. For those there is only [[artifact:karluk-cae-064|the page images]].

## The sledge journey

On about 11 March 1923 Rasmussen left Danish Island for Alaska with two sledges,
twenty-four dogs and two companions: [[person:Qaavigarsuaq]], called Miteq, then
twenty-two, and his cousin [[person:Arnarulunnguaq]], then twenty-eight, both
from Thule. "Two continents lay between us and home."

They went north through Committee Bay to Pelly Bay, spent the summer of 1923 at
Malerualik among the Netsilingmiut - the material of
[[source:rasmussen1931netsilik]] - crossed Queen Maud Gulf and the Kent Peninsula
in the autumn, wintered among the Copper Inuit of Coronation Gulf, and went west
along the mainland coast through Cape Bathurst, the Mackenzie delta and
[[place:Herschel Island]] to Point Barrow in May 1924. They reached Kotzebue in
August and [[place:Nome]] on the mail schooner Silver Wave on 31 August 1924,
then crossed Bering Strait to East Cape in Siberia to see the Asiatic Inuit
before turning for home. Rasmussen's own figure for the whole thing is "say,
20,000 miles; more or less."

Arnarulunnguaq was the first Inuit woman to cross Arctic North America. She and
Qaavigarsuaq went on from Nome to Seattle, New York and Copenhagen, and back to
Greenland by ship - round most of the world.

What Rasmussen brought back was not chiefly a map. It was
[[source:rasmussen1929intellectual]] and [[source:rasmussen1931netsilik]] and the
volumes that followed them: songs, hunting law, taboo, the words of named people.
He could do this because he spoke the language and the people he met could hear
their own speech in his. That is the difference between the Fifth Thule
Expedition and almost every other expedition on this map, and it is why the
Report of the Fifth Thule Expedition is still read in Nunavut and Greenland when
the geographical reports beside it are not.
