# Stefansson's new islands: Brock, Borden, Mackenzie King, Meighen and Lougheed

Everything else in this topic happened somewhere else. This is the part that
happened where the ship is now.

Between 1915 and 1916 three or four men on foot, hauling sledges over the moving
ice north of Banks Island, put the last large pieces of the Canadian Arctic
Archipelago on the map. No aircraft, no depots laid by ship, no relief party
behind them: they lived on the seals they killed as they went. What they walked
onto is the northwest corner of the Queen Elizabeth Islands, and it is the last
land of that size anywhere on earth to be added to a chart by men on foot.

## Why anyone was up there at all

[[person:Vilhjalmur Stefansson]] believed two things that his contemporaries
thought reckless. The first was that a trained party could live indefinitely on
the moving pack ice of the polar sea by hunting seals, so that a journey was
limited only by the men's willingness, not by what they could haul. The second
was that there was probably unknown land in the unsurveyed triangle north of
Prince Patrick Island - the area he called "the region of maximum
inaccessibility."

He tested the first proposition in 1914 on the journey from
[[place:Martin Point]] to [[place:Norway Island]], described in
[[page:karluk-cae-ninety-six-days]]. He tested the second in 1915 and 1916. The
1915 and 1916 journeys are [[artifact:karluk-cae-003]] and
[[artifact:karluk-cae-004]] on the map.

## 1915: the land north of Prince Patrick Island

Four men left the base at [[place:Cape Kellett]] on Banks Island on 20 February
1915 - Stefansson, [[person:Storker Storkerson]], [[person:Ole Andreasen]] and
[[person:Karl Thomsen]] - and left the land at [[place:Cape Prince Alfred]] on 5
April. They pushed northwest to a farthest north of 76 degrees 40 minutes and a
farthest west of about longitude 131, then were driven back south by the
currents and came ashore on [[place:Prince Patrick Island]] near Land's End on 4
June.

They then worked up the island's northeast coast, filling the gap between the
coastlines mapped by McClintock and Mecham during the Franklin search sixty
years before. On 15 June 1915, at 9:58 in the evening, Stefansson opened a cairn
at Cape McClintock and found McClintock's own record inside, dated "P. M., June
15, 1853" - [[artifact:karluk-cae-032|sixty-two years earlier to the nearest
half day]]. He took the original away for the Department of the Naval Service and
left a copy of his own in a cairn rebuilt from the same stones. It is a good
measure of how little had changed on that coast in six decades. A second
McClintock record, in an iron-hooped barrel on the north coast of Melville
Island, turned up the following year: [[artifact:karluk-cae-013]].

Three days later, at two in the morning on 18 June, Storkerson climbed a piece of
ice forty feet high and saw land to the northeast. Stefansson was five miles
behind him and read what Storkerson was seeing off his behaviour through field
glasses before he could see anything himself. [[person:Ole Andreasen]] was the
first man ashore, at 1:50 a.m. on 19 June, at the point they named
[[place:Cape Murray]]. On 20 June they hoisted the flag and left
[[artifact:karluk-cae-033|a record of possession]] in a gravel mound.

**A caution about the names.** Stefansson's narrative calls all of this one
island, Borden Island, after the prime minister. On the modern map the land he
walked in June 1915 is three islands: [[place:Brock Island]],
[[place:Borden Island]] and [[place:Mackenzie King Island]]. Brock was named for
Reginald Brock of the Geological Survey; the division of "Borden Island" into
Borden and Mackenzie King was not made until 1947, from Royal Canadian Air Force
photographs, when the channel now called Wilkins Strait was seen from the air.
Nobody on the ice in 1915 or 1916 noticed it. This is worth remembering when
comparing Stefansson's coastlines with a modern chart: his surveys were done in
fog, on foot, with a sextant and a chronometer whose rate he could not check, and
they are remarkably good, but they are not photogrammetry.

The party turned south on 22 June, crossed to Melville Island, went from Cape
Russell across M'Clure Strait to [[place:Mercy Bay]] - camping a mile from
McClure's monument of 1853, see [[topic:mcclure-investigator]] - and then made
the first crossing of the interior of [[place:Banks Island]], reaching Cape
Kellett on 9 August. Two days later [[person:Louis Lane]]'s whaler
[[vessel:Polar Bear]] arrived to rescue a party that had walked home under its
own power. Stefansson bought the ship.

## 1916: Meighen and Lougheed

The 1916 journey was longer than any of them: 229 days from the ship base at the
schooner [[vessel:North Star (Canadian Arctic Expedition)|North Star]] on 2 March
to the return to [[place:Liddon Gulf]] on 16 October, of which 212 days were
counted from [[person:Natkusiak]]'s hunting camp at Cape Prince Alfred.

The route ran along the north coast of Banks Island - where on 22 March they
found that the great bay charted east of Cape M'Clure simply does not exist -
across to Melville Island, up to [[place:Cape Murray]] on Brock Island on 3 May,
and then over the polar ice northeast to [[place:Isachsen|Cape Isachsen]] on
Ellef Ringnes Island, reached on the last day of May. [[place:Ellef Ringnes Island]]
and [[place:Amund Ringnes Island]] had been reached by Sverdrup's Norwegians in
1900-02; see [[topic:sverdrup-bernier-peary]].

On 4 June [[person:Aarnout Castel]]'s support party turned back with Natkusiak
and [[person:Emiu]], leaving three men - Stefansson, [[person:Harold Noice]] and
[[person:Karsten Andersen]] - to go on northeast along the edge of the land floe.
Stefansson had noticed that the tidal currents ran southeast and northwest, which
happens in a strait and not in an open sea, and concluded that Sverdrup's "Crown
Prince Gustav Sea" could not be open water. He was right.

On 12 June, about four in the afternoon, Karsten Andersen saw land some twenty
miles off. Stefansson himself saw it the next morning. They camped a hundred
yards off it on 14 June and reached it at 12:15 a.m. on 15 June, and Stefansson
- who had a bad ankle and, more to the point, knew what the moment was worth -
[[artifact:karluk-cae-011|stayed in camp and let Noice step ashore first]]. The
[[artifact:karluk-cae-010|record they left in a beacon]] on a hill half a mile
inland gives the position as about 79 degrees 53 minutes north. This is
[[place:Meighen Island]], named for Arthur Meighen, then solicitor general: about
950 square kilometres, ice-capped, and the last island of any size added to the
map of Arctic Canada from the sea ice.

From Meighen they went south down [[place:Hassel Sound]], landing on Amund
Ringnes Island on 3 July at latitude 78 degrees 44 minutes, taking thirty hours of
tidal observations near 78 degrees 04 minutes on 18-19 July, and finding thirteen
miles further south a cairn left by MacMillan's Crocker Land expedition on 28
April 1916 - two expeditions crossing the same empty ground within three months.
They reached the south point of King Christian Island on 3 August, and on 9 August
camped on [[place:Lougheed Island]], where they stayed until 3 September waiting
for the sea to freeze. Their own observation put the camp at latitude 77 degrees
09 minutes 30 seconds north.

Stefansson's diary on leaving records what the whole argument of his life came
down to: [[artifact:karluk-cae-012|"It is a hospitable if not a very pretentious
place. We have not been hungry nor uncomfortable."]]

They crossed to Borden Island on 9-15 September and came down to
[[place:Cape Grassy]] on Melville Island on 7 October. Three weeks later
Storkerson's party, hunting on the south coast, walked into
[[place:Winter Harbour]] and found four and a half tons of food that
[[person:Joseph-Elzear Bernier]] had left there from the
[[vessel:C.G.S. Arctic]] on 1 September 1910 - a thousand pounds of pilot bread,
a thousand of flour, sugar, tea, salt pork, still sound after six years. It fed
the fourth winter.

## What it was worth, and what it cost

The geography was real and it lasted: five islands, a strait where Sverdrup had
drawn open sea, soundings and tidal observations across a quarter of the
archipelago, and the demonstration that men could work up there for a whole
season out of a rifle rather than a supply ship. The cost is on the same page.
[[person:Peter Bernard]] and [[person:Karl Thomsen]] set out from Cape Kellett in
December 1916 to bring sledges and mail across to Melville Island, against
Stefansson's written orders and meaning only to help; their last note is dated
22 December 1916, twenty miles north-northeast of Mercy Bay, and Castel found
their sledges the following May and nothing else.

And none of it would have happened without the people whose names Stefansson
records less carefully than his own latitudes. [[person:Natkusiak]] laid the meat
depots on Melville Island that the 1916 and 1917 journeys ran on, and later
settled on Banks Island as Billy Banksland; Sachs Harbour, named for the schooner
[[vessel:Mary Sachs]], grew where his family trapped. Emiu and
[[person:Palaiyak]] drove sleds and hunted. The fur clothing that made a
seven-month journey survivable was cut and sewn by [[person:Elvina Storkerson]],
[[person:Jennie Thomsen]] and [[person:Mrs Lopez]]. Stefansson calls all three
only by their husbands' surnames, though he prints their husbands' given names,
their daughters' names and every dog's. Their own names survive because the
Canadian Museum of History catalogued the expedition's photographs
([[source:cmh2020canadian]]): Elvina Storkerson, daughter of the Danish schooner
captain Charles Klinkenberg, [[artifact:karluk-cae-053|photographed with her
daughters Martina and Aida]] aboard the Polar Bear in June 1916; and
[[artifact:karluk-cae-052|Jennie Thomsen]], indoors at Cape Kellett at Christmas
1914, two years before her husband walked out onto M'Clure Strait and did not
come back.

## What the photographs show

The Canadian Museum of History released about four thousand of the expedition's
negatives to Wikimedia Commons in 2020 ([[source:cmh2020canadian]]), and the
museum's captions carry dates and names that Stefansson's book does not. They put
firm positions on stretches of the journey the narrative leaves vague.

On **13 April 1916** Wilkins photographed
[[artifact:karluk-cae-060|Storkerson's deserted snow house and meat cache in
Liddon Gulf]], steam still coming out of the roof hole. The museum's own note
calls it the first photograph ever taken of [[place:Melville Island]]. Three days
earlier he had photographed [[artifact:karluk-cae-068|Natkusiak and his dog on
the ice of M'Clure Strait]]; the dog, [[animal:Mike]], has a name in the museum's
caption and none in the book, which is true of most of the working animals
gathered in [[topic:animals]]. The same day at Liddon Gulf he turned the camera on
[[artifact:karluk-cae-061|a sandstone outcrop above the camp]].

On **21 May 1916** Stefansson photographed [[artifact:karluk-cae-065|Castel and
Noice on the northwest coast of Borden Island]], loaded and about to start for
[[place:Isachsen|Cape Isachsen]] "and exploration beyond". The exploration beyond
was [[place:Meighen Island]]. Three weeks later there is
[[artifact:karluk-cae-066|the camp on the second land]], 16 June 1916, with the
washing hung out to dry beside the tent, and
[[artifact:karluk-cae-050|the cairn and the flag]] Noice built the day before.

The year before, on **7 April 1915**, Wilkins had photographed
[[artifact:karluk-cae-067|Stefansson and Storkerson lashing a sledge into a raft]]
to cross a lead on M'Clure Strait. The sled-raft is the whole of the "friendly
Arctic" method in one picture: the party carried no boat, so the sledge became
one.
