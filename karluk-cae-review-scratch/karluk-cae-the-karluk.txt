# The Karluk, 1913-1914

The Canadian Arctic Expedition's flagship was a thirty-nine-year-old brigantine
built for the San Francisco cod fishery and later used for whaling. She was
bought in a hurry in 1913, and [[person:Robert Bartlett]] - who had taken Peary's
Roosevelt north twice and knew what ice does to a hull - said so plainly. She was
not an ice ship. Her loss happened in the Chukchi Sea, well west of the
archipelago the ship is working in now, but it is the story this topic is named
for and it shaped everything after it.

The whole voyage is [[artifact:karluk-cae-001]]; Bartlett's own narrative,
[[source:bartlett1916last]], is held here in full as
[[artifact:karluk-cae-043]].

## Beset

The Karluk left Esquimalt on 17 June 1913, called at [[place:Nome]] and Port
Clarence, took on [[person:Claude Kataktovik]], [[person:John Hadley]] and the
family of [[person:Kuraluk]] - his wife [[person:Kiruk]] and their daughters
[[person:Helen Kuraluk|Helen]], aged seven, and [[person:Mugpi]], not much over a
year old - off Cape Smythe in early August, and pushed east along the Alaskan
coast. On 13 August 1913 she was beset in the ice near Camden Bay, about 225
miles east of [[place:Point Barrow]]. She never moved under her own power again.

On 20 September [[person:Vilhjalmur Stefansson]] left the ship with five others -
[[person:Burt McConnell]], [[person:George Hubert Wilkins]],
[[person:Diamond Jenness]], [[person:Panyurak]] and [[person:Asatshak]] - to hunt
caribou ashore for ten days. A gale came up that night and took the ship west.
They never saw her again. Whether that departure was a hunting trip or an
abandonment has been argued for a century: Bartlett's book does not accuse,
[[person:William Laird McKinlay]] in [[source:mckinlay1976karluk]] does, and the
evidence is genuinely ambiguous. It is worth saying that Stefansson could not
have known the ice would move that night, and equally that he was the only man
who got off the ship before it sank.

The drift ran northwest, reached latitude 73 north on 15 November, then turned
southwest toward [[place:Herald Island]]. The last observation taken aboard, on 7
January 1914, was 72 degrees 11 minutes north, 174 degrees 36 minutes west.

## The crushing

On the evening of 10 January 1914 the ice opened the ship's side.
[[artifact:karluk-cae-021|"I went on deck again and gave the order, 'All hands
abandon ship.'"]] Everything movable had already been carried onto the floe.
The next afternoon she went down: [[artifact:karluk-cae-020|at four o'clock, with
the blue Canadian ensign at her main-topmast-head and the Victrola in the galley
playing Chopin's Funeral March]], in thirty-eight fathoms about sixty miles north
of Herald Island.

Twenty-five people and a cat were left on the ice at what they called
[[place:Shipwreck Camp]].

## The eight who were never found

On 21 January Bartlett sent the first mate [[person:Alexander Anderson]] with
[[person:Charles Barker]], [[person:A. King]] and [[person:John Brady]] toward
Wrangel Island by way of Herald Island. [[person:Bjarne Mamen]]'s supporting
party left them at open water near the island and turned back. Nothing was ever
found of the four. The revenue cutter Corwin later cruised all round Herald
Island and saw nothing; in 1924 a landing party found remains and a camp there.

On 5 February [[person:Alister Forbes Mackay]], [[person:James Murray]],
[[person:Henri Beuchat]] and [[person:Stanley Morris]] left on their own account,
man-hauling their own sledge, having signed a letter absolving Bartlett of
responsibility. Mackay and Murray were Shackleton veterans and thought they knew
better. [[person:Ernest Chafe]]'s returning party was the last to see them, about
twenty miles from Herald Island: three men on the traces, Beuchat a mile and a
half behind with his hands and feet frozen and partly delirious, Morris with
blood poisoning in a cut hand. None of the four was seen again.

## Wrangel Island

Bartlett brought the rest to [[place:Wrangel Island]] on 12 March 1914, landing
at Icy Spit - twelve people, including the two children. Helen, at seven, walked
the whole way and sometimes helped haul.

On 18 March Bartlett and Kataktovik left for Siberia with one sledge and seven
dogs. His written order to [[person:John Munro]] before going was
[[artifact:karluk-cae-024|"You will assemble at Rodgers Harbor about the middle of
July where I hope to meet you with a ship."]]

Three of those left died. [[person:George Malloch]] died of nephritis at
[[place:Rodgers Harbour]] towards the end of May 1914 and
[[person:Bjarne Mamen]] a few days later. [[person:George Breddy]] died of a
gunshot at Waring Point in the summer. Bartlett's book gives the whole of it in
thirteen words - [[artifact:karluk-cae-026|"Breddy accidentally shot himself
later on, making the third death on the island."]] - with no date, weapon or
circumstance. The survivors' diaries record suspicion among themselves. It has
never been settled.

What kept the others alive was hunting, and the hunting was
[[person:Kuraluk]]'s: Bartlett had put him in general charge of it. And every
person who walked off that ice did so in clothing made or mended by
[[person:Kiruk]], the party's only seamstress, who also carried her baby on her
back the whole way from Shipwreck Camp.

## The walk to Siberia

[[artifact:karluk-cae-006]] is the route. Two men crossed the moving ice of the
Chukchi Sea in seventeen days and came ashore near Cape Yakan on 4 April 1914.
Kataktovik, who was between eighteen and twenty and a widower with a baby
daughter at home, expected to die twice over: on the ice -
[[artifact:karluk-cae-022|"my mother, my father, tell me long time ago Eskimo get
out on ice and drive away from Point Barrow never come back"]] - and then at the
hands of the Chukchi, who he had been told were enemies. They were not; the
Chukchi villages fed and sheltered the two men the whole way east.

It was Kataktovik who [[artifact:karluk-cae-023|saw the land first]]. They reached
Emma Town near East Cape on 24 April, thirty-seven days out, and Emma Harbour on
16 May, from where word could finally be telegraphed. Bartlett crossed to St
Michael, Alaska, on 28 May and joined the revenue cutter Bear.

## The rescue, and the count

The walrus schooner King and Winge, under Olaf Swenson, reached
[[place:Rodgers Harbour]] on 7 September 1914 and Waring Point the next day; the
Bear took the survivors on and landed them at Nome on 13 September. Bartlett's
first question and the answer to it are
[[artifact:karluk-cae-025|the whole story in seventeen words]].

Of the twenty-five people aboard when Stefansson left the ship, eleven died:
Mackay, Murray, Beuchat, Morris, Anderson, Barker, King, Brady, Malloch, Mamen
and Breddy. Fourteen lived, among them the whole of Kuraluk's family. Mugpi,
carried across the ice as a baby, died at Barrow in 2008 as Ruth Makpii Ipalook,
the last survivor of the Karluk.

Kataktovik went home to Point Hope in August 1914. Bartlett was cleared by the
Canadian government of any blame for the loss of the ship and went on to
[[topic:sverdrup-bernier-peary|thirty more years in the Arctic]].
[[person:Fred Maurer]], who had survived Wrangel Island once, went back to it in
1921 with four others on Stefansson's scheme to claim the island for the Empire,
and died there in 1923; only Ada Blackjack, the Inupiaq seamstress hired at Nome,
came out alive. [[person:E. Lorne Knight]], who had drifted with Storkerson in
1918, died with him.

The fullest modern reconstruction, built from the diaries of McKinlay, Mamen,
Hadley and others, is [[source:niven2000ice]]. It and
[[source:mckinlay1976karluk]] are in copyright and are cited here, not reproduced.
