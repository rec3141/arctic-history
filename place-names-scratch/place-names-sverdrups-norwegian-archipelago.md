# Sverdrup's Norwegian archipelago, 1898-1902
[page · place-names · draft]

Between 1898 and 1902 sixteen Norwegians in the *Fram* mapped about 260,000 square kilometres of new coast west of [[place:Ellesmere Island]] — [[place:Axel Heiberg Island]], [[place:Amund Ringnes Island]], [[place:Ellef Ringnes Island]], [[place:King Christian Island]], the western fiords of Ellesmere — and named all of it. [[source:sverdrup1904newland]] is the record. It is a different kind of naming from anything else in this region, and a scientist can use it.

## The three men who paid

Three names carry the money, and they are the three biggest: [[artifact:place-names-240|Axel Heiberg Island]] after the Christiania consul and financier, and [[artifact:place-names-241|Amund Ringnes and Ellef Ringnes islands]] after the brothers who owned the Ringnes brewery. Between them these three private backers hold about 60,000 square kilometres of Canada. There is nothing else like it: no other national mapping effort put its funders on features of that size, and it happened because Sverdrup had no Admiralty to satisfy.

## Everything else is either the crew or the ground

**The crew.** Sverdrup worked steadily through his own ship's company, and the west coast of Ellesmere is now a Norwegian muster roll: [[place:Fosheim Peninsula]] (Ivar Fosheim, hunter), [[place:Schei Peninsula]] (Per Schei, geologist), Hassel Sound ([[person:Sverre Hassel]], who later went to the South Pole with Amundsen), Baumann Fiord ([[person:Victor Baumann]], second in command), [[place:Raanes Peninsula]] (the mate), [[place:Svendsen Peninsula]] (the surgeon, who shot himself at Fram Havn in the first winter), [[place:Simmons Peninsula]] (the Swedish botanist), Bay Fiord (the zoologist), Lindstrom Peninsula (the steward), Isachsen Land (the cartographer), Hendriksen Sound, Stolz Peninsula, Braskerud Plain — and Archer Peninsula for [[person:Colin Archer]], who built the ship. See [[artifact:place-names-245]].

**The ground.** This is the part that repays reading on the bridge. Sverdrup's men named what they saw, in plain Norwegian, and the Geographic Board translated most of it faithfully in 1910:

| Gaasefjord | goose fiord | a moulting ground |

See [[artifact:place-names-244]]. Trold Fiord gave its name to the Eureka Sound Group's Trold Fiord Formation; Stenkul Fiord is one of the best Eocene fossil-forest localities in the Arctic. Norwegian sailors' shorthand became stratigraphic nomenclature.

**The calendar.** Sledge parties out for months named by the day: Cape Maundy Thursday, Whitsunday Fiord, May Point, Cape Levvel ("live-well", for a festival held there), Jammerbugten, the Bay of Woe, for six days stuck in it in 1902. See [[artifact:place-names-246]].

**And one shout.** [[artifact:place-names-242|Eureka Sound]]: Fosheim, realising the water led north between Ellesmere and the new land, "called the sound Heureka Sund, and that is the name it has kept." Sverdrup gives no date for it, but the narrative places it between 27 April and 17 May 1901. Forty-six years later a weather station on Slidre Fiord took the name, and Eureka is now the second most northerly permanently inhabited place on earth.

## Names that Canada took away

Sverdrup called the western part of Ellesmere King Oscar Land, for his sovereign. The Geographic Board of Canada abolished it in 1910 along with Grant Land, Grinnell Land, Arthur Land, Schley Land, Jesup Land and North Lincoln, by ruling in one sentence that "Ellesmere island" means the whole tract between 76° and 84° N. See [[artifact:place-names-280]]. Norway did not renounce its claim to the [[place:Sverdrup Islands]] until 1930, when Canada paid Sverdrup $67,000 for his charts and papers; he died a few months later.

The one Norwegian name that Canada replaced with a Canadian person's is Svartevoeg, "black wall", the cliff at the north end of Axel Heiberg from which [[person:Frederick Cook]] started for the Pole in 1908. It is now Cape Stallworthy, after an RCMP constable, by a Board decision of 9 June 1960. See [[artifact:place-names-249]].

## What survives underground

The sedimentary basin beneath these islands is the Sverdrup Basin, and its formations carry the *Fram*'s crew: Schei Point, Hassel, Isachsen, Christopher, Bjorne, Blaa Mountain, Blind Fiord, Trold Fiord, Eureka Sound. Every petroleum geologist and every Arctic stratigrapher working in the Queen Elizabeth Islands uses the names of sixteen Norwegians and three brewers, usually without knowing it.
