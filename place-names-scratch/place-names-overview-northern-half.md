# Place names of the Queen Elizabeth Islands and the North Water: an overview
[page · place-names · draft]

*This page covers the northern half of the topic: everything north of Lancaster Sound and Jones Sound — the Queen Elizabeth Islands, the two sounds themselves, the North Water, and the Greenland shore of Baffin Bay and Smith Sound. The southern half of the archipelago is covered in the same topic by another set of pages.*

A chart of the Queen Elizabeth Islands is a document about who had power over whom. Almost none of the names on it describe the place. They record a nineteenth-century Admiralty's chain of patronage, a Norwegian brewery, a New York gentlemen's club, a Canadian cabinet, and — in the last thirty years, and only in patches — the people who actually live there.

## Six layers, laid down in this order

**1. The Inuit and Inughuit layer** is the oldest and the least visible on a printed chart. It is not a layer of "old" names: the Inuit place names of southern Ellesmere and Cornwallis Island were mostly given after 1953, because that is when Inuit were moved there. Older Inuit and Inughuit naming survives on the Greenland shore and in the Smith Sound crossing places. See [[page:place-names-inuit-and-inughuit-names-of-the-north]].

**2. The seventeenth century**: five names, all from one voyage. Bylot and Baffin in the *Discovery* in July 1616 named [[place:Smith Sound]], [[place:Jones Sound]] and [[place:Lancaster Sound]] after three London merchants who had put money in, and then the memory of the whole voyage was suppressed for two centuries because nobody believed it. Those three names are still the doorways: everything in this half of the archipelago is reached through one of them.

**3. Parry, 1819–20.** In eleven weeks Parry named most of the eastern Queen Elizabeth Islands after the Admiralty that had sent him and the friends who had helped him, working down the list as the ships sailed west. See [[page:place-names-parrys-names-1819-20]].

**4. The Franklin search, 1850–54.** Five separate expeditions, several hundred officers and sledge crews, and a naming free-for-all: ships' names, officers' names, patrons' names, royal palaces, and a scatter of jokes. See [[page:place-names-the-franklin-searchs-names]].

**5. The Norwegians, 1898–1902.** Otto Sverdrup's second *Fram* expedition mapped and named the whole western side of Ellesmere, Axel Heiberg and the Ringnes islands in plain Norwegian. See [[page:place-names-sverdrups-norwegian-archipelago]].

**6. The Americans on Smith Sound and Ellesmere, 1853–1917**, and then **the Canadians, 1904 onward**: Kane, Hayes, Hall, Nares (British but in the American tradition of subscription naming), Greely, Peary, MacMillan; then Bernier's Ottawa deputies, Stefansson's prime ministers, the RCMP, the weather stations and the air survey. See [[page:place-names-american-names-on-smith-sound-and-ellesmere]] and [[page:place-names-twentieth-century-names-in-the-queen-elizabeth-islands]].

**7. And since 1999, the Nunavut layer** — three renamings in this whole half of the archipelago, and a great many documented Inuit names that are not yet official. See [[page:place-names-what-nunavut-has-renamed]].

## How to read a chart's names

Four questions settle almost every name in this region.

**Is it a person or a thing?** British and American names are overwhelmingly people. Norwegian names are overwhelmingly things: Bjorne (bear), Hare Fiord, Stenkul (coal), Gaasefjord (goose), Trold (troll), Kritberg (white mountain). If the name tells you something useful about the ground, a Norwegian probably gave it.

**Did the namer ever see the place?** Usually not the person commemorated. [[person:Robert Dundas, 2nd Viscount Melville]], who has an island of 42,000 square kilometres, a sound, a peninsula and a Greenland bay, never went north. Neither did [[person:Amund Ringnes]], [[person:Ellef Ringnes]] or [[person:Axel Heiberg]], who between them hold three of the largest islands in Canada.

**Whose money?** Parry's names are the Admiralty's payroll; Peary's are the Peary Arctic Club's subscription list ([[person:Thomas Hamlin Hubbard]], [[person:James Boorman Colgate]], [[person:Frederick Gilbert Bourne]], [[person:Herbert L. Bridgman]]); Sverdrup's three biggest are his brewers; Bernier's are the Department of Marine and Fisheries; Stefansson's western islands are the Canadian cabinet that paid for the Canadian Arctic Expedition. Follow the money and you have read the chart.

**When was it fixed?** A name on a modern chart is there because a board decided so, and the decision has a date. The Canadian Geographical Names Database gives it for every feature: [[place:Bathurst Island]] 30 June 1910, [[place:Cornwallis Island]] 5 December 1905, Ellesmere Island 9 June 1982, [[place:Prince Patrick Island]] 3 March 1949, Qausuittuq National Park 1 September 2015. The single most consequential decision in this region was the Geographic Board of Canada's ruling of 1910 that "Ellesmere island" means the whole tract between 76° and 84° N — which abolished Grant Land, Grinnell Land, King Oscar Land, Arthur Land, Schley Land, Jesup Land and North Lincoln at a stroke. See [[artifact:place-names-280]].

## What is missing, and why

Three things are conspicuously absent from this half of the archipelago.

*Inuit names, on the printed chart.* [[source:white1910placenames]], the Geographic Board's 1910 gazetteer, put it plainly: the striking feature of a survey of Arctic place names is "the extreme paucity of native names, due partly to the uninhabited nature of the greater part of the region and, in a minor degree, to the lack of communication with the Eskimos owing to the failure to provide the expeditions with interpreters." The second reason is the one that mattered. The Inuit Heritage Trust has since documented 346 names for the Ausuittuq and Qausuittuq areas alone ([[source:iht2025placenames]]); almost none of them are on a marine chart.

*Derivations for the Franklin search names.* White again: Austin and Ommanney "named all the features discovered by their officers" and published no reasons; Belcher gave his officers a free hand, and of them only Lieutenant Mecham said why. [[person:George Henry Richards]], "who gave more names than any other officer in the expedition, gives absolutely no information." Several hundred names in the middle of this archipelago cannot now be explained at all.

*Women.* Apart from queens, empresses and princesses, and the wives and daughters of Admiralty secretaries, there are almost none: Lady Franklin, Lady Hamilton, Laura Lakes, Mount Blanche, Mittie Island — "after the daughter of one of my friends," wrote Inglefield, and did not give her surname.

## The record this page is built on

[[source:white1910placenames]] is the foundation: an alphabetical gazetteer of every name in northern Canada with its namer, year and derivation, plus a biographical appendix on the people commemorated, published by the Geographic Board of Canada in 1910 and long out of copyright. Where it inferred a derivation rather than finding one stated, it prefaces it with "probable", and this layer keeps that hedge. [[source:cgndb2026names]] gives the modern official form, language code and decision date. [[source:iht2025placenames]] gives the Inuit layer for the Ausuittuq and Qausuittuq areas. Everything else comes from the narratives themselves — [[source:parry1821journal]], [[source:belcher1855last]], [[source:mdougall1857eventful]], [[source:osborn1852stray]], [[source:nares1878narrative]], [[source:greely1886three]], [[source:peary1898northward]], [[source:sverdrup1904newland]], [[source:stefansson1921friendly]], [[source:dunbar1956arctic]] — in which the namers say, often in a single sentence, exactly what they were doing.
