Somebody has to decide. In Canada that has been, in turn, a ship's master, an Admiralty clerk, a federal board in Ottawa, and — since 1999 — a territorial government that for several years has had nobody in the chair.

## Before there was a rule: 1576 to 1897

For three centuries a name was official because a captain wrote it on his chart and the Admiralty engraved it. That produced duplicates on a grand scale. [[person:John Davis]] named Frobisher's bay Lumley's Inlet in 1587 because he did not know he was in it; the Board's verdict on that is four words long, "this name was not adopted". [[person:William Penny]] named [[place:Cumberland Sound]] Hogarth's Sound in 1840 for the same reason. White's gazetteer carries Sabine features on Ellesmere, King William Island, Melville Island, Melville Peninsula, Baffin Island and the Yukon coast, Adelaide capes on both Boothia and Victoria Island beside an Adelaide bay on Somerset Island, and a dozen Simpsons; its entries repeatedly have to say "name now obsolete", and the decisions list has to rule "Not Akpatok".

## The Geographic Board, 1897-1910

The Geographic Board of Canada was set up in 1897 to stop this, and in 1910 it published the first dictionary of northern names: [[person:James White]]'s *[[source:white1910placenames|Place-names, Northern Canada]]*, Part IV of the Board's Ninth Report, an alphabetical gazetteer of some two and a half thousand entries giving for each name the person it commemorated and the expedition that gave it ([[artifact:place-names-060]]). It is still the single most useful document about why the chart reads as it does, and most of the naval and Elizabethan derivations quoted on these pages come from it.

White knew exactly what he had produced. His introduction says that because nine-tenths of the coast was surveyed by naval officers, the names are "of Arctic explorers, of naval officers and officials and of Arctic exploring vessels", and then makes the observation that this whole topic turns on:

> the most striking feature in a general survey being the extreme paucity of native names, due partly, to the uninhabited nature of the greater part of the region and, in a minor degree, to the lack of communication with the Eskimos owing to the failure to provide the expeditions with interpreters.

The first half of that sentence is wrong — the coasts were not uninhabited, and the Inuit had names for all of them — but the second half is an accurate confession. He allows one exception: the Hudson's Bay Company coasts of Rae, Dease and Simpson, which he calls "a veritable directory of their contemporary chief traders, chief factors".

White's own work also shows the limits of a gazetteer compiled in Ottawa from books. He glosses **[[place:Nettilling Lake]]** as an "Indian name, meaning 'flat floor'", when [[source:boas1888central|Boas]] had printed the right answer twenty-two years earlier: *nattiq*, the ringed seal, "with seals". He makes [[person:Edward Sackville, 4th Earl of Dorset|Edward Sackville]] the seventh Earl of Dorset instead of the fourth, and [[person:John Pond]] "John Ponds". The Board fixed the spellings on the chart, but nobody was checking them against the people who lived there.

## The Board, the Nunavut Act, and the territory

Naming authority for what is now Nunavut passed from the federal board to the territory. The Geographical Names Board of Canada still keeps the register — the Canadian Geographical Names Database, which for every feature records the current official name, the date the Board decided it, a language code, and the forms that were official before ([[source:cgndb2026names]]). It is the database that lets you say, precisely, that Frobisher Bay and Frobisher are previously official names of the city now called Iqaluit, and that the bay is not.

What the database also shows is the shape of the restoration. It is happening feature by feature and it is slow: Kangiqtugaapik for Clyde Inlet, Ikpiarjuk for Turton Bay, Iqaluit Kangiqtunga for Shaftesbury Inlet, Iqaluit Nuvua for Cape Griffith, Kangiqtualuk Uqquqti for Sam Ford Fiord, Tasiujarjuaq for Carew Bay and for Soper Lake, Qikiqtarjuaq for Big Island, Kinngait Ilua for Cape Dorset Harbour.

## The bottleneck

Every one of those is a name the [[person:Lynn Peplinski|Inuit Heritage Trust]] and the communities put forward. There are about ten thousand more. The Trust's Place Name Program has recorded them from elders community by community since the 1990s and published them as paper maps, and its coordinator [[person:Zipporah Ungalaq]] has had to revisit communities first surveyed in 2007 because the people who gave the names have died ([[artifact:place-names-095]]).

The names are not becoming official. The Government of Nunavut has had no toponymist since 2019, and nothing the Trust has submitted has been approved since 2012, when hundreds of names went in on twenty-seven maps. The Trust's own page notes the contrast with Greenland and Nunavik, "where Inuit place names have long been recognized". The Kugaaruk case ([[artifact:place-names-096]]) is a small early sample of the machinery: a plebiscite in 1998, a false start in April 1999, cabinet approval, a federal agency list, an announcement in December, and a minister apologising because "the government did not have a policy in place".

So the honest summary for anyone reading a chart of Baffin Island in 2026 is this. The English names are a nearly complete record of who financed, commanded or was owed a favour by an expedition between 1576 and 1910. The Inuktitut names are a nearly complete description of the coast, and most of them are not printed.
