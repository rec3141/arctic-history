Cross Davis Strait and the naming works the other way round. On the Canadian side the chart is in English and the description is in Inuktitut and mostly unprinted. On the Greenland side the chart is in Kalaallisut and the Danish hydrographic office prints the meaning of every name as a navigational aid.

## What the Greenland Pilot does

The Danish Geodata Agency's *[[source:gst2020greenland|Greenland Pilot: Explanations of the Place Names]]* exists because the names are useful ([[artifact:place-names-098]]). Its own introduction says so:

> Most Greenlandic place names are a description of the nature or appearance of the location in question rather than an actual name for the place ... Greenlandic place names can sometimes provide information about the appearance and uniqueness of the named location, and can thereby help one to orient oneself when off a part of the extensive coast of Greenland, of which one may have no prior personal knowledge.

And it warns of the cost of that usefulness in the next breath: "The same individual place names can therefore be found in virtually all inhabited areas along the Greenland coasts, and they are often so close together that it can be difficult sometimes to distinguish between places with the same name."

The list runs to some two thousand names, each with its authorised spelling, the older spelling it replaced and its meaning ([[artifact:place-names-365]]). The glossary at the front is a pilot's vocabulary. *Kangerluk* is a fjord, *kangerlussuaq* a big one, *kangerluarsuk* a small one. *Ikerasak* is a sound, *ikkarluk* a skerry, *ikkattoq* the shallows, *ikkanneq* a bank. *Qeqertaq* is an island, *nuuk* a point, *inussuk* a cairn, *isortoq* "the turbid, muddy one". Once you have the generics, half the coast reads itself.

## The colony names and their replacement

The Danish colonies of the eighteenth century each got a Danish name — Godthaab, Jakobshavn, Holsteinsborg, Frederikshaab, Godhavn, Christianshaab — laid over a Kalaallisut one that had never gone away. Greenland has now reversed that, and reversed it further and earlier than Canada.

| Now | Meaning | Danish colony name | Founded |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **[[place:Nuuk]]** | "point; foreland" | Godthaab, "good hope" | 1728 |
| **[[place:Qeqertarsuaq]]** | "big island" | Godhavn, "good harbour" | 1773 |
| **[[place:Ilulissat]]** | "icebergs" (*iluliaq*, singular) | Jakobshavn | 1741 |
| **[[place:Sisimiut]]** | "the place with fox dens" | Holsteinsborg | 1756 |
| **[[place:Uummannaq]]** | "similar to a heart", for the mountain | Umanak | 1758 |
| **[[place:Upernavik]]** | "spring place" (the southern Upernavik Kujalleq is glossed "there where one spends the springtime") | Upernavik | 1771 |

The decisive rule is administrative and dated. The naming authority, [[source:oqaasileriffik2026names|Nunat Aqqinik Aalajangiisartut]] within Oqaasileriffik, the Language Secretariat of Greenland, took single authorisation into use on **1 January 1996**: where a place has names in several languages, only the Greenlandic name is authorised for official use. The Pilot still prints the Danish name in brackets during the transition — "Nuuk (Godthåb)", "Kangertittivaq (Scoresby Sund)", "Kangikajik (Kap Brewster)" — and warns that large fiords and land areas are still being converted.

That is exactly the step Nunavut has not been able to take. The Inuit Heritage Trust's page makes the comparison itself: unlike Greenland and Nunavik, "where Inuit place names have long been recognized" ([[artifact:place-names-095]]). See [[page:place-names-who-decides]].

## The layer underneath: Norse, English and Scottish

Three older layers survive on this shore.

The **Norse** one is thin and mostly reconstructed: Herjolfsnes, Gardar, Brattahlid and Hvalsey in the south are the Eastern Settlement of the sagas, and the runestone found on **[[place:Kingittorsuaq Island]]** north of Upernavik in 1824 is the northernmost Norse inscription known. See [[topic:norse-helluland]].

The **English** one is a single great name and a handful of small ones. **[[place:Sanderson's Hope]]**, the cliff sixty kilometres north of Upernavik, is where [[person:John Davis]] stopped on 30 June 1587 at 72° 12′ N, his farthest north, and named it for [[person:William Sanderson]], the London merchant who had paid for all three voyages ([[artifact:place-names-071]]). The Kalaallisut name of the cliff is Kingittuarsuk, and *kingittoq* in the Pilot's glossary means only "the towering". Davis also named **Gilbert Sound**, the fiord at Nuuk, and the **Land of Desolation** on the south-east coast, "wast, and voyd of any sensible or vegitable creatures". Baffin added **Cockin Sound** in 1616 for a London alderman.

The **Scottish and American whaling** layer is names in daily use aboard ship that never reached the chart at all — the watering places, the anchorages in the Whalefish Islands off Qeqertarsuaq where Franklin's ships took on stores in July 1845, the dog-buying and interpreter-hiring at Godhavn that every expedition to Baffin Bay depended on. See [[topic:whaling]] and [[topic:franklin-1845]].

For everything north of Melville Bay — Qaanaaq, Etah, the Inughuit names and the American donors' names on the Smith Sound coast — see [[topic:greenland-inughuit]], [[page:place-names-inuit-and-inughuit-names-of-the-north]] and [[page:place-names-american-names-on-smith-sound-and-ellesmere]].
