Every expedition that went up [[place:Smith Sound]] between 1853 and 1917 was privately funded, and it shows. Where Parry's names are a government department and Sverdrup's are a ship's company, the names on the east coast of [[place:Ellesmere Island]] and the north-west coast of Greenland are a subscription list. [[source:white1910placenames]] noticed it at the time: "nearly all the names of features along the eastern coast of Ellesmere island are named after citizens of the United States."

## Kane and Hayes: Philadelphia and Boston on the chart

[[person:Elisha Kent Kane]] named [[place:Kane Basin]]'s surroundings after the officials and scientists of the American republic: [[place:Kennedy Channel]] after the Secretary of the Navy, Dobbin Bay after his successor, Franklin Pierce Bay and Buchanan Bay after presidents, Cape Fraser after a professor at Pennsylvania, Cape Hawks after the president of the American Geographical Society, Bache Peninsula after the head of the Coast Survey.

[[person:Isaac Israel Hayes]] went further and simply worked down his donor list: Cape Lieber (a Columbia professor), Cape Leidy and Leconte Island (naturalists), Church Mountain (a landscape painter), Gould Bay (an astronomer in Cambridge, Massachusetts), Cape Banks (the Governor of Massachusetts), Cape Lincoln — not the President, but F. W. Lincoln, Mayor of Boston in 1860 — Cape Baker after "Richard Baker, Jr., Boston, Mass.", and Cape Louis Napoleon and Cape Frederick VII for good measure. He named [[place:Foulke Fiord]] and Port Foulke after [[person:William Parker Foulke]] of Philadelphia, who had paid.

Then [[person:Charles Francis Hall]] added Hall Basin and, from the *Polaris*, Cape Belknap after the U.S. Secretary of War and [[place:Robeson Channel]] after the Secretary of the Navy. By 1876 the American cabinet held the whole channel system.

## Nares, 1875-76: the one expedition that named its seamen

The British Arctic Expedition is the exception in this whole region, and it is worth pointing out on the bridge. [[person:George Strong Nares]] and his officers named the north coast of Ellesmere after their own lower deck: Cape [[person:James Good]], Cape [[person:Joseph Good]], [[person:Thomas Jolliffe|Jolliffe]] Glacier, [[person:John N. Radmore|Radmore]] Harbour, [[person:Thomas Stubbs|Stubbs]] Point, Cape [[person:Thomas Stuckberry|Stuckberry]], [[person:William Wood|Wood]] Point, Cape [[person:William Malley|Malley]], Cape [[person:Edwin Lawrence|Lawrence]], [[person:Robert Joiner|Joiner]] Bay, [[person:Adam Ayles|Ayles]] Bay and Ayles Point, [[person:John Simmons|Simmons]] Point, [[person:James Doidge|Doidge]] Bay. Petty officers, stokers, a captain of the maintop, a carpenter's mate. These are the men who hauled the sledges to 83°20′ and came back with scurvy, and they are on the map by name.

The officers are there too — Cape Aldrich, [[place:Judge Daly Promontory]], Parr Bay, Cape Egerton, Giffard Point, Markham Bay, Cape Rawson, Cape Stephenson, [[place:Archer Fiord]] — and so are the Royal Society and half the British establishment: Challenger Mountains after Nares' previous command, Evans Glacier after the President of the Geological Society, Cooper Key Mountains, Cape Fanshawe Martin, Keppel Head, Yelverton Bay, Frere Mountain, Norman Lockyer Island, Cape Eugenie after the exiled Empress of the French.

And, once, no person at all: [[artifact:place-names-285|Cape Columbia]], "name given by Nares, as it is the most northerly point of North America." The northernmost land in Canada carries a poetic name for the United States because it is a geographical fact, not a courtesy.

The ships are everywhere: Cape Alert, Discovery Bay, [[place:Discovery Harbour]], Cape Hecla for Parry's old ship. And the ships became stations: [[place:CFS Alert]] is HMS *Alert*, and the Ayles Ice Shelf, which broke free in 2005, is Adam Ayles, petty officer.

## Greely, 1881-84: the muster roll of a party that starved

[[person:Adolphus Washington Greely]] named [[artifact:place-names-283|Fort Conger]] after the senator who had backed the expedition and [[place:Lake Hazen]] after his own commanding officer — the Chief Signal Officer whose relief ships failed twice. He then named the country around Lake Hazen after his men: Cape [[person:James Booth Lockwood|Lockwood]], Cape [[person:David Legge Brainard|Brainard]], Biederbick Island and Lake Biederbick, [[person:Frederick Fowler Kislingbury|Kislingbury]] River and Valley, Mount Lynn, [[person:Maurice Connell|Connell]] Mountain, Cape [[person:Edward Israel|Israel]], Cape [[person:Francis Long|Francis Long]], the lakes Rogers and Appleby, and the Henrietta Nesmith Glacier "which I named for my wife". Four of the men so honoured were dead within two years at Camp Clay. See [[artifact:place-names-284]] and [[topic:nares-greely]].

Greely also named descriptively when there was nothing else to say: Hogback, "from the gently curving outline of its summit"; Cascade Ravine; Bellows Valley, which the English had named in 1875 "on account of the high and constant winds"; Musk-ox Valley, after the animals in it. [[person:James Booth Lockwood]] named a glacier "The Chinese Wall" from its shape and Mount Difficult from the trouble he had climbing it.

## Peary and MacMillan: the club and the college

[[person:Robert Edwin Peary]]'s names are the Peary Arctic Club's membership: [[artifact:place-names-287|Cape Thomas Hubbard]], Cape Colgate, Cape Bourne, Bridgman Mountain, Jesup Land and Cape Morris Jesup. From the summit he named Cape Colgate, on the west coast of Grant Land, on 24 June 1906, he reported "the faint white summits of a distant land" to the north-west; he crossed to Cape Thomas Hubbard four days later, reported it again, and named it Crocker Land after another donor. There was no land. See [[topic:crocker-land-1913]].

In north-west Greenland Peary named on a different principle. [[source:peary1898northward]] is full of names for what he saw and where he came from: Bowdoin Glacier, "named in honour of my alma mater", with Bowdoin Bay below it; Academy Glacier "in honour of the Academy of Natural Sciences, Philadelphia"; Navy Cliff; Independence Bay, "in honour of the date, July 4th"; the Glacier of the Scarlet Heart, from the colour of the ice; the Little Matterhorn; the Bronze Sphinx, from a profile in a bluff; Verhoeff Glacier, for the party member who died in it; Kahkoktah Cove, from the Inughuit word for white whale; Camp Tooktoo, "the Eskimo word for deer". Peary was perfectly willing to use Inughuit words for things; he was not willing to keep Inughuit names for places.

[[person:Donald Baxter MacMillan]], working out of Etah in 1914-17, named the Ellesmere fiords after his own scientists — [[person:W. Elmer Ekblaw|Ekblaw]], [[person:Maurice Cole Tanquary|Tanquary]], [[person:Fitzhugh Green|Green]], [[person:Jerome Lee Allen|Allen]], [[person:Harrison J. Hunt|Hunt]], [[person:Jonathan C. Small|Small]] — Borup Fiord after his dead friend, the Osborn Mountains after the man who paid, and the pass across Ellesmere "Sverdrup Pass, in honor of the stalwart old Norwegian explorer". See [[artifact:place-names-288]]. [[place:Tanquary Fiord]] is now the southern gate of [[place:Quttinirpaaq National Park]] and where most parties land.

## What this layer costs

The east coast of Ellesmere and the Greenland shore are among the most Inuit-inhabited parts of the region — the Inughuit have lived at [[place:Etah]] and along Smith Sound continuously — and they carry the fewest Inuit names on the chart, because the expeditions that mapped them were selling naming rights to their subscribers. The men who found the routes are in the narratives, not on the map. See [[page:place-names-inuit-and-inughuit-names-of-the-north]] and [[topic:greenland-inughuit]].
