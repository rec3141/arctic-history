Between 1818 and 1833 the whole east and centre of the archipelago was charted by two kinds of expedition — the Royal Navy's and one private venture — and the two left two different kinds of name.

## The Admiralty list, 1818-1825

[[person:John Ross]] in 1818 and [[person:William Edward Parry]] from 1819 wrote the Navy Office onto the coast in order of rank. Going west down Lancaster Sound in 1819 Parry named **[[place:Barrow Strait]]** for John Barrow, Second Secretary to the Admiralty and the man who had got the expeditions funded; **Croker Bay** for John Wilson Croker, the First Secretary, and did it, the Board notes drily, "when he found that the 'Croker mountains' of Ross were non-existent"; **[[place:Admiralty Inlet]]** simply "after the Board of Admiralty"; **[[place:Melville Peninsula]]** and Melville Island for the First Lord; **Beechey Island** "out of respect to Sir William Beechey", a portrait painter, because his son was a lieutenant aboard; and **Banks Island** "out of respect to the late venerable and worthy President of the Royal Society".

On 12 August 1819 he reached a great opening to the south. "This being the anniversary of the birthday of His Royal Highness the Prince Regent, it naturally suggested to us the propriety of honouring the large inlet, which we had been exploring, and in which we still were sailing, with the name of Prince Regent's Inlet" ([[artifact:place-names-087]]). It is a fair sample of the method: the name records the calendar of the British court, not the place.

Ross's 1818 run down the Baffin coast is the same in a Scottish key — **[[place:Kangiqtugaapik|Clyde]] Bay** "after Clyde river, Scotland", **Cape Eglinton** "in compliment to the noble Earl", **Home Bay** probably for the surgeon Sir [[person:Everard Home]], **Broughton Island** "after a friend", **[[place:Ponds Inlet|Pond's Bay]]** for [[person:John Pond]] the Astronomer Royal. Not one of them describes anything. Parry's one purely personal name is the exception that shows the rule: **[[place:Somerset Island]]**, "North Somerset, after his native county in England".

## Foxe Basin, and a chart drawn by an Iglulingmiut woman

Parry's second voyage, 1821-23, wintered at **Winter Island** — "so named by Parry, as he wintered at this island, 1821-22" — and then at Iglulik, and produced the densest patch of Admiralty naming anywhere in the region: Lyon Inlet for his captain, Amherst Island at Lieutenant Hoppner's request for the Governor General of India, Quilliam Creek "after Captain John Quilliam, of the Royal Navy" under whom Parry had served as a lieutenant in 1810, Ormonde Island, Hooper Inlet, Cape Fisher for the chaplain-astronomer, Mount Sabine for the physicist, Cape Brown for the botanist, Culgruff Creek "at the request of James Ross, after a friend". Fifty-seven names in the 1824 *[[source:parry1824journal|Journal of a Second Voyage]]* are Parry's own: four Admiralty officials, fifteen naval officers, sixteen friends, seven of his own crew, two naturalists, five patrons.

The one big name of that voyage came from somewhere else. Parry could not find the way west out of Foxe Basin. At Winter Island in the winter of 1821-22 an Iglulingmiut woman, [[person:Iligliuk]], drew him the coast to the north and the channel through it. He sailed to find what she had drawn, and named it **[[place:Fury and Hecla Strait]]** after his two ships ([[artifact:place-names-088]]). The strait is on the chart under the ships' names; the person who put it there is not. The Iglulingmiut name for the community at its western end, **[[place:Iglulik]]**, is in Grant's list "the place where there are igloos or houses". See [[topic:ross-parry]] and [[topic:inuit-oral-history]].

## Boothia: one merchant, over and over

In 1829 John Ross went north again in the paddle steamer *Victory*, paid for almost entirely by [[person:Felix Booth|Sir Felix Booth]], gin distiller and Sheriff of London. Ross spent four winters in the ice and paid Booth back the only way he could. He named the gulf after the "singularly generous and spirited individual, whose fame and deeds will go down to posterity among the first of those whose characters and conduct have conferred honour on the very name of a British merchant" ([[artifact:place-names-089]]). Booth's name is on the peninsula (**[[place:Boothia Peninsula]]**, Boothia Felix), the gulf, the isthmus, Booth Island, **[[place:Felix Harbour]]**, Cape Felix "after the founder of our expedition", and **[[place:Sheriff's Harbour]]**, "after Sir Felix Booth, sometime Sheriff for London and Middlesex". No other private backer in Arctic history is written so many times across one coast.

The rest of Ross's Boothia names are family, friends and the Parliamentary committee that later voted him £5,000: **Agnew River** after the M.P. who sat on it; Cape Isabella, named by his nephew [[person:James Clark Ross]] in 1829, "being that of my sister, on whose birthday it was discovered"; Isabella Louisa Island after Lady Parry; **[[place:Spence Bay]]** — now [[place:Taloyoak]] — "in compliment to my relation of that name"; **Matty Island**, named "in compliment to the fair donors of the beautiful silk colours, which we then displayed in honour of the day"; and **Eclipse Harbour**, where on 12 September 1829 "at midnight there was a visible eclipse of the moon ... I named the place Eclipse harbour". **[[place:Victoria Harbour]]** and **Victory Point** are the ship.

Ross also copied down a good deal of Netsilik geography. In January 1830 [[person:Ikmallik]] and [[person:Apelagliu]] drew him a coastline, and his *[[source:ross1835narrative|Narrative]]* carries Inuit names for the country round Neitchille — the Netsilik heartland at the isthmus — with his own attempts at their meanings. It is the earliest substantial Inuit place-name record for the central Arctic, and it is almost the only respect in which Ross's chart records what was already there. See [[topic:ross-victory]].
