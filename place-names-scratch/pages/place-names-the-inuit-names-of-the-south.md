Almost every place in the southern archipelago has two names, and the older of the two usually tells you something you can use. The English name on the chart says who was owed a favour in London; the Inuktitut name says what the place is like.

## Names that describe

Inuit names are descriptive, and the description is normally practical. **[[place:Kimmirut]]** is *kimmirut*, "heel", for the rock by the harbour that looks like the back of a foot. **[[place:Kinngait]]** is "where the hills are" — the Qikiqtani Truth Commission's community history says the name "describes the high, undulating hills surrounding the community's small, protected harbour". **[[place:Ikpiarjuk]]** is "a pocket", for the way the settlement sits in a bowl of hills. **[[place:Iqaluit]]** is the plural of *iqaluk*, the Arctic char — the place of many fish, for the river at the head of the bay. **[[place:Panniqtuuq]]** is the place of the bull caribou. **[[place:Naujaat]]** is "small gulls", for the nesting cliffs. **[[place:Kangiqtugaapik]]** — Clyde River — is, in Shelagh Grant's glossary, "a nice little inlet near a high point of land".

Franz Boas wintered in Cumberland Sound in 1883-84 and came away with the first systematic list, about two hundred names with their meanings, published in *[[source:boas1888central|The Central Eskimo]]* in 1888 ([[artifact:place-names-079]]). Reading it is like reading a pilot book. **Qeqerten**, the whaling station the English wrote Kekerten, is simply "the islands". **Nettilling**, the great lake, is "with seals" — *nattiq*, the ringed seal, which lives in it. **Kingnait** is "the high land". **Qingua** is "its head", the head of the sound. **Anarnitung** is "smelling of excrements". **Padli** is "with mouth of river". Two names on opposite shores of Cumberland Sound, **Talirpia** and **Saumia**, mean "its right side" and "its left side" — the sound described by somebody facing up it, which is how you would describe it to a person about to travel there.

Boas also recorded the names of the groups, which are place names with people attached: the Oqomiut of Cumberland Sound, the Nugumiut of Frobisher Bay, the Akudnirmiut of the middle Davis Strait coast — *akudnirn*, "the intervening country" — the Padlimiut of Padli Fiord, the Sikosuilarmiut of the south coast, whose name means the people of the place where the sea does not freeze.

Two passages of Boas are worth having in front of you, because they describe a way of naming that a printed chart cannot hold. The first is the largest division of all: "The whole of the land from Prince Regent Inlet to the plateau of Nugumiut is divided by the Eskimo into three parts, **Aggo**, **Akudnirn**, and **Oqo** - i.e., the weather side, the center, and the lee side" — and these "denote rather directions and the intervals between the localities situated in these directions", not bounded regions ([[artifact:place-names-170]]). The second is Cumberland Sound seen from its head: Qingua "its head", Talirpia "its right one", Saumia "its left one", Kingnait "the higher land as compared to the opposite shore" ([[artifact:place-names-171]]). Four names and one point of view.

Two Inuit-drawn charts survive in print from this region and both were used by the men who then renamed what was on them: [[artifact:place-names-163|Iligliuk's chart of the way out of Foxe Basin]], drawn at Winter Island in 1822, and [[artifact:place-names-164|the coastline Ikmallik and Apelagliu drew for John Ross]] at Felix Harbour in January 1830. Boas's own [[artifact:place-names-165|map of the tribes]] is a third: every group name on it is a place name with *-miut*, "the people of", on the end.

## Names that remember

Some names hold an event. **Mittimatalik**, the community at the mouth of Eclipse Sound, is glossed by Shelagh Grant as a place "believed by some Inuit to be the place where Mittima died" — a person whose name the settlement has kept for longer than anyone can now date. Peter Freuchen thought instead it meant "where the gulls land", for a sand bar once offshore, and Grant says plainly that the origin of the name is still in dispute. The Qikiqtani Truth Commission's Pond Inlet history adds a third layer: the people of the region are the Tununirmiut, "thought to mean people of the shaded place", or the Mittimatalingmiut, "people of Mittimatalik" ([[artifact:place-names-081]]). The readings are not reconciled; the community uses the name without needing them to be.

**Titiralik**, on the west side of [[place:Navy Board Inlet]], means "where there is writing", because Bernier's men cut their ship's name and the date into the rock there — an Inuktitut name recording a European act. **Tallurutit**, at Dundas Harbour, means the tattoo marks on a woman's chin, for the striped cliffs above it. **Qurluqtuq** on the Robertson River is "where the water falls". **Tulukkaat**, at the mouth of the Patricia River, is "the ravens". **Igarjuaq**, at Albert Harbour, is "the big fireplace", for a rock shaped like a *qulliq*, the soapstone lamp. Grant's Appendix 2 in *[[source:grant2002arctic|Arctic Justice]]* prints thirty-five of these with their meanings ([[artifact:place-names-080]]).

## Names that describe the land in relation to a mountain

The north Baffin regional names work outward from one landmark. **Tununiq** is "in the shade of the big mountain"; **Tununirusiq**, the Arctic Bay side, is "in the shade of the lesser mountains". A traveller who knows the two knows which side of the range he is on.

## What has been lost, and what is being written down

The Inuit Heritage Trust has been recording these names community by community since the 1990s and publishing them on topographic maps: Uqsuqtuuq, Arviat, Rankin Inlet, Igluligaarjuk, Iglulik, Sanirajak, Iqaluit and Kimmirut done by 2023, with Kinngait, Clyde River, Arctic Bay and Naujaat in progress ([[artifact:place-names-095]]). The programme's manager, [[person:Lynn Peplinski]], and its coordinator, [[person:Zipporah Ungalaq]], have had to go back to communities they first surveyed in 2007 because the elders who gave the names have died.

There is a warning in their fieldwork. At Resolute Bay in 2023 people gave them *Nanuit Itillinga* for Polar Bear Pass — a literal translation back out of the English on the map. Their notes from 2007 had the older name for the same valley: *Kitturajaaq*, described by the elders as to "cut into, but still attached, like a string under tension" — the Trust's own layer spells it Kitturajjaq. The English name says a bear crossed there. The Inuktitut name says what the ground does.

About ten thousand Inuit names still have no official standing in Canada. The Government of Nunavut has had no toponymist since 2019, and no name the Trust has submitted has been approved since 2012, when hundreds went in on twenty-seven maps. The Trust's own page puts it plainly: this is unlike Greenland and Nunavik, "where Inuit place names have long been recognized".
