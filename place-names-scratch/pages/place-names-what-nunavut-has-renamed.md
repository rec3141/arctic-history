Since 1 April 1999 the Government of Nunavut, not Ottawa, has had authority over geographic names in Nunavut, and the Inuit Heritage Trust's Traditional Place Names Program has a mandate under the Nunavut Land Claims Agreement to document them. In the southern half of the territory the effect has been visible: Iqaluit (1987), Kinngait, Kimmirut, Igluligaarjuk, Sanirajak, Uqsuqtuuq. In the northern half — the Queen Elizabeth Islands, Lancaster Sound and Jones Sound — very little has changed on the chart, and it is worth being precise about what has and has not.

## What has been renamed

**[[place:Quttinirpaaq National Park]]**, "the top of the world". Ellesmere Island National Park Reserve, created in 1988, took the Inuktitut name in 1999 and it was formally approved on 12 January 2001. This is the only major feature in the northern archipelago that the Canadian Geographical Names Database carries with the language code **ike** — Eastern Canadian Inuktitut — rather than **und**, undetermined. See [[artifact:place-names-307]].

**Qausuittuq National Park**, on northern [[place:Bathurst Island]], established 2015 and approved by the Board on 1 September 2015, in both an English and a French form (*Parc national Qausuittuq du Canada*) that keep the Inuktitut word. It is named for the community at [[place:Qausuittuq]] whose people asked for it. The Names Database still records its language as **und**. See [[artifact:place-names-306]].

**[[place:Tallurutiup Imanga]]**, the National Marine Conservation Area over [[place:Lancaster Sound]] agreed in 2019 — 108,000 square kilometres, the largest protected area in Canada. It is the waters of Tallurutit, [[place:Devon Island]], from the chin-tattoo lines in its cliffs. The sound itself is still officially Lancaster Sound in the names database (decision 7 March 1951); the protected area over it carries the Inuit name. See [[artifact:place-names-326]].

**Cape Stallworthy**, replacing Sverdrup's Svartevoeg, decided 9 June 1960 — a Norwegian descriptive name replaced by a Canadian policeman's. Not an Inuit renaming, but the clearest single case in this region of one nation's name displacing another's. See [[artifact:place-names-249]].

**Pituffik**, on the Greenland side: Thule Air Base was officially renamed Pituffik Space Base in 2023, restoring the Inughuit name of the place, though the Inughuit themselves were moved off it in 1953. See [[artifact:place-names-324]].

## What has not been renamed

The hamlets. [[place:Qausuittuq]] is still officially **Resolute Bay** in the Canadian Geographical Names Database (decision 4 September 1952) and [[place:Ausuittuq]] is still officially **Grise Fiord** (decision 1 October 1987) — Sverdrup's Norwegian name Grisefjord, which his own index glosses as "Pig Fjord" (the walrus-grunting story often attached to it is not in [[source:sverdrup1904newland]]). Both communities use their Inuit names in every other context. See [[artifact:place-names-305]].

Everything else. Melville, Bathurst, Cornwallis, Devon, Ellesmere, Axel Heiberg, the Ringnes islands, Prince Patrick, Meighen, Lougheed, Borden, Mackenzie King, Barrow Strait, Wellington Channel, Jones Sound, Smith Sound: unchanged. The 346 Inuit names the Inuit Heritage Trust has documented for the Ausuittuq and Qausuittuq areas ([[source:iht2025placenames]]) are almost all unofficial. The Trust says so itself: "Keep in mind these names are still being developed, many aren't official yet, and there may be errors," and notes that Nunavut is behind Greenland and Nunavik in getting Inuit names onto official maps.

## Why so little, here

Three reasons, and none of them is indifference.

**Population.** Official renaming in Nunavut follows community process, and there are two communities in this whole half of the archipelago, both founded in 1953 and after, with about 350 people between them. The Trust's northern layer exists because those families learned the land; there is no comparable body of names for Ellef Ringnes or Prince Patrick because no one has lived there.

**Language coding.** The Names Database's language field is not much help. Almost every name in the Queen Elizabeth Islands, including Norwegian ones like Gaasefjord and Bjorne and Inuktitut ones like Qausuittuq National Park, is recorded as **und**. Anyone asking the database which names in the Queen Elizabeth Islands are Inuktitut would get almost nothing back: only Quttinirpaaq is coded **ike** here, although in the southern half of the territory the community names — Kinngait, Sanirajak, Iqaluit, Taloyoak, Naujaat — mostly are.

**Layering rather than replacement.** The Trust's own note on [[artifact:place-names-260|Kanguq / Goose Fiord]] describes the alternative that is actually happening: a name "originally (1900) given in Norwegian (by Sverdrup), and finally, Inuktitut, demonstrating the variety of cultures present here over time." The Inuit names are being recorded beside the European ones rather than instead of them.

## For a reader on the bridge

If you are looking at a chart and want the Inuit name of what you are passing in this region, the chart will usually not have it. The Inuit Heritage Trust's Grise Fiord and Resolute Bay layer will, for southern Ellesmere, Devon, Cornwallis, Bathurst and the waters between; the Trust asks to be contacted for its most current data. On the Greenland side, use the Greenlandic and Inughuit forms: [[place:Qaanaaq]], [[place:Siorapaluk]], [[place:Savissivik]], [[place:Etah]] / Iita, [[place:Pituffik]], [[place:Qimusseriarsuaq]], Innaanganeq, Pikialasorsuaq.

And when the model or the chart gives you an English name, the useful next question is not "who was that?" but "who was paying?" See [[page:place-names-overview-northern-half]].
