### place-names-060 | text | 1910 | Place-names, Northern Canada (Geographic Board of Canada, 1910) | bib=white1910placenames pages=pp. 229-456 | lat=68.0 lon=-75.0 | people=[] | tags=["gazetteer,geographic-board,etymology"] | credit=Geographic Board of Canada; Internet Archive | licence=public domain | file=place-names/files/place-names-white1910-geographicboard.txt | url=https://archive.org/details/1911v45i13p21a_0745
Part IV of the Board's Ninth Report: an alphabetical gazetteer of the place-names of Arctic Canada, giving for each the person or thing it commemorates and the expedition that gave it. White divides Arctic exploration into three periods - Frobisher 1576 to Foxe and James 1631; Ross 1818 to Belcher 1852-54; Nares 1875-76 onward - and remarks on 'the extreme paucity of native names, due partly to the uninhabited nature of the greater part of the region and, in a minor degree, to the lack of communication with the Eskimos owing to the failure to provide the expeditions with interpreters.' The exception he allows is the Hudson's Bay Company coast explored by Rae, Dease and Simpson, which he calls 'a veritable directory of their contemporary chief traders, chief factors'.

### place-names-061 | place | 1987-01-01 | Iqaluit, once Frobisher Bay | bib=cgndb2026names pages= | lat=63.7467 lon=-68.517 | people=["Martin Frobisher"] | tags=["community,renaming,inuktitut"] | credit=Canadian Geographical Names Database (NRCan), record OATRP | licence=Open Government Licence - Canada | file= | url=https://geogratis.gc.ca/services/geoname/en/geonames/OATRP
Iqaluit, ᐃᖃᓗᐃᑦ, 'place of many fish' - the plural of iqaluk, the Arctic char that run in the river at the head of the bay. The settlement grew around the United States air base built in 1942 at the head of Frobisher Bay, and took the bay's English name; the bay in turn had carried Martin Frobisher's since 1576, when he took it for a strait to Cathay. The people of the town voted to restore the Inuktitut name and it became official on 1 January 1987. The Canadian Geographical Names Database still lists Frobisher Bay and the shorter Frobisher as previously official names of the same feature, and the bay itself is Frobisher Bay still. Language of the official name: Inuktitut (ike).

### place-names-062 | place | 1577 | Meta Incognita, the limit unknown | bib=best1578true pages= | lat=62.75 lon=-68.5 | people=["Martin Frobisher", "Michael Lok"] | tags=["frobisher,elizabethan,latin"] | credit=George Best, A True Discourse (1578); Internet Archive | licence=public domain | file= | url=
The peninsula between Frobisher Bay and Hudson Strait carries the only Latin name on the chart of the eastern Arctic. Best records that on the return of the second voyage in 1577 the Queen herself named the new land Meta Incognita - 'the unknown limit', or bound - a name meant for the whole of Frobisher's discovery and now attached to the peninsula alone. It is the oldest surviving European place name in the Canadian Arctic. See [[topic:frobisher]].

### place-names-063 | place | 1578 | Kodlunarn Island, the Countess of Warwick's Island | bib=white1910placenames pages= | lat=62.8167 lon=-65.4333 | people=["Anne Dudley, Countess of Warwick", "Martin Frobisher", "Charles Francis Hall"] | tags=["frobisher,elizabethan,inuktitut,mining"] | credit=Geographic Board of Canada, Place-names, Northern Canada (1910) | licence=public domain | file= | url=
A low island in the Countess of Warwick's Sound, off the north shore of Frobisher Bay, where Frobisher's men dug the black ore they believed was gold and raised a stone house in 1578. Frobisher named island and sound after Anne Dudley, Countess of Warwick, wife of the Cathay Company's governor. The Inuit name that survived is Kodlunarn - Qallunaat, 'the white men's island' - and it was Inuit oral tradition, three hundred years old, that led Charles Francis Hall to the site in 1861. Both names are on the chart today; the island is a National Historic Site. See [[topic:frobisher]] and [[topic:inuit-oral-history]].

White's entry for Hall Island quotes the voyage narrative: 'the master of the ship did land upon the first iland, and named it Hall's Iland after his own name' - Christopher Hall, master of the Gabriel in 1576 and of the Ayde in 1577. ### place-names-065 | quote | 1577 | 'Named Jackmans Sounde after the name of the maisters mate' | bib=white1910placenames pages=p. 380 | lat=62.6 lon=-66.2 | people=["Charles Jackman"] | tags=["frobisher,elizabethan,crew"] | credit=Geographic Board of Canada, Place-names, Northern Canada (1910) | licence=public domain | file= | url=
'This was named Jackmans Sounde after the name of the maisters mate, who had first liking unto the place.' Charles Jackman was master of the Ayde in 1577 and chief pilot of the Thomas Allen in 1578; he was lost in 1581 seeking the north-east passage. ### place-names-066 | place | 1578 | Loks Land, for the man who paid and was ruined | bib=white1910placenames pages= | lat=62.4333 lon=-64.6333 | people=["Michael Lok"] | tags=["frobisher,elizabethan"] | credit=Geographic Board of Canada, Place-names, Northern Canada (1910) | licence=public domain | file= | url=
The island at the northern gate of Frobisher Bay is 'after Michael Lok, who wrote an account of Frobisher's first voyage and was one of the venturers'. ### place-names-067 | quote | 1585-08 | 'Cape of God's Mercy, as being the place of our first entrance' | bib=white1910placenames pages=p. 395 | lat=64.8833 lon=-63.5333 | people=["John Davis"] | tags=["davis,elizabethan"] | credit=Geographic Board of Canada, Place-names, Northern Canada (1910) | licence=public domain | file= | url=
Cape Mercy, at the southern entrance of Cumberland Sound, was 'named by Davis on his first voyage, Cape of God's Mercy, as being the place of our first entrance for the discovery'. Its Inuktitut name in the Pangnirtung region is Saumia, 'its left side'.

### place-names-068 | place | 1585 | Exeter Sound, for the merchants who paid | bib=white1910placenames pages= | lat=66.7 lon=-61.3 | people=["John Davis"] | tags=["davis,elizabethan,merchants"] | credit=Geographic Board of Canada, Place-names, Northern Canada (1910) | licence=public domain | file= | url=
'Named by Davis, 1585, after Exeter, Devon, England, to commemorate the assistance given by merchants of Exeter towards the discovery of the North West passage.' ### place-names-069 | place | 1585 | Cumberland Sound: Kangiqtualuk, Cumberland, Hogarth's | bib=white1910placenames pages= | lat=65.3 lon=-65.6 | people=["George Clifford, 3rd Earl of Cumberland", "William Penny", "Eenoolooapik", "William Hogarth"] | tags=["davis,whaling,inuktitut,renaming"] | credit=Geographic Board of Canada, Place-names, Northern Canada (1910) | licence=public domain | file= | url=
The great sound of eastern Baffin Island has carried at least four names. Its Inuktitut name is Kangiqtualuk, 'the very large bay'; Grant's north Baffin list gives Kangigtuasluk, 'this large body of water is surrounded by high mountains'. Davis named it in 1585-87 after George Clifford, 3rd Earl of Cumberland, his patron, 'a famous mathematician and navigator'. In 1840 Captain William Penny, guided there by a chart drawn for him by the young Inuk Eenoolooapik, took it for a new discovery and called it Hogarth's Sound, after William Hogarth of Aberdeen who had been kind to Eenoolooapik in Scotland. Davis's name was restored. Cumberland Gulf was the official form until the Board changed it to Cumberland Sound. See [[topic:whaling]].

### place-names-070 | place | 1587 | Killiniq, once Cape Chidley | bib=white1910placenames pages= | lat=60.4 lon=-64.5 | people=["John Davis", "John Chidley"] | tags=["davis,elizabethan,inuktitut"] | credit=Geographic Board of Canada, Place-names, Northern Canada (1910) | licence=public domain | file= | url=
The southern gate of Hudson Strait. Davis named it in 1587 after 'The Worshipful M. John Chidley of Chidley in the countie of Devon, esquire', a friend who died two years later on a voyage to the Strait of Magellan. The Inuktitut name is Killiniq - the edge, the boundary - and it is still the boundary: Nunavut, Nunavik and Nunatsiavut meet there.

### place-names-071 | place | 1587-06-30 | Sanderson's Hope, for the merchant who found the money | bib=white1910placenames pages= | lat=72.2 lon=-55.9 | people=["John Davis", "William Sanderson"] | tags=["davis,elizabethan,greenland,merchants"] | credit=Geographic Board of Canada, Place-names, Northern Canada (1910) | licence=public domain | file= | url=
The great cliff north of Upernavik that marks Davis's farthest north, 72 degrees 12 minutes, in June 1587. He called it 'Sanderson his Hope' after William Sanderson, the London merchant adventurer who financed all three of his voyages - hope in both senses, of the passage and of a return on the money. White 1910 also records a Sanderson Tower on Baffin Island, 'named by Davis, after William Sanderson, merchant adventurer and friend of Davis'. The Greenlandic name of the cliff is Kingittuarsuk. See [[topic:davis-baffin-hudson]].

### place-names-072 | place | 1610-08 | Cape Wolstenholme and the Digges Islands: two adventurers at the gate | bib=white1910placenames pages= | lat=62.5583 lon=-77.8 | people=["Henry Hudson", "John Wolstenholme", "Dudley Digges"] | tags=["hudson,merchants,mutiny"] | credit=Geographic Board of Canada, Place-names, Northern Canada (1910) | licence=public domain | file= | url=
At the western end of Hudson Strait, Hudson in August 1610 named the mainland cape after Sir John Wolstenholme (1562-1639), 'one of the adventurers for the discovery of the North West passage', and the islands off it after Sir Dudley Digges (1583-1639), 'one of the principal promoters of the early voyages'. ### place-names-075 | place | 1631 | Salliq: Southampton Island and the Earl who rebelled | bib=white1910placenames pages= | lat=64.3333 lon=-84.6667 | people=["Luke Foxe", "Henry Wriothesley, 3rd Earl of Southampton"] | tags=["foxe,inuktitut"] | credit=Geographic Board of Canada, Place-names, Northern Canada (1910) | licence=public domain | file= | url=
Foxe named the island at the mouth of Hudson Bay after Henry Wriothesley, 3rd Earl of Southampton (1573-1624), Shakespeare's patron, who joined Essex's rising, was attainted, and was pardoned by James I. ### place-names-076 | place | 2020-02-24 | Kinngait, once Cape Dorset | bib=cgndb2026names pages= | lat=64.2333 lon=-76.5417 | people=["Luke Foxe", "Edward Sackville, 4th Earl of Dorset"] | tags=["community,renaming,inuktitut,foxe"] | credit=Canadian Geographical Names Database (NRCan) | licence=Open Government Licence - Canada | file= | url=
Kinngait, ᑭᙵᐃᑦ, 'high place' or 'where the hills are' - the QTC community history says the name 'describes the high, undulating hills surrounding the community's small, protected harbour'; Grant's list gives 'high mountains'. The region is also Sikusilaq, and its people the Sikusilarmiut, 'a reference to the place of open water in winter'. Luke Foxe named the cape south-east of the harbour in 1631 after Edward Sackville, 4th Earl of Dorset, courtier and promoter of colonisation, and the settlement took the cape's name. The hamlet's name became Kinngait officially in 2020; the Database now lists Cape Dorset as previously official, and Kinngait Ilua has replaced Cape Dorset Harbour and Kinngait the hill formerly Kingnait Hill. See [[topic:relocations-qtc]].

### place-names-077 | place | 1616 | Qikiqtaaluk: Baffin Island, Baffin Land, Helluland | bib=white1910placenames pages= | lat=68.0 lon=-70.0 | people=["William Baffin", "Robert Bylot"] | tags=["island,inuktitut,norse"] | credit=Geographic Board of Canada, Place-names, Northern Canada (1910) | licence=public domain | file= | url=
The largest island in Canada is Qikiqtaaluk, 'the very big island', in Inuktitut, and the region of Nunavut it gives its name to. Its English name honours William Baffin, master's mate of the Discovery in 1616, though Baffin never named it and it was Robert Bylot who commanded. White 1910 gives the entry bare - 'Baffin; island, Frank.' - with no derivation at all, and the Names Database records Baffin Land as a previously official form. The Norse Helluland, 'the land of flat stones', of the Vinland sagas is usually identified with its eastern coast; see [[topic:norse-helluland]].

The island at the eastern gate of Hudson Strait. The chart of the island is a memorial to a disaster that had not yet happened when it was begun and to the search that followed: Cape Felix and Victory Point from Ross's sledge journey of 1830, for Booth and for the ship; Point Franklin, also 1830, laid down seventeen years before Franklin's men walked past it; Cape Crozier, Gore Point, Erebus Bay and Terror Bay from McClintock in 1859, for the lost officers and ships; Cape Sabine, Irving Island for Lieutenant John Irving of the Terror; Jane Franklin Point, named by John Ross in 1830 for the wife who would spend the rest of her life searching; Matty Island, named by James Clark Ross 'in compliment to the fair donors of the beautiful silk colours, which we then displayed'; and the Tasmania Islands, named by McClintock in 1859 'at the request of Lady Franklin, in grateful acknowledgment of many proofs of affectionate sympathy received from the colony over which her husband presided'. ### place-names-095 | text | 2023-01-17 | The Inuit Heritage Trust's place-names programme, and the names that are not official | bib=jamal2023mapping pages= | lat=63.7467 lon=-68.517 | people=["Lynn Peplinski", "Zipporah Ungalaq"] | tags=["inuktitut,toponymy,nunavut,policy"] | credit=Nunatsiaq News; Inuit Heritage Trust | licence=copyright Nunatsiaq News and Inuit Heritage Trust; quoted for research | file= | url=https://nunatsiaq.com/stories/article/inuit-heritage-trust-maps-traditional-names-before-theyre-lost/
Thousands of Inuit names for the land are documented but not on the chart. The Inuit Heritage Trust's Place Name Program has recorded elders' names community by community and published them as paper maps - Uqsuqtuuq, Arviat, Rankin Inlet, Igluligaarjuk, Iglulik, Sanirajak, Iqaluit and Kimmirut done by 2023, Kinngait, Clyde River, Arctic Bay and Naujaat in progress - but the names have not been made official: the Government of Nunavut has had no toponymist since 2019, and no name the Trust submitted has been approved since 2012, when hundreds were sent in on twenty-seven maps. The Trust's own page notes the contrast with Greenland and Nunavik, 'where Inuit place names have long been recognized'. The article carries a good example of what is at stake: at Resolute Bay people now call Polar Bear Pass Nanuit Itillinga, a literal translation back out of English, while the elders' notes from 2007 give the older Kitturajaaq, 'to cut into, but still attached, like a string under tension', for the long valley itself. When a name is lost the description goes with it.

Pelly Bay - named by John Rae after Sir John Pelly, Governor of the Hudson's Bay Company 1822-52 - became Kugaaruk, 'little stream'. The account of how long it took is the interesting part. ### place-names-098 | text | 2020-07-08 | The Greenland Pilot's explanations of the Greenlandic place names | bib=gst2020greenland pages=pp. 8-30 | lat=69.25 lon=-53.53 | people=[] | tags=["greenland,kalaallisut,gazetteer,navigation"] | credit=Danish Geodata Agency, Greenland Pilot: Explanations of the Place Names | licence=copyright Danish Geodata Agency; quoted for research | file=place-names/files/place-names-greenlandpilot-placenames.pdf | url=https://eng.gst.dk/media/9097/gp-explanations-of-the-place-names_2015_skr_27_2020.pdf
The Danish chart authority prints, as a navigational aid, the meaning of every Greenlandic name on its charts - which is a thing no Canadian chart does. Its own reasoning is worth quoting: 'Most Greenlandic place names are a description of the nature or appearance of the location in question rather than an actual name for the place ... Greenlandic place names can sometimes provide information about the appearance and uniqueness of the named location, and can thereby help one to orient oneself when off a part of the extensive coast of Greenland, of which one may have no prior personal knowledge.' It then warns of the same trap the Inuktitut names of Nunavut set: 'The same individual place names can therefore be found in virtually all inhabited areas along the Greenland coasts, and they are often so close together that it can be difficult sometimes to distinguish between places with the same name.' The glossary is a pilot's vocabulary: kangerluk a fjord, kangerlussuaq a big fjord, kangerluarsuk a small one, ikerasak a sound, ikkarluk a skerry, ikkattoq the shallows, qeqertaq an island, nuuk a point, inussuk a cairn, isortoq 'the turbid, muddy one'.

### place-names-099 | place | 1979 | Qeqertarsuaq, once Godhavn: the big island and the good harbour | bib=gst2020greenland pages=p. 26 | lat=69.2472 lon=-53.535 | people=[] | tags=["greenland,kalaallisut,renaming"] | credit=Danish Geodata Agency, Greenland Pilot: Explanations of the Place Names | licence=copyright Danish Geodata Agency; quoted for research | file= | url=
Qeqertarsuaq, 'big island' - the Greenlandic name of both the island the Danes called Disko and the town on its south coast that they founded in 1773 as Godhavn, 'good harbour'. Godhavn was the northern seat of the Royal Greenland Trade and the harbour where almost every British and American expedition to Baffin Bay watered and took on dogs and interpreters. Greenland's names went through the same restoration as Nunavut's but earlier and more completely: since 1 January 1996 only the Greenlandic form of a name is authorised for official use, so that Godthaab is Nuuk ('point, foreland'), Jakobshavn is Ilulissat ('icebergs'), Holsteinsborg is Sisimiut ('the place with fox dens'), Frederikshaab is Paamiut and Godhavn is Qeqertarsuaq. The Greenland Pilot still prints the Danish name in brackets during the transition. See [[topic:greenland-inughuit]].

### place-names-160 | place | 1771 | Upernavik, the spring place | bib=gst2020greenland pages=p. 30 | lat=72.7868 lon=-56.1549 | people=[] | tags=["greenland,kalaallisut"] | credit=Danish Geodata Agency, Greenland Pilot: Explanations of the Place Names | licence=copyright Danish Geodata Agency; quoted for research | file= | url=
The Greenland Pilot glosses Upernavik as 'Spring place ... there where one spends the springtime', and the older form Upernivik as 'Spring place; place which one stares at'. It is the northernmost of the old Danish colony sites, founded in 1771, and the last port of call for expeditions bound up Baffin Bay - the Greely relief expedition brought its six survivors here in 1884. Sixty kilometres north stands Sanderson's Hope, [[place:Sanderson's Hope|Kingittuarsuk]], which Davis named in 1587 at his farthest north; kingittoq in the Pilot's glossary simply means 'the towering'.

### place-names-161 | place | 1758 | Uummannaq, similar to a heart | bib=gst2020greenland pages=p. 30 | lat=70.6747 lon=-52.1264 | people=[] | tags=["greenland,kalaallisut"] | credit=Danish Geodata Agency, Greenland Pilot: Explanations of the Place Names | licence=copyright Danish Geodata Agency; quoted for research | file= | url=
'Similar to a heart' - the Pilot's gloss for the heart-shaped mountain that stands over the settlement, and for Uummannarsuaq, the great one, which is Cape Farewell at the other end of Greenland. The same descriptive word recurs on the Canadian side: Uumanarjuaq is the Inuktitut name of Blacklead Island in Cumberland Sound, and Umanaq of several other hills. This is the trap the Greenland Pilot warns about - a name that describes a shape fits every place with that shape.

### place-names-162 | place | 1728 | Nuuk, the point, once Godthaab | bib=gst2020greenland pages=p. 22 | lat=64.1814 lon=-51.6941 | people=["John Davis", "Adrian Gilbert"] | tags=["greenland,kalaallisut,renaming"] | credit=Danish Geodata Agency, Greenland Pilot: Explanations of the Place Names | licence=copyright Danish Geodata Agency; quoted for research | file= | url=
Nuuk means simply 'point; foreland' - the same word the Pilot's glossary gives as the generic for a headland. The Danish mission and colony founded on it in 1728 was Godthaab, 'good hope', and that is the name every nineteenth-century expedition uses. It is also the fiord John Davis called Gilbert Sound in 1585 after [[person:Adrian Gilbert]], and used as his landfall on all three voyages: three names for one place, of which the descriptive Kalaallisut one is now the only official form.

### place-names-168 | place | 1888 | Nettilling: what Boas knew and the Geographic Board did not | bib=boas1888central pages= | lat=66.4833 lon=-70.3333 | people=["Franz Boas", "James White"] | tags=["inuktitut,error,gazetteer"] | credit=Franz Boas, The Central Eskimo (1888); Geographic Board of Canada (1910) | licence=public domain | file= | url=
The largest lake on Baffin Island. [[source:boas1888central|Boas]] recorded the name in Cumberland Sound in 1883-84 and glossed it correctly: Nettilling, 'with seals' - nattiq, the ringed seal, a landlocked population of which lives in it and is one of the reasons the lake is scientifically interesting. ### place-names-170 | quote | 1888 | Boas on Aggo, Akudnirn and Oqo: the weather side, the centre and the lee | bib=boas1888central pages=The Central Eskimo, 'The Oqomiut' | lat=66.5 lon=-66.0 | people=["Franz Boas"] | tags=["inuktitut,ethnography,orientation"] | credit=Franz Boas, The Central Eskimo (Bureau of Ethnology, 1888); Internet Archive | licence=public domain | file= | url=
'The whole of the land from Prince Regent Inlet to the plateau of Nugumiut is divided by the Eskimo into three parts, Aggo, Akudnirn, and Oqo - i.e., the weather side, the center, and the lee side - and accordingly the tribes are called the Aggomiut, Akudnirmiut, and Oqomiut.' Boas then explains that these are not fixed regions: 'they denote rather directions and the intervals between the localities situated in these directions. In asking for the position of Oqo one would be directed southeast, as this is considered the lee side; in the same way, if asking for Aggo, one would be directed to the shore of Prince Regent Inlet, the farthest land in the northwest, the weather side.' A whole system of geography with no fixed boundary in it, and no European chart could hold it.

Two islands named in the same afternoon: Griffith Island 'after Rear-Admiral Edward Griffith', Lowther Island 'after Viscount Lowther, one of the lords of His Majesty's treasury'. Parry named Somerville Island 'after his friend, Dr. Somerville' and Browne Island 'out of respect to my much esteemed friend, Mr. Henry Browne, of Portland-Place, London'. Parry gave the south shore of Lancaster Sound the name North Somerset 'in honour of my native county' and the north shore 'North Devon, after that of Lieutenant Liddon' - Matthew Liddon, who commanded the Griper. Two English counties, chosen because the two commanders came from them, on either side of the same strait. The 'North' has since been dropped from Devon Island, which is the largest uninhabited island on earth; its Inuktitut name is Tallurutit.

On the sledge journey across Melville Island in June 1820 Parry named the gulf 'after my much-esteemed friend and brother-officer, Lieutenant Liddon' and, in the same paragraph, capes after Lieutenants Beechey and Hoppner, an island after his friend Mr Hooper, and Cape Edwards after the man who first guessed there would be water at its foot. Belcher named the sound at the head of Wellington Channel 'Northumberland Sound, in honour of Her Majesty's Minister' - the Duke of Northumberland, then First Lord of the Admiralty - and decided that 'the names of Sir John and Lady Franklin should occupy the limiting points', so Cape Franklin and Cape Lady Franklin stand at either end. Belcher gave the island in Queens Channel the name Exmouth because he passed it on the anniversary of Lord Exmouth's bombardment of Algiers in 1816. Naval anniversaries are a small but real category of Arctic name: a captain with a calendar and an empty coast.

Belcher named the island North Kent after the English county and the strait beside it after the Earl of Cardigan - a year before Cardigan led the Charge of the Light Brigade and made the name famous for the wrong reason. Cardigan Strait and Hell Gate are the two tidal passages between Jones Sound and Norwegian Bay; both run hard enough to stay open in winter, which is why the North Water polynya reaches this far west.

'To this I gave the name of Port Refuge.' A working name, given for what the place did for the ship. Port Refuge later turned out to hold some of the oldest Palaeo-Inuit house remains in the High Arctic, excavated in the 1970s - so a name meaning shelter to a Victorian captain marks a place that had been shelter for four thousand years.

### place-names-226 | place | 1853 | Emerald Isle, named for lying next to Prince Patrick | bib=white1910placenames pages=p. 361 | lat=76.8008 lon=-114.1695 | people=["Francis Leopold McClintock"] | tags=["mcclintock,joke,western-islands"] | credit=Geographic Board of Canada 1910 (White); CGNDB LAHLV | licence=public domain | file= | url=
McClintock named the small island west of Melville 'Emerald Isle', 'the name applied to Ireland on account of its verdure; so named from its proximity to Prince Patrick I.' Stefansson passed it in 1917 and remarked that it is 'as thickly covered with vegetation as the name given it by McClintock' would suggest - which is to say, hardly at all.

Mecham of HMS Resolute named Eglinton Island after the Lord Lieutenant of Ireland and, in the same season, 'named Mould bay, at the request of Nares - presumably after a friend of Nares'. Nobody knows who Mould was. In 1948 the joint Canadian-American weather station on Prince Patrick Island took the bay's name, and Mould Bay became one of the five High Arctic weather stations; the friend nobody can identify is on every synoptic chart of the western Arctic.

In one season the whaling captain William Penny named an island after the Second Secretary of the Admiralty, a bay after his wife Harriet, lakes after his daughter Laura, a point after his son Alexander and a bay after his son Robert. On the same shores he put Cape Graham Gore, Le Vesconte Point, Little Cape and Cape Read after officers of the missing Franklin expedition, and De Haven Point after the American who had come to help. ### place-names-229 | place | 1850 | Assistance Bay and the Austin squadron's names | bib=osborn1852stray pages=pp. 184, 190-194 | lat=74.67 lon=-94.55 | people=["Horatio Thomas Austin", "Erasmus Ommanney", "Sherard Osborn"] | tags=["austin,franklin-search,ships"] | credit=Osborn 1852 | licence=public domain | file= | url=
Austin's four ships - Resolute, Assistance, Pioneer, Intrepid - left their own names along the shores of Barrow Strait and Cornwallis Island. Parry named the low sandy island after Mr Dealy of the Hecla and the inlet beside it 'from regard to the memory of the late Lord Bridport'. ### place-names-240 | place | 1900 | Axel Heiberg Island | bib=white1910placenames pages=p. 327 | lat=79.75 lon=-91.0 | people=["Otto Sverdrup", "Axel Heiberg"] | tags=["sverdrup,norwegian,patron"] | credit=Geographic Board of Canada 1910 (White); CGNDB OABKA, decision 12 December 1939 | licence=public domain | file= | url=
'After Consul Axel Heiberg, one of the patrons of the Sverdrup expedition, 1898-1902.' Heiberg was a Christiania financier and part-owner of the Ringnes brewery. ### place-names-241 | place | 1901 | Amund Ringnes and Ellef Ringnes islands: two brewers on the map | bib=white1910placenames pages=pp. 324, 361 | lat=78.3333 lon=-96.4167 | people=["Otto Sverdrup", "Amund Ringnes", "Ellef Ringnes"] | tags=["sverdrup,norwegian,patron"] | credit=Geographic Board of Canada 1910 (White); CGNDB OAAVU (decision 31 December 1910) and OAFCD | licence=public domain | file= | url=
Amund and Ellef Ringnes ran the Ringnes brewery in Christiania and paid for four years of Norwegian exploration west of Ellesmere. Sverdrup named an island for each. Ellef Ringnes Island, at 78.5 N, carried the Isachsen weather station from 1948 to 1978 and is where the Canadian polar-continental-shelf work on the Sverdrup Basin began; the oil and gas province beneath these islands is still called the Sverdrup Basin. ### place-names-242 | place | 1901-04 | Eureka Sound, named by Ivar Fosheim on finding the passage | bib=sverdrup1904newland pages=vol. 'He called the sound Heureka Sund, and that is the name it has kept.' Fosheim had the Greek for 'I have found it' ready before anyone else could speak, on the day the party climbed a talus and saw 'a beautiful large sound extending northward as far as the eye could reach' - the water that leads north between Ellesmere and the new land to the west. Sverdrup gives no date for it; the narrative places it a few days after 27 April 1901 and before the Seventeenth of May, so late April or early May 1901. The Norwegian spelling was anglicised; in 1947 the weather station on Slidre Fiord took the name from the sound, and Eureka is now the second most northerly permanently inhabited place in the world.

### place-names-243 | place | 1899 | Norwegian Bay and Hell Gate | bib=white1910placenames pages=pp. 378, 412 | lat=77.5 lon=-90.5 | people=["Otto Sverdrup"] | tags=["sverdrup,norwegian,ice"] | credit=Geographic Board of Canada 1910 (White); CGNDB OALXN, OAHCI | licence=public domain | file= | url=
Sverdrup named the bay 'after his fellow-countrymen' and the narrow tidal passage out of it Hell Gate, because, in his words as the Board recorded them, 'none of us had ever seen waters so absolutely impossible to navigate as this sound'. It is still one of the most dangerous pieces of water in the archipelago: the tide runs hard enough through Hell Gate and Cardigan Strait to keep them from freezing over, and the open water feeds the western end of the North Water.

### place-names-244 | place | 1899 | The plain Norwegian names of Ellesmere: Bjorne, Trold, Stenkul, Hare, Gaasefjord | bib=white1910placenames pages=pp. 334, 368, 376, 440, 445 | lat=77.6167 lon=-87.0 | people=["Otto Sverdrup", "Per Schei", "Ivar Fosheim"] | tags=["sverdrup,norwegian,descriptive"] | credit=Geographic Board of Canada 1910 (White) | licence=public domain | file= | url=
Where the British put patrons on the map, the Norwegians put what they saw. Bjorne Peninsula is 'bear'; Hare Fiord 'after the number of hares seen here'; Moskusfjord musk-ox bay; Gaasefjord goose fiord; Hvalros bay walrus bay; Renbugten reindeer bay; Skaare Fiord gulls; Stenkul Fiord 'signifying coal, after coal found there'; Trold Fiord 'troll', described as 'a horrible hole, with high threatening walls of rock on either side'; Kritberg white mountain, Blaafjeld blue mountain, Tommen the thumb, Ytteroen the outer island. These names are a field notebook. A geologist reading the chart today is told where the coal is.

### place-names-245 | place | 1899 | The Fram's crew written across the west coast | bib=white1910placenames pages=pp. 325, 331, 366, 377, 396, 424, 436, 441, 442 | lat=79.6667 lon=-83.75 | people=["Otto Sverdrup", "Ivar Fosheim", "Per Schei", "Sverre Hassel", "Victor Baumann", "Oluf Raanes", "Johan Svendsen", "Herman Georg Simmons", "Edvard Bay", "Adolf Henrik Lindstrom", "Rudolf Stolz", "Gunerius Ingvald Isachsen", "Colin Archer"] | tags=["sverdrup,norwegian,crew"] | credit=Geographic Board of Canada 1910 (White) | licence=public domain | file= | url=

### place-names-246 | place | 1901 | Cape Maundy Thursday, Whitsunday Fiord, May Point: naming by the church calendar | bib=white1910placenames pages=pp. 404, 451 | lat=79.6 lon=-95.0 | people=["Otto Sverdrup", "Gunerius Ingvald Isachsen", "Sverre Hassel"] | tags=["sverdrup,norwegian,calendar"] | credit=Geographic Board of Canada 1910 (White) | licence=public domain | file= | url=
Sledge parties out for months named what they found by the day they found it. Cape Maundy Thursday on Axel Heiberg Island is a translation of Skjaertorsdagskap, 'so named by Sverdrup as it was discovered on that day'; Whitsunday Fiord was discovered on Whitsunday, 1901; May Point on the first of May; Cape Levvel, 'Norwegian for live-well', commemorates a festival held there; Jammerbugten, the Bay of Woe, records six days the party was stuck in it in 1902. The far western water Isachsen and Hassel reached in 1901 was named for the Crown Prince of Sweden, then still Sverdrup's sovereign's heir. ### place-names-249 | place | 1900 | Svartevoeg, renamed Cape Stallworthy | bib=cgndb2026names pages=CGNDB OAPOO, decision 9 June 1960 | lat=81.3833 lon=-93.5 | people=["Otto Sverdrup", "Per Schei", "Harry Stallworthy", "Frederick Cook"] | tags=["sverdrup,rcmp,renaming"] | credit=Canadian Geographical Names Database, Natural Resources Canada | licence=public domain | file= | url=
The black cliff at the north end of Axel Heiberg was named Svartevoeg, 'black wall', by Sverdrup's party. Frederick Cook made it the starting point of his 1908 polar journey and called it Svartevoeg too. It is now Cape Stallworthy, after the RCMP constable Harry Stallworthy, who patrolled Ellesmere in the 1930s; the Board's decision is dated 9 June 1960. ### place-names-260 | place | 2025 | Kanguq (Goose Fiord): Norwegian, then English, then Inuktitut | bib=iht2025placenames pages=layer entry ᑎ2-3 | lat=76.5932 lon=-88.501 | people=[] | tags=["inuktitut,iht,sverdrup,layers"] | credit=Inuit Heritage Trust Traditional Place Names Program, Grise Fiord and Resolute Bay layer, updated May 2025 | licence=Inuit Heritage Trust | file= | url=
The Inuit Heritage Trust's own note on this fiord is the best short statement of what this topic is about: 'There used to be an abundance of geese here. ### place-names-261 | place | 2025 | Umimmaup Kangiqlua (Muskox Fiord) and the Umimmak names | bib=iht2025placenames pages=layer entries ᑎ3-3, ᑎ3-4, ᑎ3-6 | lat=76.4918 lon=-87.455 | people=[] | tags=["inuktitut,iht,muskox"] | credit=Inuit Heritage Trust Traditional Place Names Program, Grise Fiord and Resolute Bay layer, updated May 2025 | licence=Inuit Heritage Trust | file= | url=
A cluster of names built on one root: Umimmak, muskox, names a peninsula; Umimmaup Kangiqlua is the fiord belonging to it, officially Muskox Fiord; Umimmaup Qinngua is the head of that inlet; Umimmaujaup Kangiqlua, the fiord of the thing-that-looks-like-a-muskox, is officially Baad Fiord; and Umimmaup Ungaliqlia, 'the other one, in relation to Umimmak', is officially Cape Storm. Inuit naming works outward from a landmark by relation - the far one, the near one, the one belonging to it - where the British system works by importing a new patron for each feature.

### place-names-262 | place | 2025 | Mount Aqiatushuk, named for a man who died in the relocation | bib=iht2025placenames pages=layer entry | lat=76.4262 lon=-83.2339 | people=["Patrick Aqiattusuk"] | tags=["inuktitut,iht,relocation"] | credit=Inuit Heritage Trust Traditional Place Names Program, Grise Fiord and Resolute Bay layer, updated May 2025 | licence=Inuit Heritage Trust | file= | url=
'This mountain is named for Patrick Aqiattusuk whose remains are at the base of the mountain.' Aqiattusuk was one of the Inukjuak people put ashore on southern Ellesmere in the 1953 High Arctic relocation; he died there. The mountain above his grave is now official as Mount Aqiatushuk. It is the only feature in this half of the archipelago whose official name commemorates a person who was moved there against his will.

### place-names-263 | place | 2025 | Names that record what happened: Qiavik, Supuuqtuutitalik, Nattiarvik, Pirlirarvirjuaq | bib=iht2025placenames pages=layer entries | lat=76.2967 lon=-84.6481 | people=[] | tags=["inuktitut,iht,events"] | credit=Inuit Heritage Trust Traditional Place Names Program, Grise Fiord and Resolute Bay layer, updated May 2025 | licence=Inuit Heritage Trust | file= | url=
Where a British chart records who paid for the ship, the Inuit names record what happened on the ground. Qiavik, on Cape Waldegrave: 'someone lost her dog here and cried'. Supuuqtuutitalik: 'someone once lost their pipe here'. Nattiarvik: 'named for a young seal caught here when people were hungry'. Utiriarvik: 'place to return to, during fox trapping days'. Pirlirarvirjuaq, a fishing lake: 'also a place where people from Qillarsuaq's travels starved on their return from Greenland' - a reference to the 1860s migration from Baffin Island to Greenland and back. Qimakutalik: 'oil drums left behind by geology and exploration camps'. The most recent layer of Inuit naming records the oil industry as calmly as it records the seal.

### place-names-264 | place | 2025 | Greenlandic names on the Ellesmere coast: Ingeersarfik, Sermitajaaq, Perlerarfissuaq | bib=iht2025placenames pages=layer entries ᐃ8-2, ᐃ8-3, ᐃ9-1, ᐱ6-2, ᐱ7-1 | lat=77.8782 lon=-76.3438 | people=[] | tags=["inughuit,greenlandic,iht,thule-dialect"] | credit=Inuit Heritage Trust Traditional Place Names Program, Grise Fiord and Resolute Bay layer, updated May 2025 | licence=Inuit Heritage Trust | file= | url=
Along the east coast of Ellesmere, facing Greenland, the Trust's layer records names in Greenlandic with their Thule-dialect (Inughuit) forms beside them: Ingeersarfik / Ingeerrar, Sermitajaaq / Hermitajaaq ('this low area is covered by ice'), Aapalukisaarsuaq / Aapalukihaarraaq, Qilalugartooq ('something to do with catching beluga'), Perlerarfissuaq / Perlerarviggiaq. The Inughuit dialect turns Greenlandic s into h. These names are the trace of the Inughuit crossings of Smith Sound - the same road Qitdlarssuaq's people took eastward about 1860 and Peary's drivers took westward after 1898. The Trust notes one case where a Greenlandic and a Canadian Inuktitut name for the same inlet may not agree: 'maybe there was a miscommunication between Greenlanders and Canadian Inuit about the specific place with this name.'

### place-names-301 | place | 1953 | The Queen Elizabeth Islands, 1953 | bib=cgndb2026names pages=CGNDB OAUJD | lat=76.334 lon=-114.5028 | people=[] | tags=["decision,royal,coronation"] | credit=Canadian Geographical Names Database, Natural Resources Canada | licence=public domain | file= | url=

### place-names-321 | place | 1616 | The North Water / Pikialasorsuaq | bib=white1910placenames pages=not in the gazetteer | lat=77.0 lon=-74.0 | people=[] | tags=["polynya,inughuit,whaling,science"] | credit=compiled from the whaling record and Inughuit usage; see topic whaling | licence=public domain | file= | url=
The great polynya at the head of Baffin Bay has an English name that is a sailing direction and an Inuit name that is a description of the thing itself. The whalers called it the North Water: the open water you emerge into after fighting through the ice of Melville Bay. The Inughuit and the Inuit of north Baffin call it Pikialasorsuaq, the great upwelling - which is what it is, and why it is open, and why the eiders, narwhal, walrus and belugas are there. ### place-names-360 | place | 1967-06-07 | Barbeau Peak and Hattersley-Smith's names on northern Ellesmere | bib=cgndb2026names pages=CGNDB OABPE, decision 14 October 1969 | lat=81.9083 lon=-75.025 | people=["Geoffrey Hattersley-Smith", "Marius Barbeau"] | tags=["hattersley-smith,operation-tanquary,science"] | credit=Canadian Geographical Names Database, Natural Resources Canada | licence=public domain | file= | url=
The highest mountain in Nunavut, 2,616 m by the names database and 2,665 m by Hattersley-Smith's own reckoning, was first climbed on 7 June 1967 by a Defence Research Board and RAF party under Hattersley-Smith, who named it after the anthropologist Marius Barbeau. The Board approved the name on 14 October 1969, the year Barbeau died. Hattersley-Smith, working on northern Ellesmere from 1953 through Operation Hazen and Operation Tanquary, was the last individual to name a large number of features in this region; he named after Canadian scientists and scholars rather than after ministers or donors. See [[topic:operation-hazen-1957]].

### place-names-361 | place | 1947-03-13 | The Findlay Group, Hazen Strait and Desbarats Strait: names made from photographs, 1947 | bib=cgndb2026names pages=CGNDB OAFOZ, OAUHR, OAEIH | lat=77.4 lon=-104.7667 | people=[] | tags=["air-survey,decision,western-islands"] | credit=Canadian Geographical Names Database, Natural Resources Canada | licence=public domain | file= | url=
On one day, 13 March 1947, the Board approved Findlay Group, Hazen Strait, Desbarats Strait and Wilkins Strait - features in the western Queen Elizabeth Islands that no ship had seen and that existed on the map because aircraft had photographed them. It is the point at which naming in this region stops being something a captain does from a deck and becomes something a committee does from a photograph, and the names change character with it: no more crews, no more patrons, mostly surveyors and officials. See [[topic:qei-from-the-air-1947]].

### place-names-362 | place | 1908 | Cape Sparbo / Nunakallak, where Cook wintered | bib=iht2025placenames pages=IHT layer entry | lat=75.8157 lon=-83.9669 | people=["Frederick Cook", "Ahwelah", "Etukishook"] | tags=["inuktitut,sverdrup,cook,devon"] | credit=Inuit Heritage Trust Traditional Place Names Program; name origin from the 1910 gazetteer | licence=public domain | file= | url=
Sparbo is a Norwegian parish name, given by Sverdrup's party. The Inuit Heritage Trust records the two headlands here as Nunakallak Kangilliq, 'the more westerly point', and Nunakallak Kitiqliq, 'the more easterly point' - a pair distinguished by position, in the Inuit manner, rather than by two separate honorands. ### place-names-363 | place | 1818 | Cobourg Island / Qurvik and the bird cliffs of Jones Sound | bib=iht2025placenames pages=IHT layer entries | lat=75.9273 lon=-79.3828 | people=["John Ross"] | tags=["inuktitut,seabirds,jones-sound,science"] | credit=Inuit Heritage Trust Traditional Place Names Program; name origin from the 1910 gazetteer | licence=public domain | file= | url=
The island at the mouth of Jones Sound replaced what the 1910 gazetteer calls 'the non-existent Coburg bay, of John Ross, 1818, which he named after Prince Leopold of Saxe-Coburg'. Its Inuit name is Qurvik: 'nesting place for murres and kittiwakes. Smells like a sewage lagoon' - and the Trust adds, honestly, 'it may also be an Inuktitutization of Coburg Island.' Nearby, Cambridge Point is Akpait, 'cliffs where murres nest', and Princess Charlotte Monument is Qaqulluit, 'fulmars nest here'. The three Inuit names identify the three seabird colonies; the three English names identify a German princeling, an English university and a dead princess. A seabird biologist working the Jones Sound colonies is better served by the Inuit chart.
