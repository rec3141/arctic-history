# Meta Incognita: the Elizabethan names of Frobisher Bay and Davis Strait
[page · place-names · draft]

The oldest European names on the chart of the eastern Arctic are Elizabethan, and they are a list of people who put money into three voyages that lost it.

## Frobisher, 1576-1578

[[person:Martin Frobisher]] entered the bay that carries his name in August 1576 believing it a strait to Cathay. The very first name he left was not his own and not a patron's. **Hall Island**, at the entrance, was named, in the words the Geographic Board quotes from the voyage narrative, because "the master of the ship did land upon the first iland, and named it Hall's Iland after his own name" ([[artifact:place-names-064]]). [[person:Christopher Hall]] was master of the *Gabriel*. Two more of the south-shore names are his officers': **Jackman Sound**, "named Jackmans Sounde after the name of the maisters mate, who had first liking unto the place" ([[artifact:place-names-065]]) — [[person:Charles Jackman]], later lost seeking the north-east passage — and **York Sound**, after [[person:Gilbert Yorke]], captain of the *Gabriel* in 1577. **[[place:Luke Sound]]** was named in 1578 "by reason of one Luke Ward that went with him on land". Working seamen got capes before courtiers did.

Then the money arrived. When Frobisher came home in 1576 with a lump of black rock that an assayer said held gold, the second and third voyages became mining ventures with a court behind them, and the names change register. **Mount Warwick** honours [[person:Ambrose Dudley, Earl of Warwick]], principal shareholder of the Cathay Company. The **[[place:Countess of Warwick's Sound]]** and the island in it are for his wife, [[person:Anne Dudley, Countess of Warwick]] — "that right honourable and vertuous lady, Anne, Countess of Warwicke". **Hatton's Headland** is for Sir Christopher Hatton, the Queen's favourite. And **[[place:Loks Land]]**, the island at the northern gate of the bay, is "after Michael Lok, who wrote an account of Frobisher's first voyage and was one of the venturers" ([[artifact:place-names-066]]). [[person:Michael Lok]] was the company's treasurer; he put in his own fortune, the ore turned out to be worthless hornblende, and he went to debtors' prison. The chart is more loyal to him than the accounts were.

Two names from those voyages are of a different kind. On the return of the second voyage the Queen herself named the new land **[[place:Meta Incognita Peninsula]]**, "the unknown limit" — the only Latin name in the eastern Arctic and the oldest European name in Canada's north still in use ([[artifact:place-names-062]]). And on the island in the Countess of Warwick's Sound where the miners dug and built their stone house, the Inuit gave the other name that survived: **[[place:Kodlunarn Island]]**, *Qallunaat*, the white men's island ([[artifact:place-names-063]]). It was Inuit oral tradition, three centuries old, that led [[person:Charles Francis Hall]] to the site in 1861 — see [[topic:frobisher]] and [[topic:inuit-oral-history]].

## Davis, 1585-1587

[[person:John Davis]]'s three voyages were financed out of the West Country and the City, and his names are the subscription list. **[[place:Exeter Sound]]** was named in 1585 "after Exeter, Devon, England, to commemorate the assistance given by merchants of Exeter towards the discovery of the North West passage" ([[artifact:place-names-068]]); **Totnes Road** is another Devon town. **[[place:Mount Raleigh]]** is for Sir Walter Raleigh and **Gilbert Sound** — the fiord at Nuuk, [[place:Nuup Kangerlua]] — for his half-brother [[person:Adrian Gilbert]], both promoters of the voyage. **[[place:Cape Walsingham]]** is [[person:Francis Walsingham]], the Queen's Principal Secretary of State. **[[place:Cape Dyer]]** is, on the Geographic Board's cautious reading, "probably named after Sir Edward Dyer, who was Chancellor of the Order of the Garter ... and who was a great favorite of Queen Elizabeth; or else after Sir James, who was Chief Justice of Common Pleas" — nobody now knows which [[person:Edward Dyer|Dyer]] was meant.

Two of Davis's names carry feeling rather than debt. **[[place:Cape Mercy]]**, at the mouth of Cumberland Sound, he called "Cape of God's Mercy, as being the place of our first entrance for the discovery" ([[artifact:place-names-067]]). **[[place:Killiniq]]** — Cape Chidley, at the southern gate of Hudson Strait — is for "The Worshipful M. John Chidley of Chidley in the countie of Devon, esquire", a Devon friend who died two years later in the Strait of Magellan ([[artifact:place-names-070]]).

The largest of Davis's names is the one his backer paid for. Standing at 72° 12′ N off the Greenland coast in June 1587, his farthest north, he named the great cliff **[[place:Sanderson's Hope]]** after [[person:William Sanderson]], the London merchant adventurer who financed all three voyages ([[artifact:place-names-071]]) — hope of the passage, and hope of a return. And **[[place:Cumberland Sound]]** is George Clifford, [[person:George Clifford, 3rd Earl of Cumberland|3rd Earl of Cumberland]], "a famous mathematician and navigator" ([[artifact:place-names-069]]).

Davis's last piece of naming was an unwitting duplication. Coming into Frobisher's bay in 1587 without knowing whose it was, he called it **Lumley's Inlet** after [[person:John Lumley, 1st Baron Lumley|Lord Lumley]], one of his patrons. The Geographic Board's verdict is four words long: "this name was not adopted".
