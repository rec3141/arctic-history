# Parry's names, 1819-20: an Admiralty payroll written on the ice
[page · place-names · draft]

Between 1 August and 26 September 1819 [[person:William Edward Parry]], aged twenty-nine, sailed HMS *Hecla* and HMS *Griper* from the mouth of [[place:Lancaster Sound]] to a bay on the south coast of [[place:Melville Island]], through water no European had seen. He named roughly a hundred and thirty features on the way. Most of them are still there, and between them they carry the entire senior management of the British state in 1819.

[[source:parry1821journal]] records the naming as it happened, usually in a single clause dropped into the day's run. Read consecutively, it is a remarkable document: a young officer methodically discharging every obligation he has.

## The order he worked in

**The Admiralty first.** [[artifact:place-names-203|Barrow Strait]] "after my friend, Mr. Barrow, Secretary of the Admiralty" — [[person:John Barrow]], the man who had got the expedition sent at all. Croker's Bay after John Wilson Croker, the First Secretary. Cape Hurd after the Hydrographer. Cape Hay after the First Lord's private secretary. Cape Walker "after Mr. Walker, of the Hydrographical Office". [[artifact:place-names-208|Byam Martin Island]] after the Comptroller of the Navy. Cape Cockburn, Moore Bay, Cape Seppings, Port Bowen, Barlow Inlet, Cunningham Inlet, Jackson Harbour, Fitzgerald Bay, Cape Ricketts: lords of the Admiralty, Navy commissioners, post-captains.

**Then the ministry and the royal family.** [[artifact:place-names-205|Bathurst Island]] after the Secretary of State for War and the Colonies. [[artifact:place-names-200|Melville Island]] after [[person:Robert Dundas, 2nd Viscount Melville]], the First Lord. Prince Regent's Inlet, Cape Clarence, Cape York, Prince Leopold's Isles, Cape Yorke after a former First Lord. [[artifact:place-names-207|Wellington Channel]] after the Master-General of the Ordnance, four years after Waterloo.

**Then science.** Davy Island after the President of the Royal Society; [[place:Young Island]] after Thomas Young of the Board of Longitude; Wollaston Islands after the chemist; Cape Kater after the man who had made his pendulum apparatus; Cape Rennell after "the ablest geographer of the age"; Cape Hearne after Samuel Hearne. Sabine Island after [[person:Edward Sabine]], the expedition's own astronomer.

**Then his own people.** [[place:Somerville Island]] after his doctor. [[place:Browne Island]] after a friend in Portland Place. [[place:Garrett Island]] after "my much-esteemed friend Captain Henry Garrett". Maxwell Bay after "my friend, Mr. Maxwell". Benjamin Hobhouse Inlet "after my relation". Cape Riley "after my friend Mr. Richard Riley, of the Admiralty". [[artifact:place-names-206|Beechey Island]] "out of respect to Sir William Beechey" — the portrait painter, father of one of his lieutenants. And when the ships got into their winter quarters and the officers went sledging, the crew went onto the map too: Point Ross, Skene Bay, Beverly Inlet, Cape Palmer, [[place:Dealy Island]], Bushnan Cove, Point Nias, Cape Fisher, Point Reid, Fife Harbour after the Greenland master of the *Griper*, Allison Inlet after the Greenland master of the *Hecla*, Griffiths Point, Halse and Wakeham headlands. See [[artifact:place-names-212]] and [[artifact:place-names-230]].

**And two counties.** He called the south shore of the sound North Somerset "in honour of my native county" and the north shore [[artifact:place-names-211|North Devon]], "after that of Lieutenant Liddon". [[person:Matthew Liddon]] commanded the *Griper*. See [[place:Devon Island]].

## Names that came from below

Twice the crew named something and Parry let it stand.

[[artifact:place-names-202|Cape Bounty]]: Parliament had offered £5,000 to the first ship past 110° W inside the Arctic Circle. They crossed on 4 September 1819. "The bluff head-land, which we had just passed, was subsequently called by the men Bounty Cape." It is on the chart because the seamen were thinking about the money.

Hecla and Griper Bay, and the Hecla and Griper Banks, after the two ships. Ships' names are the most durable class of Arctic name in the whole region: *Alert*, *Discovery*, *Resolute*, *Investigator*, *Assistance*, *Intrepid*, *Pioneer*, *North Star*, *Advance*, *Rescue*, *Fram* and *Gjoa* all became places.

## The name that did not stick

Parry called the whole discovery "New Georgia", then, remembering that the name was taken, changed it to the North Georgian Islands "in honour of our gracious Sovereign, George the Third". Nobody used it. The group was the Parry Islands for a century and a third, and then in 1953 it became the [[place:Queen Elizabeth Islands]] — so the collective name has honoured three monarchs and settled on the third. See [[artifact:place-names-301]].

## Winter Harbour

The one name on Melville Island that is not a person is the one everybody uses. [[artifact:place-names-201|Winter Harbour]] is where the *Hecla* and *Griper* were sawn into the ice on 26 September 1819 and stayed until 1 August 1820, and Parry's Rock above the beach carries the ships' names cut into the sandstone. Every later expedition on this coast called there and left a record: [[person:Francis Leopold McClintock]], [[person:Robert McClure]], [[person:Joseph-Elzear Bernier]] in 1908, and [[person:Henry Larsen]] in the *St Roch*. A descriptive name given for a purely practical reason became the most-visited spot in the western archipelago.

The voyage itself is told in [[topic:ross-parry]].
