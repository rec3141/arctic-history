# The whalers' and the Company's names
[page · place-names · draft]

After the Navy came the fleets, and they named things differently: not for patrons in London but for ships, for captains, for what a place was good for, and — because they had to work with the people who lived there — sometimes in Inuktitut.

## Ships' names

Whaling gave the archipelago a set of names that commemorate hulls. **[[place:Eclipse Sound]]**, the water between Baffin Island and Bylot Island, is "after the whaling vessel *Eclipse*". **Dexterity Harbour** is "after a whaling vessel". **[[place:Ikpiarjuk|Arctic Bay]]** takes its English name from Captain [[person:William Adams]]'s *Arctic*, which surveyed Eclipse Sound and Admiralty Inlet in 1872 — the QTC's community history for Arctic Bay says so directly, and Adams's own name is on Adams Sound off Admiralty Inlet and on **Adams Island** in Navy Board Inlet, which the Inuit call Tuujjuk. **[[place:Milne Inlet]]**, up which the Mary River ore now goes out, was named by [[person:Joseph-Elzear Bernier]] in 1906 "after Capt. W. F. Milne of the whaling steamer *Eclipse*" ([[artifact:place-names-093]]).

Two of the whaling captains got their names onto the central Arctic instead. When [[person:Roald Amundsen]] came through in 1905 he named **Cape Adams** and **Cape Milne** in the Royal Geographical Society group "after Capt. Adams, a Scotch whaler, who deposited stores for him on Dalrymple rock" and after Milne, who had done the same. Whalers were the region's only supply line, and the chart says so.

## Hogarth's Sound, and the chart an Inuk drew

The clearest case of the whalers' naming being both generous and wrong concerns [[place:Cumberland Sound]]. In 1839 the Aberdeen whaling master [[person:William Penny]] brought a young Inuk of the sound, [[person:Eenoolooapik]], home to Scotland. Eenoolooapik drew him a chart of a great inlet, Tenudiakbeek, that Penny could not find on any English map, and in 1840 Penny sailed into it with him aboard. Believing it a new discovery, Penny named it **Hogarth's Sound**, in compliment to [[person:William Hogarth]] of Aberdeen "to whom our expedition was deeply indebted" — Hogarth had been kind to Eenoolooapik through the winter. But it was not new: it was Davis's Cumberland, entered in 1585. Davis's name was restored; Penny's survives only in Hogarth Inlet on Baffin Island and Hogarth Point on Devon Island. Eenoolooapik's Inuktitut name for the sound, the one that had actually got them there, did not survive at all. See [[topic:whaling]] and [[source:mdonald1841narrative|McDonald's narrative of 1841]].

## Places named for what they yielded

Whalers and traders name places the way Inuit do — for use. **[[place:Uumanarjuaq|Blacklead Island]]**, the shore station and later the Anglican mission at the mouth of Cumberland Sound, is "after the graphite found there"; its Inuktitut name Uumanarjuaq is "the big heart-shaped one", for the hill. **[[place:Kekerten]]**, the other Cumberland Sound station, is Boas's *Qeqerten*, "the islands", taken straight from Inuktitut into English spelling. **Marble Island** in Hudson Bay is "from the white marble of which it is formed" — Foxe had tried to call it Brooke Cobham after a knight, and lost. **[[place:Qatiktalik|Cape Fullerton]]** was named by Scroggs in 1722 "after James Fullerton, Governor of the Hudson's Bay Company at fort Albany", and became the wintering harbour for the American whaling fleet and the site of the first police post in the north.

**Kivitoo**, **Cape Haven**, **Durban Harbour** and **Niginganiq** (Isabella Bay) are all stations or anchorages where an English and an Inuktitut name lie on top of each other on the same rock; [[source:low1906report|A. P. Low]] listed the working stations from the deck of the *Neptune* in 1903-04, and [[source:boas1888central|Boas]] had listed the Inuit names twenty years earlier.

## The Company's names, and the police

The Hudson's Bay Company named the western Arctic more than the eastern. The Geographic Board noticed it in 1910 and said so: the coasts explored by Rae, Dease and Simpson "are a veritable directory of their contemporary chief traders, chief factors, etc., of the Hudson's Bay Company" ([[artifact:place-names-060]]). **[[place:Kugaaruk|Pelly Bay]]** is Sir John Pelly, Governor of the Company 1822-52; **Ballenden Lake** is a Company officer; **[[place:Chesterfield Inlet|Chesterfield]]** and **Wager** come from the eighteenth-century Company and Admiralty. On Baffin the Company arrived late — Lake Harbour 1911, Pangnirtung 1921, Pond Inlet 1921, Arctic Bay 1926 — and mostly took the names it found.

The QTC community histories say plainly what happened when the police arrived: at Pangnirtung, they record, the RCMP settled on that spelling of Panniqtuuq in 1923. A name written down by an outsider tends to stay written that way. See [[topic:posts-rcmp-missions]] and [[topic:relocations-qtc]].
