# The Franklin search's names, 1850-54: the densest layer, and the least explained
[page · place-names · draft]

Between 1850 and 1854 more ships and more men worked in this region than at any time before or since. Austin's four in 1850–51, Penny's two, De Haven's two Americans, John Ross's *Felix*, and then Belcher's five in 1852–54 — and the sledge parties from all of them fanning out over Cornwallis, Bathurst, Devon, Melville, Prince Patrick and the Ringnes approaches for weeks at a time. They found no trace of Franklin north of [[place:Beechey Island]]. What they left was the map.

They also left a naming problem that has never been solved. [[source:white1910placenames]], compiling derivations sixty years later, wrote:

> Unfortunately, the reports of the two most important expeditions sent out during this period — the Austin and the Belcher — do not give much information respecting the many names given by them. Apparently, Austin and Ommanney named all the features discovered by their officers as the reports of their subordinates and the sketch-maps accompanying them are devoid of new names. Belcher seems to have given his officers a fairly free hand in this respect, but only one, Lieut. Mecham, gives anything like a full statement of the derivations of the names for which he is responsible. [[person:George Henry Richards]], who gave more names than any other officer in the expedition, gives absolutely no information.

So several hundred names in the middle of this archipelago — capes, points, bays on Bathurst and Melville and Prince Patrick — cannot now be explained. Where White could not find a statement he guessed, and marked his guess "probable", and the honest reader keeps the hedge.

## Belcher: royal palaces and a free hand

[[person:Edward Belcher]] was disliked by nearly everyone who served under him and abandoned four of his five ships, but [[source:belcher1855last]] does record his naming, and the pattern is unmistakable. He worked from the top of society down.

Royal palaces: [[artifact:place-names-222|Buckingham Island and Windsor Mountain]], and the group around them the Victoria Archipelago. Ministers: [[artifact:place-names-220|Northumberland Sound]] "in honour of Her Majesty's Minister", Cape Disraeli after the Chancellor of the Exchequer, Mount Parker after the senior naval Lord. Royalty: [[artifact:place-names-224|Prince Patrick Island]], named on the third birthday of Queen Victoria's third son; Prince Albert's Island; Prince Edward's Cape; Arthur's Strait. English counties: [[artifact:place-names-223|North Kent]], North Yorkshire. Friends: Mount Pym "after my esteemed friend Admiral Sir Samuel Pym", Mount Acland, Cape Ekins "in compliment to my old captain".

And, because he was Belcher, the two Franklins as bookends: "the names of Sir John and Lady Franklin should occupy the limiting points" of the sound he had just failed to find them in.

He also named things for what they were, which is the part a scientist can use: Pitch Mount, "which derived its name from a very strong naphtha and asphalt" effluvium; Fossil Station, from the fossil limestone; Grave Mount; [[artifact:place-names-225|Port Refuge]]; Pemmican Station; Point Repose. His men supplied Desolation Island and Hungry Island — "a name which I do not think even Penny will dispute."

## Penny: naming upward

[[person:William Penny]] was a whaling master among naval officers, and his 1851 names read like a man who knows exactly whose good opinion he needs. In one season he put the Second Secretary of the Admiralty, the Secretary's wife, the Secretary's daughter and two of the Secretary's sons on the shores of Wellington Channel — [[artifact:place-names-228|Baillie-Hamilton Island]], Lady Hamilton Bay, Laura Lakes, Point Alexander, Robert Bay — and Lord Haddo and Deans Dundas nearby. See [[place:Baillie-Hamilton Island]] and [[place:Dundas Island]].

But Penny also did something none of the naval officers did: he named features after the missing men. Cape Graham Gore "after the first lieutenant of the *Erebus*, one of the ships of the missing expedition"; Le Vesconte Point after the second lieutenant of the *Erebus*; Little Cape after Lieutenant Edward Little of the *Terror*; Cape Read after the ice-master of the *Erebus*; Fairholme Harbour after another *Erebus* lieutenant. On the shores where he was searching for them, Penny wrote the names of the men he was looking for. See [[topic:franklin-1845]].

He gave De Haven Point to the American commander "out of compliment to the Commander of the American expedition, who so nobly came out in search of our lost countrymen", and Cape Grinnell to [[person:Henry Grinnell]], the New York merchant who paid for it.

## Austin's squadron, and the jokes

[[source:osborn1852stray]] is the only search narrative that records what naming actually felt like on the lower deck. Osborn notes a rock the seamen "immediately christened 'Prince Albert's Hat,' from its resemblance to a marine's shako"; a bay "facetiously christened by the seamen" *Oh! no we never mention it*, and a nearby headland *Cape No Name*, because newly discovered places were forbidden to be named without authority. He records Austin naming the Trenter Mountains "in compliment to the family of Sir John Barrow" — Trenter was the maiden name of the Dowager Lady Barrow. See [[artifact:place-names-229]].

The four ships wrote themselves across Barrow Strait: [[place:Assistance Bay]], Resolute names, Pioneer Island, Intrepid Inlet, [[artifact:place-names-209|Griffith Island]] where they wintered.

## Kellett, McClintock and Mecham in the west

The most productive sledging of the whole search was Kellett's, out of [[artifact:place-names-230|Dealy Island]] on Melville Island in 1852–53. His lieutenants covered the western islands and named them.

[[artifact:place-names-226|Emerald Isle]] is McClintock's joke: Ireland's nickname set beside Ireland's patron saint on [[place:Prince Patrick Island]]. [[artifact:place-names-227|Eglinton Island]] is Mecham's, after the Lord Lieutenant of Ireland — same joke, extended. Mecham also named [[place:Mould Bay]] "at the request of Nares", after a friend of Nares whom nobody has since identified, and Petoural Point after one of his own sledge crew — a name the Admiralty or a draughtsman turned into Pedder Point, which is what the chart still says. A seaman lost his name to a copying error. See [[person:Petoural]].

[[source:mdougall1857eventful]], from the *Resolute*, records the western names as they came in: the Giant's Causeway "from the peculiar appearance of the ice surrounding it"; Comfort Cove, where driftwood for fuel was found — "such was deemed a luxury, as the name of Comfort Cove was assigned to the spot"; Land's End, "the western extremity, appropriately named".

## Why it matters now

The Franklin search laid down the densest layer of names in the archipelago, and it is the layer we understand least. When a scientist on the bridge asks who Cape Fisher or Point Wilkie or Arnott Bay was named for, the honest answer is often that nobody wrote it down, and that the best available guess is a line in a 1910 government report prefaced by the word "probable".

The search itself is told in [[topic:franklin-search]] and [[topic:mcclure-investigator]].
