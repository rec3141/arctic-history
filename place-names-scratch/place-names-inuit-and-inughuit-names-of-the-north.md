# The Inuit and Inughuit names of the north
[page · place-names · draft]

The Geographic Board of Canada's 1910 gazetteer of northern place names, [[source:white1910placenames]], stated the problem in one sentence: the most striking feature of the whole survey is "the extreme paucity of native names, due partly to the uninhabited nature of the greater part of the region and, in a minor degree, to the lack of communication with the Eskimos owing to the failure to provide the expeditions with interpreters."

The second reason is the real one. Parry carried no interpreter in 1819. Belcher's officers, who named several hundred features between Devon Island and Prince Patrick Island in 1852–54, met almost nobody. The Inughuit hunters who made Peary's and Cook's and MacMillan's journeys possible were on the ice for months and their knowledge of the ground went onto the charts under American names.

There are two distinct Indigenous naming traditions in this half of the archipelago, and they belong to different centuries.

## The Inughuit names of the Greenland shore

Avanersuaq — the great north, the Thule District — has been continuously inhabited, and its names are old and dense. [[place:Etah]] is Iita. [[place:Siorapaluk]] is the little sandy place, and is the northernmost settlement in the world with a permanent civilian population. [[place:Nerky]] is Neqi, meat. Anoritooq is the windy place. [[place:Herbert Island]] is Qeqertarsuaq, the big island. [[place:Melville Bay]] is [[place:Qimusseriarsuaq]], the great dog-driving place — the frozen bay that was the winter road south. [[place:Savissivik]] is the place where one gets knife-metal, the settlement beside the Cape York meteorites, from which Inughuit had cut iron for harpoon tips for centuries before Peary took the three largest stones to New York in 1897. See [[artifact:place-names-323]] and [[topic:greenland-inughuit]].

[[place:Uummannaq (Thule)]] is the heart-shaped mountain. In 1910 Rasmussen and Freuchen built a trading post beneath it and called it Thule; the district took the Greek name; in 1953 the people were moved 130 km north to [[place:Qaanaaq]] to make room for the American air base. The base sits on [[place:Pituffik]], the place where the dogs are tied, and in 2023 was officially renamed Pituffik Space Base. An Inughuit name was displaced for seventy years by a classical one, and came back to a place its owners cannot live in. See [[artifact:place-names-324]].

The Trust's records on the Ellesmere side pick up the crossing. Along the east coast, facing Greenland, the Inuit Heritage Trust's layer carries names in Greenlandic with their Thule-dialect forms beside them — Ingeersarfik / Ingeerrar, Sermitajaaq / Hermitajaaq, Perlerarfissuaq / Perlerarviggiaq — the s becoming h in Inughuit speech. These are the trace of the Smith Sound crossings: [[person:Qitdlarssuaq]]'s migration eastward about 1860, and the westward hunting journeys after 1898. See [[artifact:place-names-264]].

## The Inuit names of southern Ellesmere and Cornwallis

These are not ancient names, and it matters to say so. The Queen Elizabeth Islands had no permanent Inuit population when the Franklin searchers arrived, and none when [[source:dunbar1956arctic]] was written: "No Eskimos lived in Cornwallis Island from the time of its discovery until 1953, though there is evidence of earlier occupation." In 1953 the federal government moved families from Inukjuak in Nunavik and from Mittimatalik to [[place:Qausuittuq]] and to Craig Harbour, from which they founded [[place:Ausuittuq]]. See [[topic:relocations-qtc]].

The names those families and their children gave are now the best-documented Inuit toponymy in the northern archipelago. The Inuit Heritage Trust's Traditional Place Names Program has recorded, reviewed and verified 346 of them for the Ausuittuq and Qausuittuq areas since 2008 ([[source:iht2025placenames]]). They are working names. They tell you what a place is for and what happened there:

- **Kanguq**, Goose Fiord: "there used to be an abundance of geese here." The Trust's own note adds that the name "was originally (1900) given in Norwegian (by Sverdrup), and finally, Inuktitut, demonstrating the variety of cultures present here over time." See [[artifact:place-names-260]].
- **Umimmaup Kangiqlua**, Muskox Fiord, and a whole family of names built off the root *umimmak*: the fiord belonging to it, the head of that inlet, "the other one, in relation to Umimmak." See [[artifact:place-names-261]].
- **Qiavik**, on Cape Waldegrave: "someone lost her dog here and cried."
- **Nattiarvik**: "named for a young seal caught here when people were hungry."
- **Utiriarvik**: "place to return to, during fox trapping days."
- **Pirlirarvirjuaq**, a fishing lake: "also a place where people from Qillarsuaq's travels starved on their return from Greenland."
- **Qimakutalik**: "oil drums left behind by geology and exploration camps."
- **Naujaat**: a gull cliff. **Akpait**: "cliffs where murres nest." **Qaqulluit**: "fulmars nest here." These three are the seabird colonies that the ship's own science programme works on.
- **Qurvik**, [[place:Cobourg Island]]: "nesting place for murres and kittiwakes. Smells like a sewage lagoon." The Trust adds, honestly, "it may also be an Inuktitutization of Coburg Island."
- **Mount Aqiatushuk**: "this mountain is named for [[person:Patrick Aqiattusuk]] whose remains are at the base of the mountain" — one of the Inukjuak people landed on Ellesmere in 1953, who died there. See [[artifact:place-names-262]].

Two structural differences from the English layer are worth a navigator's attention. First, Inuit names work by relation to a landmark rather than by importing a new person for each feature: Umimmak the peninsula, then the fiord of Umimmak, then the head of that fiord, then the other one. Second, generics do real work: *qinngua* is the head of an inlet, *kangiqlua* the inlet belonging to something, *itilliq* a valley that is a route, *sirmik* a glacier, *nuvuk* a point, *aqquti* a way through. A reader who knows six generics can read half the layer.

## The two big waters

[[place:The North Water]] is a sailing direction in English — the open water you come out into after Melville Bay. In Inuktitut it is Pikialasorsuaq, the great upwelling, which is the physical explanation of why it is there. The Pikialasorsuaq Commission recommended in 2017 that it be managed as an Inuit-governed area under that name. See [[artifact:place-names-321]].

[[place:Lancaster Sound]] is a London merchant. Tallurutiup Imanga is "the waters of Tallurutit" — Tallurutit being [[place:Devon Island]], from *talluruti*, the vertical lines tattooed on a woman's chin, which is what the pale streaks in its dark south-coast cliffs look like. In 2019 Canada made the 108,000 sq km National Marine Conservation Area and used the Inuit name. See [[artifact:place-names-326]] and [[page:place-names-what-nunavut-has-renamed]].

## What is still missing

Nothing comparable to the IHT layer exists for the northern Queen Elizabeth Islands — Ellef Ringnes, Meighen, Borden, Mackenzie King, Prince Patrick, Melville — because nobody has lived on them. The Inuit Heritage Trust's own page notes that Nunavut is behind Greenland and Nunavik in getting recorded names onto official maps, and that many of the names it holds are not yet official. On a Canadian Hydrographic Service chart of this region today you will find perhaps a dozen Inuit names among several thousand.
