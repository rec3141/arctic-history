# Hall among the Inuit: Tookoolito, Ebierbing and the Frobisher tradition
[page · kane-hall-polaris · draft]

[[person:Charles Francis Hall]] was a Cincinnati engraver and small-time newspaper printer with no money, no ship, no crew and no Arctic experience. What he had was a conviction that survivors of [[topic:franklin-1845|Franklin's expedition]] might still be living among the Inuit, and that the way to find out was to go and live among the Inuit himself. He is the only nineteenth-century Arctic traveller of any nation who did that as a method rather than as an emergency.

"It was on Tuesday, May 29th, 1860, that I departed from" New London, a passenger on the whaling barque *George Henry*, thirty-one souls aboard, with one interpreter, [[person:Kudlago]]. Kudlago died at sea on Sunday morning, 1 July, "in lat. 63 deg N. when near the coast of Greenland, and about 300 miles from his native place", asking *Teik-ko se-ko? teik-ko se-ko?* — "Do you see ice? do you see ice?" Hall had lost his interpreter before the voyage had properly begun. "At noon, August 8th, 1860, we reached our anchorage" in Cornelius Grinnell Bay.

## Two accidents that made him

The first was the gale of 27 September 1860 in [[place:Field Bay]]:

> "On the 27th day of September (1860) there broke upon us that fearful gale which caused the loss of my expedition boat and the far-famed *Rescue*, drove the *Georgiana* on shore, and came near proving the destruction of the *George Henry* and all on board."

Of his own boat there was "nothing remaining but the stern-post fast to a three-inch cable". The plan of sailing to King William Island was finished. What was left was to travel as Inuit travelled — by sledge, on foot, in igloos, eating what was hunted — and that is what made him.

The second was 2 November 1860.

> "While intently occupied in my cabin, writing, I heard a soft, sweet voice say, Good morning, sir. The tone in which it was spoken — musical, lively, and varied — instantly told me that a lady of refinement was there greeting me. I was astonished. Could I be dreaming?" ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-022]])

## Tookoolito and Ebierbing

[[person:Tookoolito]] — Taqulittuq, called Hannah by the whalers ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-117]]) — was a young Inuk woman from Qikiqtaaluk (Baffin Island), sister of Toto and of [[person:Eenoolooapik]], the traveller who had gone to Aberdeen in 1839. She and her husband [[person:Ebierbing]] (Ipiirvik, "Joe", [[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-115]]) "had been taken to England in 1853, and presented to Her Majesty Queen Victoria", and "had remained nearly two years in Great Britain, and were everywhere well received". She spoke, read and wrote English. She poured tea from a tin caddy in her own tupik and kept the manners of an English drawing room in a sealskin tent, and she was entirely and unsentimentally Inuk. A tintype of the two of them is [[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-043]].

She also assessed the qallunaat, which almost nobody in this literature was in a position to do:

> "I feel very sorry to say that many of the whaling people are very bad, making the Innuits bad too; they swear very much, and make our people swear. I wish they would not do so. Americans swear a great deal — more and worse than the English. I wish no one would swear." ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-023]])

From that November until Hall's death eleven years later, Tookoolito and Ebierbing worked with him on every expedition. She interpreted; he hunted ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-075]]), drove, guided and built. Neither is a servant in the record and neither is a curiosity: they are the two people without whom nothing Hall achieved would have happened.

The apprenticeship was literal. On Thursday 10 January 1861 Hall left the ship by sledge with Ebierbing, Tookoolito and Koodloo at thirty below and did not sleep aboard again for six weeks: "My first night (February 21st, 1861) on board the *George Henry*, after forty-three days away in an igloo." Three days before he left, on 7 January, he had sat all night beside a dying woman, Nukertou, whom custom required be left alone — "At last I could count nineteen between her inspirations... At length Nukertou ceased to live." Tookoolito made him withdraw before the death itself: she told him "that if we delayed till Nukertou's death, the skin dresses we had on would never do to be put on again". Nukertou died on 8 January. He recorded the custom and obeyed it, which is more than most of his contemporaries did.

## Nine generations: the Frobisher tradition

Hall never found a Franklin survivor. What he found instead, in Field Bay in the spring of 1861, was one of the best-attested pieces of long-transmitted oral history anywhere in the Arctic record.

On 11 May 1861, through Tookoolito, an elder named [[person:Ookijoxy Ninoo]] — Hall thought her about a hundred years old — described ships that had come long ago: first two, then three, then many. She described bricks, coal and timber left on an island called Niountelik. She described five white men taken by her people, who wintered ashore, built a boat with a mast and sails, and went away in it.

[[person:Martin Frobisher]] came into this bay in 1576 with two vessels, in 1577 with three, and in 1578 with fifteen. On the first voyage he lost five men. See [[topic:frobisher]] and the portrait engraved for Hall's own narrative, [[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-056]].

> "Nine generations had passed away since the visit of Frobisher, yet now, on the 11th of May, 1861, I received from an old woman, probably a hundred years old, statements which I could not otherwise than believe to be facts concerning him and his co-adventurers!" ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-024]])

Hall wrote **nine** generations, not five; the smaller number belongs to later retellings, not to him.

He then found the physical corroboration. Setting out at ten at night on 27 May and sorting through Inuit oddments at Twerpukjua the next day, "my eye accidentally caught sight of a piece of brick". Its owners said their mother had given it to them long ago and pointed to Niountelik; Ebierbing took it from his hand, looked at it, and said, "That is the same as I have seen on that island." Hall: "This relic, then, was more precious to me than the gold which Frobisher sought there under the direct patronage of Queen Elizabeth."

In September 1861 he landed on [[place:Kodlunarn Island]] — *qallunaat*, the white man's island, as the Inuit had called it from time immemorial — and found stone house remains, coal, flint-stone, fragments of tile, glass and pottery, everything "covered with the moss of ages", a trench some eighty-eight feet long, and the slip on the shore where a boat had been built. That landing is in volume II of [[source:hall1864life|Life with the Esquimaux]]; a later plate of the scene is [[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-119]].

He also settled a three-hundred-year-old cartographic error. Frobisher had taken this inlet for a strait leading through to Asia. Working from Inuit knowledge and from a chart drawn for him by [[person:Koojesse]] on Friday 24 August 1860, Hall showed that [[place:Frobisher Bay]] is a bay ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-019]]).

## Coming home, and what it cost

Hall came into New London on 13 September 1862 with three cases and a cask of Frobisher relics and a twenty-pound piece of iron. Tookoolito and Ebierbing came with him, and stayed. Their first child, Tukeliketa — "the Butterfly", born about August 1861 — was exhibited with his parents on Hall's American lecture tour and died of pneumonia in New York on 28 February 1863, aged eighteen months.

He was the first of the three children they buried. Their second, called Little King William, died on the trail on 13 May 1866 during the second expedition. Their adopted daughter [[person:Panik]] — born at Iglulik in July 1866, and six years old when she was carried across Baffin Bay on an ice floe — died at [[place:Groton]] on 18 March 1875, under a stone cut *Sylvia Grinnell Ebierbing (Punna)* ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-116]]). Tookoolito died there of consumption on 31 December 1876, "at the early age of 38" — "Among her last words was the petition, Come, Lord Jesus, and take thy poor creature home!" ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-020]]).

Ebierbing outlived them all. Before going north again with Schwatka in 1878 he was seen at the burying ground weeding the grass from his wife's grave:

> "Hannah gone! Punna gone! Me go now again to King William's Land; if have to fight, me no care." ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-027]])

He went back to the Arctic and is thought to have died there about 1881.
