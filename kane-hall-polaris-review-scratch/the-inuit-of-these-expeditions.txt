# The Inuit who made these expeditions possible
[page · kane-hall-polaris · draft]

Four American expeditions in twenty years went up Smith Sound with white commanders whose names are on the maps. None of them would have come home without Inuit hunters, drivers, seamstresses and interpreters. This page collects what the record actually says about those people, and what it says about how they were treated.

## Hans Hendrik of Fiskernaesset

[[person:Hans Hendrik]] — Suersaq — was shipped by [[person:Elisha Kent Kane]] at Fiskernaesset in south Greenland in July 1853, aged about eighteen, as hunter and dog driver. He then served [[person:Isaac Israel Hayes]] (1860-61), [[person:Charles Francis Hall]] on the *Polaris* (1871-73) and George Nares (1875-76): twenty-three years under four commanders, more Arctic service than any of them. He is on almost every page of this topic, and he is the only person in it who wrote a book of his own.

*[[source:hendrik1878memoirs|Memoirs of Hans Hendrik]] (1878)* was written in Greenlandic and translated by Henry Rink. It is short, plain and completely unlike the commanders' narratives, and on three occasions it directly contradicts them.

**1855, Rensselaer Harbour.** Kane's book records that Hans left the party in the spring of 1855 and lets the reader draw the obvious conclusion. Hans Hendrik's reason is in his own book: he did not think the retreat would work, and the Inughuit did not either. "Eventually my companions began to think of abandoning their vessel and repairing to Upernivik. I did not believe they would be able to reach it... they began persuading me to remain. My companions would never reach Upernivik, they said, and they would take me along with them when they removed." And, of Kane: "A pity it was that our Master behaved with haughtiness towards his crew. Also, once he treated me in a similar way." ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-028]])

**1860, Cape York.** Hayes found him five years later living in a sealskin tent above the sea, watching for a ship. Hayes's version is an act of rescue. Hans Hendrik's version is a negotiation, and the point of it is his family: "They said: Better let her stay behind, next winter thou canst go to fetch her. I replied: I don't like to leave her, I pity her and her baby." He brought his wife [[person:Merkut]] and their daughter [[person:Pingasuk]] aboard. ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-029]])

**1860-61, Sorfalik.** [[person:August Sonntag]] went through the ice on a midwinter journey to buy dogs and died in a snow hut with Hans Hendrik beside him. Hayes cross-questioned him for an hour and printed his doubts — that Sonntag would not have travelled wet, that the dry clothes and the sleeping bag went unused, that no message came ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-016]]). He explicitly declined to allege desertion; he did not withdraw the suspicion.

Hans Hendrik's account is a rescue that failed: he could not reach Sonntag from the ice edge and threw him a sledge strap; he hauled him out; Sonntag stripped and got into the sleeping bag; he could not light a fire in the hut; "Our road being very rough, I cried from despair for want of help." Sonntag's breathing came at longer and longer intervals through the night. ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-030]]) And on what happened when he got back to the ship: "they conceived suspicions, thinking our deaths might be owing to some crime committed by the natives, although, notwithstanding their being unbaptized, they abhor manslaughter." Of Sonntag himself: "that friend of mine who had loved me so kindly." ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-031]])

Neither account can be checked against the other. Both belong in the record, and the reason to say so plainly is that only one of them was believed for a hundred years.

**1872-73, the floe.** Hans Hendrik was on the ice with Merkut and four children — [[person:Augustina Hendrik]], [[person:Tobias Hendrik]] (seven), [[person:Sophie Elisabeth Hendrik]] (four) and [[person:Charlie Polaris]], two months old and born aboard the ship. His description of the move to the big floe is the plainest sentence in this whole literature: "my wife carrying the baby in her hood... these poor things walked over the rough ice, my wife and daughter pulling the sledge, and I assisting those who dragged the boat — a sad sight." ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-033]]) All six of them lived. See [[page:the-polaris-and-the-floe-drift]].

## Tookoolito and Ebierbing

[[person:Tookoolito]] (Taqulittuq, "Hannah") and [[person:Ebierbing]] (Ipiirvik, "Joe") were from Qikiqtaaluk (Baffin Island). They had spent two years in England from 1853 and she spoke, read and wrote English. From 2 November 1860 to Hall's death in November 1871 they worked on all three of his expeditions.

What Tookoolito did was not translation in the ordinary sense. On 6 December 1864, in a snow house on [[place:Roes Welcome Sound]], she stopped Hall from speaking so that he could hear four men talking among themselves: "She then said, They are saying something that I will like much to hear." ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-025]]) What he then heard was the Inuit testimony on Crozier and the Franklin retreat — the most valuable thing Hall ever obtained, and he obtained it because she knew when to keep him quiet. She also decided what he was not to see: at Nukertou's death in January 1861 she made him leave before the end, so his presence would not spoil the skin clothing.

She judged the whalers too, in a passage almost unique in this literature for reversing the direction of the gaze ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-023]]).

Ebierbing hunted, drove, guided, built and — on the floe — kept nineteen people alive. They buried three children: Tukeliketa, eighteen months old, at [[place:Groton]], Connecticut in February 1863; the child called Little King William on the trail near Colvile Bay in May 1866; and their adopted daughter [[person:Panik]], who had survived six months on the ice as a small child, at Groton in March 1875. Tookoolito died there on 31 December 1876, aged thirty-eight ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-020]]). Ebierbing went back north with Schwatka in 1878 after weeding the grass from her grave ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-027]]), and is thought to have died in the Arctic about 1881.

## The Inughuit of Etah

Kane's whole company survived on walrus and seal traded from or given by the people of the Etah district, and the nine men who walked away from the *Advance* in August 1854 were fed all winter and then carried back to the ship on Inughuit sledges in December ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-006]]). [[person:Metek]] hauled Kane's sick south on his own sledge at the start of the boat retreat in 1855. [[person:Kalutunah]], [[person:Ootuniah]], [[person:Myouk]], [[person:Tattarat]], [[person:Amalatok]], [[person:Nesark]], [[person:Tcheitchenguak]] and [[person:Kablunet]] are all named in the record for work without which nobody would have come home.

They were not repaid in kind. Kane had to arbitrate his own men's theft of clothing and fox skins from Kalutunah's hut. And in 1861 Kalutunah said goodbye to Hayes with a sentence that should be read as a demographic statement, not a sentiment:

> "'Alas!' said he, 'we will soon be all gone.' I told him that I would come back, and that white men would live for many years near [[place:Etah]]. 'Come back soon,' said he, 'or there will be none here to welcome you!'" ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-015]])

Hayes counted the whole group at about a hundred people, "a very considerable diminution since Dr. Kane left them, in 1855". Hayes never returned. The descendants of these families live at Qaanaaq today.

## How the record is written, and what to do about it

Three habits of the published narratives are worth naming, because they shape what a reader takes from them.

**Names disappear.** Hayes never once writes "Hans Hendrik" or "Suersaq" in *[[source:hayes1867open|The Open Polar Sea]]*: the man is "Hans" for four hundred pages. In Rink's list of the nineteen on the floe, Ebierbing and Tookoolito appear as "Joe, Eskimo from the West Coast, with his wife, Hanna". Where a first name and a nickname is all the record gives, this database enters the Inuit name and carries the nickname in the alternative names.

**Credit is passive.** Expeditions "obtained" meat and "were assisted". The active verbs belong to hunters who are usually unnamed.

**Suspicion is durable.** Rink, editing Hans Hendrik in 1878, saw exactly this and asked the question nobody had answered: whether the two Inuit men were the only ones on the floe who caught seals, "and, if not, how many were killed by the other ten" ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-034]]). It is still the right question to put to any of these narratives.
