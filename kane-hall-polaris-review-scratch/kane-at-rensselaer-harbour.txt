# Kane at Rensselaer Harbour, 1853-1855
[page · kane-hall-polaris · draft]

[[person:Elisha Kent Kane]] was a naval surgeon of thirty-three, small, consumptive and rheumatic, when he took command of the Second Grinnell Expedition, paid for by the New York merchant [[person:Henry Grinnell]] ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-234]]). The plan was to sail up Smith Sound, find a way to a supposed open sea at the top of the world, and look for [[topic:franklin-1845|Franklin]] there. The brig *Advance* was "a hermaphrodite brig of one hundred and forty-four tons, intended originally for carrying heavy castings from an iron-foundry, but strengthened afterward with great skill and at large expense". She carried Kane and the seventeen men of his party. "We left New York on the 30th of May, 1853."

The expedition's own chart of what it covered is [[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-235]]; the outward voyage is the track [[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-001]].

## Going in

At [[place:Qeqertarsuatsiaat]] (Fiskernaesset) in south Greenland in July he shipped a Kalaallit hunter, [[person:Hans Hendrik]] Suersaq — "one Hans Cristian, a boy of nineteen, as an expert with the kayak and javelin", Kane wrote, after Hans had "given me a touch of his quality by spearing a bird on the wing". It was the start of twenty-three years of Arctic service under four commanders, and it is the reason the only Inuit-authored book in this topic exists ([[source:hendrik1878memoirs]]). The Danish interpreter and dog driver [[person:Carl Petersen]], who had lived years at Upernavik, joined in Greenland too; five years later he would interpret the Inuit testimony that settled the fate of Franklin's men. Kane's plate of the settlement as it looked from the Governor's house is [[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-227]].

On 7 August the brig passed [[place:Cape Alexander]] and entered Smith Sound — "August 7, Sunday. We have left Cape Alexander... We are fairly inside of Smith's Sound." No ship had been so far up the channel. Kane pushed north into worsening ice ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-236]]), was driven back, and in early September warped the *Advance* in among rocky islets on the Greenland shore at about 78 deg 37 min N, 70 deg 40 min W — [[place:Rensselaer Harbour]]. He wrote afterwards, knowing what came next:

> "We found seven-fathom soundings and a perfect shelter from the outside ice; and thus laid our little brig in the harbor, which we were fated never to leave together, — a long resting-place to her indeed, for the same ice is around her still." ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-003]])

He set up an observatory ashore and left permanent marks so a later traveller could recover his instrument stations: lead poured into a crevice in the gneiss with a copper bolt at its centre, a chiselled arrow twenty-nine feet above the mean tide of that winter, a glass jar of documents five feet due west of it, and a cairn ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-007]]). The brig frozen in among the floes is [[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-231]].

## Two winters

Kane knew before the ice closed what the winter would be. On 10 September 1853 he wrote: "we shall lose the sun. Astronomically, he should disappear on the 24th of October if our horizon were free; but it is obstructed by a mountain ridge, and, making all allowance for refraction, we cannot count on seeing him after the 10th." The dogs died of a distemper that winter; the sledge parties failed and had to be rescued ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-238]]); men came back frostbitten.

On 24 June 1854 [[person:William Morton]] and Hans Hendrik, sledging north along the Greenland coast, ran out of ice at [[place:Cape Constitution]]. Morton stood "from a height of four hundred and eighty feet, which commanded a horizon of almost forty miles", and looked out on water breaking in surf.

> "Not a speck of ice, to use his own words, could be seen." ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-004]])

Kane published this as the finding of an open polar sea and it shaped Arctic exploration for a generation. What the two men had actually reached was the edge of the recurring polynya of Kennedy Channel — real, remarkable, and not an ice-free ocean.

Hans Hendrik was the other man on that shore, and twenty-four years later he described the same day without a trace of the rapture: "Again we went on, and fell in with a large open water. We followed the shore-ice, but when this ceased we were obliged to stop... The next afternoon we ascended the hill to build up a beacon, showing the point we had reached, and we marked the rock." ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-050]]) Open water, the end of the ice-foot, a beacon, and a bear shot on the way.

The brig did not get out in 1854. On 24 August Kane called all hands and told them he meant to stay: "At noon to-day I had all hands called, and explained to them frankly the considerations which have determined me to remain where we are." He gave leave to any man who wanted to walk south, on condition that he renounce all claim on the commander in writing. Nine men left on 28 August under [[person:Isaac Israel Hayes]] and Carl Petersen. Eight stayed: in Kane's own words, "eight out of the seventeen survivors of my party resolved to stand by the brig" — Henry Brooks, James McGary, [[person:John Wall Wilson]], Henry Goodfellow, William Morton, Christian Ohlsen, Thomas Hickey and Hans Hendrik.

The nine got no further than the Etah district. They wintered in a hut at [[place:Anoatok]], were fed all winter on frozen seal and walrus by the Inughuit, and were finally carried back to the brig on Inughuit sledges. At three in the morning of 12 December 1854, with the thermometer at fifty below:

> "They stopped at the gangway, and, as I was about to challenge, one of them sprang forward and grasped my hand. It was Doctor Hayes." ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-006]])

Kane then had to settle the Inughuit's complaint that his own men had taken clothing and fox skins from [[person:Kalutunah]]'s hut while sheltering in it. The whole company survived the second winter chiefly on walrus and seal bought or begged from [[person:Metek]], [[person:Ootuniah]], [[person:Myouk]] and the other hunters of Etah. Two men — Jefferson Baker and Peter Schubert — died of what Kane called "an anomalous spasmodic disorder, allied to tetanus", and by spring every man aboard was scorbutic.

## Leaving the brig

They abandoned the *Advance*. Every man signed a paper concurring in an attempt to reach the south by boat; Kane nailed a memorial to a stanchion by the gangway as his vindication, hoisted the flags and hauled them down, and the party walked once round the brig.

> "I regard the abandonment of the brig as inevitable. We have by actual inspection but thirty-six days' provisions... Scurvy has enfeebled more or less every man in the expedition." ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-005]])

**The date is not settled, and this page does not settle it.** Kane's memorial and the signed pledge are dated **20 May 1855**. But Kane's own report counts **eighty-three days** from leaving the *Advance* to reaching Upernavik on 6 August — "on the 6th at Upernavik, eighty-three days after leaving the Advance" — which puts the departure at **15 May**. Both dates are in the record and both are Kane's, so both stand here and neither is chosen. The likeliest reconciliation — not a certainty — is that the sledges and the sick were moved forward to Anoatok in stages during the week before the formal ceremony of the 20th: Kane's own narrative of the first days of the ice-journey describes "the sick at Anoatok always dreary in their solitude" while the sledge party was still working down from the brig.

The retreat itself is [[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-002]]: two boats and a whaleboat dragged over the ice to open water ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-239]]), then some 1,300 miles rowed and sailed down the Greenland side of Baffin Bay. [[person:Nesark]] carried the sick men John Wall Wilson and George Whipple south on his own sledge, first up to the hut at Anoatok and then down to the boats off Pekiutlik — "under the faithful charge of old Nessark", as Kane writes it; [[person:Metek]] hauled stores forward to the farther depots. They rounded the north Greenland coast near Horse's Head on 3 August and came into [[place:Upernavik]] on the 6th.

> "We drank coffee that night before many a hospitable threshold, and listened again and again to the hymn of welcome, which, sung by many voices, greeted our deliverance."

One man, Christian Ohlsen, died on the way, near Pekiutlik, after breaking down while getting a boat across a tide-hole; the cape above him carries his name. Every other man came through — an outcome with almost no parallel in the period, and one that Kane owed to Inughuit food and Inughuit sledges at least as much as to his own discipline. He sailed from Upernavik on 6 September 1855 in the Danish barque *Mariane* under Captain Ammondson, carrying the boat *Faith*: "Except the furs on our backs, and the documents that recorded our labors and our trials, it was all we brought back of the *Advance* and her fortunes."

Kane died at Havana on 16 February 1857, aged thirty-six, less than a year after [[source:kane1856arctic|Arctic Explorations]] appeared. His funeral train from New Orleans to Philadelphia was the largest American public mourning between Washington's and Lincoln's. The *Advance* was never seen again.
