[[person:Isaac Israel Hayes]] had been Kane's surgeon. He was one of the nine who walked away from the *Advance* in August 1854 and one of the nine carried back to her on Inughuit sledges in December ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-006]]); he lost toes doing it. Five years later he went back as commander, in a schooner of his own, to prove the thing Kane's man [[person:William Morton]] thought he had seen from [[place:Cape Constitution]]: an ice-free ocean at the top of the world.

"Late in the evening of July 6th, 1860, the schooner *United States* was hauled into the stream" at Boston. "My party, when at length completed, numbered fourteen persons all told." Hayes gives no date for finally standing out to sea — one of many gaps in [[source:hayes1867open]], which is a literary book rather than a log. His own chart of the ground he covered is [[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-193]].

## Going north

He put to sea early on 12 August and was at [[place:Upernavik]] the same evening ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-203]]). His boatswain and carpenter [[person:Gibson Caruthers]] "had retired the night before in perfect health, and was found dead in his berth next morning"; he was buried in the Upernavik ground the following day. Hayes was held four days and engaged [[person:Jensen]] as interpreter and hunter.

"We had passed Melville Bay in fifty-five hours," and at Cape York on 25 August he found [[person:Hans Hendrik]] living in a sealskin tent "pitched about two hundred feet above the level of the sea", watching for a ship. Six years after Kane's party had left him behind, Hans Hendrik came aboard with his wife [[person:Merkut]] and their daughter [[person:Pingasuk]], "about ten months old, and rejoices in the name of Pingasuk — 'The Pretty One'". Hayes never once writes the name Hans Hendrik or Suersaq in the whole book: the man is "Hans" for four hundred and fifty pages.

The schooner was frozen in at [[place:Port Foulke]], on the Greenland side just south of Etah, at "this latitude, 78 deg 17 min N, longitude 73 deg W" ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-194]]). Hayes gives no date for entering the harbour, naming it, or being locked in; his remark of 12 July 1861, "It is just ten months to a day since we were locked up", puts it in the middle of September 1860. Counting his own "one hundred and twenty-six days" back from the sun's return on 18 February 1861 gives about 15 October 1860 for the last of the light. The schooner in her winter cradle is [[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-211]]; the observatory they built ashore is [[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-222]].

Of that winter he wrote the sentence the book is remembered for:

> "I have seen no expression on the face of Nature so filled with terror as THE SILENCE OF THE ARCTIC NIGHT." ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-014]])

He was also, in the middle of it, a working naturalist: the book's tailpieces include a snowflake magnified three diameters ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-204]]), the head of the sledge dog Oosisoak ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-219]]) and a portrait of Birdie, the Arctic fox that lived aboard ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-214]]).

## Sonntag's death

In the first fortnight of December 1860 a distemper killed eighteen of the thirty-six dogs Hayes had obtained. "Seven died during four days, and I saw with consternation my fine teams melting away and my hopes endangered." Without dogs there would be no sledge journey and no farthest north, so on 21 December — midwinter, in the dark — he sent his astronomer and second, [[person:August Sonntag]], and Hans Hendrik south to buy dogs from the Inughuit: one sledge, nine dogs, twelve days' provisions, no tent.

Sonntag never came back. By Hans Hendrik's account, given to Hayes on 1 February 1861, Sonntag ran ahead of the dogs to warm himself, went through thin ice at a recently opened tide-crack, refused to stop and change into the dry clothes on the sledge, and was speechless by the time Hans Hendrik got him into an abandoned snow hut at [[place:Sorfalik]]. He died there after nearly a day unconscious. Hayes gives no day of death; the only date anywhere is the one chiselled on the gravestone, "AUGUST SONNTAG. Died December, 1860, AGED 28 YEARS" ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-215]]).

Hayes cross-questioned him for an hour on 2 February and put three things he could not reconcile on the page — that Sonntag travelled five miles in wet clothing with an experienced hunter beside him, that the sledge apron and sleeping bag and a full change of dry clothes went unused, and that no message was sent:

> "Hans sticks to the story of yesterday; and, after questioning and cross-questioning him for an hour, I get nothing new." ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-016]])

He then formally declined the conclusion: "it would be unreasonable as well as unjust to suspect him of desertion." Note carefully what is and is not being said. The charge Hayes rejects is desertion, not killing; and he had already suspected Hans over the disappearance of the hunter [[person:Peter]] a month earlier and sent him out anyway, because "Hans was the only available driver, for he alone knew where to find the native villages". A white commander's suspicion of an Inuk employee, printed and never withdrawn, is part of this record; [[source:hendrik1878memoirs|Hans Hendrik's own memoirs]] are the other part, and neither should be read without the other. See [[page:the-inuit-of-these-expeditions]].

The teams were only rebuilt when [[person:Kalutunah]], [[person:Tattarat]] and [[person:Myouk]] came in with dogs at the end of February ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-223]]).

## The farthest north that is not accepted

At seven on the evening of 3 April 1861 Hayes left Port Foulke, twelve in number, with two dog sledges — Jensen's *Hope* with eight dogs and [[person:George F. Knorr]]'s *Perseverance* with six — and a twenty-foot metallic lifeboat on a man-hauled sledge, to cross Smith Sound and go north up the Ellesmere shore. "The distance from land to land, as the crow flies, did not exceed eighty miles"; the journey "consumed thirty-one days" and covered "about five hundred miles in all" over broken ice ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-197]]). He made the western shore near Cape Hawks on 11 May — "In camp at last, close under the land; and as happy as men can be who have achieved success and await supper."

Pushing on with Knorr alone after Jensen broke down, he stopped on 18-19 May at what he called [[place:Cape Lieber]] and left a record in a glass vial under a cairn:

> "This point, the most northern land that has ever been reached, was visited by the undersigned, May 18th, 19th, 1861... My observations place us in latitude 81 deg 35 min, longitude 70 deg 30 min W." ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-012]])

From a cliff he took to be eight hundred feet high he saw water again — Morton's sea, from the other side ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-198]]):

> "all the evidences showed that I stood upon the shores of the Polar Basin, and that the broad ocean lay at my feet." ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-013]])

**The claim is not accepted and never has been.** Hayes's own preface concedes that his positions were computed in the field without reducing the magnetic variation or fixing absolute time. Later surveys of the coast he described cannot be reconciled with 81 deg 35 min, and he was probably some degrees short. The coordinates carried here are *his figures*, flagged as such in the place and the event, and must not be read as a position. A headland he could only see in outline at the north, "I judged it to be in latitude 82 deg 30 min", he named Cape Union ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-220]]).

He was back aboard on 3 June: "Since I left her deck on the 3d of April" the party had "traveled not less than 1300 miles".

## Leaving

The ice let go on 11 July 1861 — "the schooner gave a lurch to port... and we were really afloat" — and by the next day, "We are fairly and truly afloat... It is just ten months to a day since we were locked up." Hayes said goodbye to the Inughuit at [[place:Etah]] on the 13th ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-212]]). Kalutunah wept.

> "'Alas!' said he, 'we will soon be all gone.' I told him that I would come back... 'Come back soon,' said he, 'or there will be none here to welcome you!'" ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-015]])

That was a demographic statement, not a sentiment. "From the information which I obtained through Hans and Kalutunah, I estimated the tribe to number about one hundred souls, — a very considerable diminution since Dr. Kane left them, in 1855." Hayes had a complete list of the deaths in those six years: thirty-four, against nineteen births.

He sailed on 14 July, was dismasted in an equinoctial gale, put into Halifax, and reached Boston in October — no day given; the only fix in the book is the first newspaper he bought, carrying the Ball's Bluff battle of 21 October 1861. He was refitting for a second attempt when the Civil War took his ship and his men. He never went back.

The open polar sea did not exist. But the water Morton and Hayes both saw does: the North Water polynya and the recurring open leads of Kennedy Channel, which is why both men, standing on opposite shores seven years apart, honestly reported an ocean.
