# Hall at Naujaat: five years, and the Inuit testimony on Franklin, 1864-1869
[page · kane-hall-polaris · draft]

[[person:Charles Francis Hall]]'s second expedition is the least known and, on the evidence, the most valuable. He reached New London on 30 June 1864 and embarked on the whaling brig *Monticello* the following day, and came home to New Bedford on 26 September 1869. Five years, five winters, two attempts at King William Island, and for almost all of it no white companions at all — he lived in Inuit households, travelled with Inuit families, and worked through [[person:Tookoolito]] and [[person:Ebierbing]], who were with him the whole time.

It began badly. Early in the morning of 31 August 1864 the tender *Helen F.* put his party ashore about forty miles south of where they meant to be, at lat. 64 deg 35 min N, long. 87 deg 33 min W on [[place:Roes Welcome Sound]]. "It was more than a disappointment to Hall, for it proved to be the loss of a whole year to the expedition."

Everything he saw in those five years was drawn as well as written down. [[source:nourse1879narrative|The narrative compiled from his manuscripts]] carries some sixty wood engravings cut from the objects themselves and from his own field sketches, and they are the fullest picture of Aivilingmiut and Netsilingmiut life in this whole layer.

## Living in the household

He did not visit Inuit camps; he lived in them. From his first autumn, writing to the captain of the *Monticello*:

> "I exchanged tent for snow-house, and have now been in the igloo sixty days, and all the time as comfortable as I ever was in winter in my life... one main igloo for myself and Eskimo children (Ebierbing and Too-koo-li-too), and three igloos, all joined to the main, for store-houses. A low, crooked passage-way of some 50 feet in length, made of snow, leads into our residence." ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-053]])

He drew the first igloo he ever lived in, with its ground plan ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-061]]), and went on drawing them: a village plan ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-071]]), a plan of one thrown up in a night on a sledge journey ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-072]]), the plan of a *qaggiq*, the big feasting igloo ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-085]]), a whole snow village ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-102]]). The snow-knife that cuts the blocks is [[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-060]] and the snow-shovel [[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-106]]; the lamp that heats the house is the *qulliq*, [[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-062]], and Hall's own lamp is [[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-093]]. He recorded the sledge ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-063]]), the kayak and its ornament at Repulse Bay ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-081]]), harpoon heads ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-076]]), the lance and its parts ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-069]]), arrows ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-094]]), snow-goggles ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-097]]), fur scrapers ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-064]]), ivory and bone combs ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-077]]), a stone pot ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-109]]), dog-skin mittens ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-068]]), deer-skin gloves ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-080]]) and sealskin boots ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-074]] and [[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-104]]). He also drew the games and the music: ball and cup ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-065]]), the tight-ropes ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-083]]), and the frame drum he spells *key-low-tik*, with its beater ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-067]]) and in use in a dance ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-066]]).

The same letter records what actually made five years possible: "I never before knew any Eskimos so provident as this tribe or clan I am wintering with. I doubt not they have four hundred or more of reindeer, killed last summer, on deposit within the distance of a circle of 20 miles in diameter." A caching system covering a circle twenty miles across, holding several hundred caribou through the winter, is not a detail of local colour. It is the food economy that carried the expedition, and Hall knew it.

(He also called two adults his "Eskimo children". The condescension and the accurate observation sit in the same sentence, and this page leaves both.)

## The testimony

His base for most of the five years was Naujaat (Repulse Bay), the same ground [[topic:franklin-search|John Rae]] had worked. What Hall did there that nobody else did was sit in winter igloos, night after night, listening.

The single best illustration is 6 December 1864. Walrus hunters were calling in at his igloo through the evening: Ar-mou first, then Ar-too-a the *angakkuq*, then Oong-oo-too, then [[person:Ou-e-la]] and Shoo-she-ark-nuk, who were Ar-too-a's brothers. Ar-too-a began telling them what he knew about Crozier. Tookoolito was sitting on the caribou-skin bed by the lamp, knitting Hall a worsted nose-guard for the winter's sledging, and listening.

> "I had not got two words out before Too-koo-li-too signaled to me by a motion of her hand to keep silent. She then said, They are saying something that I will like much to hear." ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-025]])

What followed was the Inuit account of [[person:Francis Crozier]]: that Crozier and three men, starving, would not eat the flesh of their dead companions as the others did. Hall obtained the most important body of Franklin evidence of his career because his interpreter knew the moment to tell him to be quiet. That sentence is worth more than most of the exploration literature of the century put together, and it belongs to Tookoolito.

## The shooting of Patrick Coleman

Hall was not a saint, and the record should not be tidied. In 1868 he had five white men hired from the whalers at Repulse Bay. Believing [[person:Patrick Coleman]] to be the leader of a mutiny among them, on **31 July 1868** he shot him:

> "His reply being still more threatening, I pulled trigger, and in a few minutes he staggered and fell."

Coleman "lingered from the 31st of July until the 14th of the following month" and died. Two days later, on 16 August, the whalers *Ansell Gibbs* and *Concordia* appeared at anchor and the four surviving hired men were discharged and sent home. Nothing in the record shows any inquiry, any charge or any consequence for Hall at all. (Some earlier accounts date the shooting to 1864; [[source:nourse1879narrative|the text]] is unambiguous that it was 1868.)

## King William Island, 1869

On 23 March 1869 Hall finally set out for King William Island ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-103]]): eleven souls, two sledges loaded to 2,724 and 2,521 pounds, eighteen dogs, ninety days out and back. The eleven were Hall, five Inuit men, three women and two children — Ebierbing and Tookoolito with their adopted daughter [[person:Panik]]; Ou-e-la; [[person:Pa-pa]]; [[person:Nu-ker-zhoo]] and his wife, who was Panik's birth mother, so that the adoption was an arrangement between two families travelling together rather than a removal; [[person:Eek-choo-ar-choo]], called Jerry, and his wife with an infant in her hood.

He had failed at the same journey in 1866, and on that return Tookoolito's and Ebierbing's second child died on the trail on 13 May and was buried the same evening at the *angakkuq*'s insistence — "The almost distracted mother, the moment she found that it was really dead, rushed out of the igloo, pressing the dead baby to her bosom." Hall built a monument of five stones over the grave the next day.

He reached the island in the spring of 1869 and put his question. He had come nine hundred miles to ask it, and it was four words:

> *Nou-tima Ag-loo-kar* — where is Crozier?

Aglooka was the Inuit name for Francis Crozier. The man he asked, about 1 May 1869, was [[person:In-nook-poo-zhee-jook]], a Netsilingmiut traveller who gave him over the following days the fullest Inuit account of the Franklin retreat anyone had collected. Hall wrote to [[person:Henry Grinnell]]:

> "His name is In-nook-poo-zhe-jook, and he is a native of Neitchille, a very great traveler and very intelligent. He is, in fact, a walking history of the fate of Sir John Franklin's Expedition." ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-026]])

In the same letter he recorded, with anger, what he had been told: that the Inuit "did secretly and hastily abandon Crozier and his party to suffer and die for need of fresh provisions, when in truth it was in the power of the natives to save every man alive." That judgement is Hall's, made from a warm igloo he had been fed in for five years, about people facing a starving party of forty white men in a season when their own food would not stretch. It should be read as his verdict, not as a finding.

He found a thigh bone on the Todd Islands on 11 May and an unburied skeleton near the mouth of the Peffer River on the 12th; he half-masted the American flag, built a monument of stones five feet high, fired a salute and sent the remains home. He raised three monuments in all, and drew what he saw of earlier qallunaat camps: a cairn built by white men ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-098]]), Monumental Inlet ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-099]]) and a tenting-place ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-100]]). He never crossed to the west coast of King William Island, where the retreat had actually passed — which is why the physical search there had to wait for Schwatka in 1878-80.

What he did bring back was the relics: spoons and forks ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-091]]), sabres ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-105]]), a desk from one of the ships and a needle-case made from Franklin-expedition material ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-108]]), and the assembled group ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-090]]). A leaf from his own note-book is [[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-110]].

The *Ansell Gibbs* left Repulse Bay on 13 August 1869 and lay off Whale Point until the 28th, Hall and Ebierbing hunting ashore for her — nearly three thousand pounds of meat in nine days' work, and a dangerous bear hunt on the 26th. Then she sailed, and came into New Bedford on 26 September: "How thankful to High Heaven ought my poor heart to be for the blessed privilege of again placing my foot upon the land of my country."

Two years later he had a ship, a naval commission and a mandate to reach the North Pole. See [[page:the-polaris-and-the-floe-drift]].
