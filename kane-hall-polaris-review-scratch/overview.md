Between 1853 and 1873 four American expeditions pushed north through Smith Sound, the narrow gate between northwest Greenland and Ellesmere Island that leads to the Lincoln Sea and the pole. All four went out under the flag of the search for [[topic:franklin-search|Sir John Franklin]] or of a theory about the pole. All four depended on Inuit hunters, drivers, seamstresses and interpreters for their lives, and all four said so unevenly. Two of them ended with people walking or drifting hundreds of kilometres to save themselves.

## The four voyages

**[[person:Elisha Kent Kane]], the brig *Advance*, 1853-1855.** Kane sailed from New York on 30 May 1853 with the seventeen men of his party in a hermaphrodite brig of 144 tons; he shipped the Kalaallit hunter [[person:Hans Hendrik]] at Fiskernaesset in July — "a boy of nineteen," he wrote, "an expert with the kayak and javelin" — and the Danish interpreter [[person:Carl Petersen]] in Greenland as well. He pushed further up Smith Sound than any ship had gone. In early September he warped the brig into [[place:Rensselaer Harbour]] at 78 deg 37 min N and never got her out again. Two dark winters, scurvy, a company that split in two, and then an 83-day boat retreat of some 1,300 miles to [[place:Upernavik]]. Three men died in all. See [[page:kane-at-rensselaer-harbour]], the expedition's own chart [[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-235]], and the two tracks [[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-001]] and [[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-002]].

**[[person:Isaac Israel Hayes]], the schooner *United States*, 1860-1861.** Kane's surgeon went back as commander to prove the open polar sea he believed Kane's men had seen. He wintered at [[place:Port Foulke]], lost his astronomer [[person:August Sonntag]] on a midwinter dog-buying journey with Hans Hendrik, and in May 1861 claimed a farthest north at [[place:Cape Lieber]] that has never been accepted. His own chart of the ground is [[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-193]]. See [[page:hayes-and-the-open-polar-sea]].

**[[person:Charles Francis Hall]], three expeditions, 1860-1871.** A Cincinnati printer with no money, no ship and no training, Hall went north as a passenger on a whaler, lost his boat in a gale in his first month, and so was forced to travel as Inuit travelled. On 2 November 1860 [[person:Tookoolito]] said "Good morning, sir" to him through the cabin door, and from that day she and her husband [[person:Ebierbing]] made his career possible. He recovered the Inuit memory of Frobisher's voyages of the 1570s, held nine generations ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-024]]), and then spent five years around Naujaat (Repulse Bay) collecting the fullest Inuit testimony on the Franklin retreat that anyone had gathered. The only photograph of him is [[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-042]]. See [[page:hall-among-the-inuit]] and [[page:hall-at-repulse-bay]].

**The *Polaris*, 1871-1873.** Hall got a ship at last ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-139]]), took her to the highest latitude any ship had reached — 82 deg 11 min N by the figure usually cited, though the official narrative itself prints four different numbers — wintered at [[place:Thank God Harbour]] and died there on 8 November 1871, saying in his delirium that he was being poisoned. A year later, in a gale near [[place:Littleton Island]] on 15 October 1872, nineteen people were left on an ice floe when the ship broke away ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-164]]). They drifted **198 days** and, by [[person:George Emory Tyson]]'s reckoning, about **fifteen hundred miles**, down Baffin Bay and along the Labrador coast, and every one of them lived — including a two-month-old baby — because [[person:Ebierbing]] and [[person:Hans Hendrik]] hunted for them. The whole company is in [[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-184]]. See [[page:the-polaris-and-the-floe-drift]], the track [[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-035]] and the contemporary drift chart [[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-182]].

## What the record is made of

Almost everything here comes from six published primary narratives: [[source:kane1856arctic]], [[source:hayes1867open]], [[source:hall1864life]], [[source:nourse1879narrative]], [[source:blake1874arctic]] and [[source:davis1876narrative]]. Between them they also carry several hundred wood engravings, charts and plates — Inuit tools and clothing drawn from the objects themselves, igloo ground plans, portraits of everyone aboard the *Polaris*, and Hall's own field sketches — and those pictures are as much of the record as the text.

Only one of these books was written by an Inuk: [[source:hendrik1878memoirs]], the memoirs of Hans Hendrik of Fiskernaesset, who served under Kane, Hayes, Hall and later Nares, and who was on the floe with his wife [[person:Merkut]] and their four children ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-173]]). Read against the commanders' books, his is the correction. Hayes cross-questioned him for an hour over Sonntag's death and put the suspicion in print ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-016]]); Hans Hendrik's own account answers it, and the two must be set side by side rather than either taken alone. See [[page:the-inuit-of-these-expeditions]].

## Why it matters here

Four reasons a scientist aboard this ship might care.

The **hydrography** is still live. What Kane's man [[person:William Morton]] saw from [[place:Cape Constitution]] on 24 June 1854 — "not a speck of ice", [[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-004]] — was not an open polar sea. It was the recurring polynya at the head of Baffin Bay and in Kennedy Channel, the North Water, which opens most years and which the Amundsen's own programmes study. Nineteenth-century expeditions read it as a gateway; it is a physical feature with a mechanism.

The **people** are not gone. Kane, Hayes and Hall all worked among the Inughuit of the Etah district, whose descendants live at Qaanaaq. [[person:Kalutunah]] told Hayes in 1861, "Come back soon, or there will be none here to welcome you" ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-015]]); Hayes put the whole group at about a hundred souls, "a very considerable diminution since Dr. Kane left them, in 1855", and counted thirty-four deaths against nineteen births in those six years. Tookoolito and Ebierbing were from Qikiqtaaluk (Baffin Island); two of their three children are buried at [[place:Groton]], Connecticut, and the third on the trail near Colvile Bay.

The **ice drift is a data set**. Tyson's floe was a drifting buoy with a man aboard writing down where it was, nearly every week from October 1872 to April 1873, from Smith Sound to 53 deg N: [[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-035]]. It is a 150-year-old trajectory through the Baffin Bay and Labrador Current system that can be set against a modern drifter track — with the caveat that Tyson gives a longitude only twice in the whole journal, so the east-west part of the line is reconstruction, not observation.

The **oral history was right**. Hall's method — ask, listen, record, check — produced testimony about Frobisher's 1576-1578 voyages that matched the written record in the number of ships and the five men lost, 285 years later, and testimony about Franklin's men that later searches confirmed. That is the strongest single argument in this whole layer for treating Inuit knowledge as evidence.
