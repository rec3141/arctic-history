*Saimaqatigiingniq*: "when past opponents get back together, meet in the middle,
are at peace" ([[artifact:relocations-qtc-032]]). It is the word the Qikiqtani
Inuit Association chose for what it wanted out of the Commission, and the title
of the Commissioner's final report. It is not the same as truth-telling, and it
is not the same as compensation.

## How the Commission worked

The QIA established the Qikiqtani Truth Commission in 2007. Between January 2008
and May 2009 Commissioner [[person:James Igloliorte]] — a retired judge from
Labrador — and his staff, under Executive Director
[[person:Madeleine Redfern]], visited all thirteen communities in the region and
also held hearings for Inuit in Ottawa. With interviews the QIA had made in
2004, they had testimony from about 350 people. They also did archival research
and interviewed qallunaat who had worked in the region, including retired RCMP
officers and government officials. In early 2010 they went back to every
community to report the findings and ask whether the proposed recommendations
were right.

Hearings were less formal than a court, but every witness was asked to affirm
they would tell the truth, and people who asked to speak privately were allowed
to. The results were published in 2013: the Commissioner's report and
twenty-two companion volumes — thirteen community histories and nine thematic
reports and special studies. The QIA reissued them in a revised edition in 2024.

**One point of scope has to be stated plainly.** In the Commissioner's own
words, "the QTC's mandate specifically excluded the High Arctic relocations and
residential schools issues": the relocations had been examined by the Royal
Commission on Aboriginal Peoples and the schools were the subject of the
then-ongoing Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Canada. The QTC's community
histories for [[place:Ausuittuq]] and [[place:Qausuittuq]] nevertheless tell the
relocation story at length, because those communities have no other story of
their founding — but the Commission's own investigation did not cover it. Most
of what [[page:relocations-the-1953-and-1955-high-arctic-relocations]] rests on
is therefore RCAP's 1994 report and the testimony given to it in 1993.

## What the Commission found

The findings across the reports run together. Inuit were moved into settlements
by a mixture of promise, pressure and necessity, under conditions where real
consent was rarely possible ([[page:relocations-moving-into-the-settlements]]).
The qimmiit were destroyed, not by a conspiracy but by "a series of
inter-connected government policies and laws"
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-022]], and
[[page:relocations-the-killing-of-the-qimmiit]]). Children were removed from
families for long periods, and families were separated by a medical system that
could only treat people two thousand kilometres from home
([[page:relocations-the-ships-that-took-people-south]]). By 1975 the change was
complete and could not be reversed ([[artifact:relocations-qtc-033]]).

The Commissioner's first recommendation was procedural and pointed: that the QIA
present the full report to the Government of Canada and ask for a formal
acknowledgement of its findings. He wrote that "Healing and reconciliation are
only possible when the party responsible for past wrongs fully accepts its
responsibility and commits to restoring the relationship." Inuit would be
receptive to a sincere apology, he added — "as long as that apology signals a
willingness to work with Inuit in a respectful partnership".

Twenty-five recommendations follow, in four groups: acknowledging and healing
past wrongs; strengthening Inuit governance so that decisions reflect Inuit
culture and needs; strengthening Inuit culture; and creating healthy
communities, against the alcohol and substance abuse, poor diet, unemployment,
low graduation rates, crime and bad housing that the Commission treats as
consequences rather than causes. The recommendation the qimmiit report ends on
asks the Government of Canada to acknowledge formally "that the high rates of
suicide, substance abuse, incarceration, and social dysfunction among Inuit are
in part symptoms of intergenerational trauma caused by historical wrongs."

The Commission also noted why this is not only a northern matter: in the
Canada's North poll conducted by Ipsos Reid for Inuit Tapiriit Kanatami in 2009,
one respondent in three scored a D or failed a simple true-or-false quiz about
Canada's Arctic, and 53 per cent strongly or somewhat disagreed that they were
"generally aware of the realities of life for the Inuit in the Canadian
Arctic."

## The apologies

There have been three, and they are separate acts about separate wrongs.

**Inukjuak, 18 August 2010.** [[person:John Duncan]], Minister of Indian Affairs
and Northern Development, apologised for the High Arctic relocation
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-013]]) — two and a half years after
[[person:John Amagoalik]] told the QTC that the file could not be closed until
it came ([[artifact:relocations-qtc-012]]):

> You can't forgive someone who doesn't acknowledge what happened. So they need
> to acknowledge, they need to apologize so we can at least have the opportunity
> to forgive them.

**Iqaluit, 8 March 2019.** Prime Minister [[person:Justin Trudeau]] apologised
for the federal government's management of the tuberculosis epidemic of the
1940s to the 1960s — the ships, the sanatoria and the graves that are the
subject of [[page:relocations-the-ships-that-took-people-south]]. The statement
is blunt about intent ([[artifact:relocations-qtc-043]]):

> Today, I am here to say sorry. To offer an official apology for the federal
> government's management of the tuberculosis epidemic from the 40s to the 60s.
> This policy wasn't an accident — it was purposeful. It was done even though
> the Government of Canada knew the toll on Inuit families.

It is worth noticing what else the same speech says. It does not treat the
tuberculosis evacuations as a single bad policy but as one strand of a
colonial programme, naming in one breath the forced relocations, the residential
schools, the disc numbers, and the qimmiit: "It happened during the same years
when the government identified Inuit with numbers on disks, and when families
had entire teams of qimmiit, sled dogs, killed by officials." That is close to
the QTC's own finding of "a series of inter-connected government policies"
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-022]]), arrived at from the other side.

The apology came with a programme rather than a payment. The
**Nanilavut Initiative** — *nanilavut*, "let us find them" —
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-044]]) opens the federal tuberculosis patient
records to families, funds travel to a burial place once it is found, and pays
for grave markers, plaques, community commemorations and public education. It
was built with Inuit Tapiriit Kanatami, the Inuvialuit Regional Corporation,
Makivik Corporation, the Nunatsiavut Government and Nunavut Tunngavik
Incorporated. The 2013 health report had made the need exact: patients who died
in the south were buried there, often in unmarked graves, and their families
were frequently never told ([[artifact:relocations-qtc-026]]).
[[person:Natan Obed]], president of ITK, called the apology "a necessary first
step toward closing this dark chapter in Canada's history" and noted that it was
"accompanied by actions". The same day's announcements attached $27.5 million
over five years to Inuit-led tuberculosis elimination — the goal is no
tuberculosis in Inuit Nunangat by 2030 — and, from Budget 2018, more than $640
million for housing in Inuit Nunangat, on the reasoning that tuberculosis is not
cured by medicine alone while overcrowding lasts. The incidence rate for Inuit
in Inuit Nunangat was then more than three hundred times that of Canada's
non-Indigenous population.

**A statement of apology regarding the findings of the QTC** — the thing
Commissioner Igloliorte's first recommendation actually asked for — was made
afterwards by the Government of Canada. It is the least documented of the three:
the Commission's own site carries it as a recorded video with no transcript and
no date, and no departmental news release for it has been traced, so its wording
is not quoted here.

## What it means for people working here now

The QTC's argument is that the years 1950-1975 are not background. They are the
proximate cause of the present shape of every community in Qikiqtaaluk: where it
stands, who lives in it, what languages are spoken in its houses, and how much
its people trust an official who arrives by ship with a plan.

Two of the communities the CCGS *Amundsen* works around exist only because of a
decision taken in Ottawa in 1953. The families put ashore at Qausuittuq are named
one by one in [[artifact:relocations-qtc-016]], and some of them are still alive;
[[page:relocations-qausuittuq]] follows one of the two,
[[place:Qausuittuq]], from the beach in September 1953 to the present townsite.
Research relationships in the region are built, or not built, on that history;
the Commission's word for what would make them possible is the one at the top of
this page.
