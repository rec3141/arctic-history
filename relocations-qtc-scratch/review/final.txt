########## relocations-overview
# What the government did in Qikiqtani, 1950-1975
[page · relocations-qtc · draft]

Between 1950 and 1975 the Canadian government changed almost everything about
how Inuit lived in the Qikiqtani region: where they camped, who fed them, who
taught their children, who owned their dogs, and who decided whether a sick
person went south on a ship. Most of the people who lived through it are still
alive, and their communities are the ones the *Amundsen* calls at.

In 2007 the Qikiqtani Inuit Association set up the **Qikiqtani Truth
Commission** to find out what had happened. Over three years the QTC took
statements from about 350 people in thirteen communities, set that testimony
beside the departmental files and the RCMP's own reports, and published its
findings in 2013: Commissioner Igloliorte's final report and twenty-two
companion volumes — thirteen community histories and nine thematic reports and
special studies. This topic follows the QTC's account, in the QTC's own terms.

One boundary has to be marked at the outset. In the Commissioner's own words,
"the QTC's mandate specifically excluded the High Arctic relocations and
residential schools issues": the relocations had already been examined by the
Royal Commission on Aboriginal Peoples, and the schools were before the Truth
and Reconciliation Commission of Canada. The QTC's community histories for Ausuittuq and Qausuittuq tell the
relocation story anyway, because it is their founding story, but the weight on
the 1953-55 material here rests on
[[source:rcap1994high|RCAP's 1994 report]] and the testimony given to it.

## The words the Commission uses

The QTC found that Inuit did not experience these years as a policy but as a
series of things done to them by people they could not refuse. Four words carry
most of the argument:

- **ilira** — the deep, deferential fear of a person with power over you: a fear
  that produces agreement without consent. The QTC uses it to explain why a
  file can record a "volunteer" where a family remembers having no choice.
- **qimmiit** — the dogs. Not pets and not equipment: the means of hunting,
  travel, rescue and independence. When the dogs went, the ability to leave the
  settlement went with them.
- **ilagiit nunagivaktangit** — the places where extended families lived and
  camped, the ordinary settlement pattern before centralization.
- **saimaqatigiingniq** — "when past opponents get back together, meet in the
  middle, and are at peace." It is the Commission's name for what it was
  trying to reach, and the title of its final report is *Achieving
  Saimaqatigiingniq*.

## What happened

**The High Arctic relocations.** In the summer of 1953 ten families — 54
people, seven from [[place:Inukjuak]] in Nunavik and three from
[[place:Mittimatalik]] — were carried north aboard the C. D. Howe and the
d'Iberville and put ashore in two places more than 2,000 km from home:
[[place:Qausuittuq]] on Cornwallis Island and Craig Harbour on Ellesmere
Island, from which the camp moved to the [[place:Lindstrom Peninsula]] and
eventually to [[place:Ausuittuq]]. Six more families followed in 1955. They had
been promised game, and told they could come home. Neither promise was kept.
See [[page:relocations-the-1953-and-1955-high-arctic-relocations]].

**The qimmiit.** Between the late 1950s and the early 1970s the dog teams of
Qikiqtaaluk were destroyed — shot by police and other officials, killed by
distemper spread through the new settlements, and made unusable by the
ordinances and the crowding of settlement life. The QTC did not find a written
order to kill the dogs, and did not need one to find that Inuit lost them
because of what governments did. See [[page:relocations-the-killing-of-the-qimmiit]].

**Tuberculosis.** Through the 1950s the annual Eastern Arctic Patrol ship —
the C. D. Howe, [[artifact:relocations-qtc-042]] — x-rayed whole communities at
the ship's rail and took the positives south on the spot, sometimes without a
word to the family on shore. By 1955 almost a thousand Inuit had been taken
south, and in 1956, the QTC reckons, one Inuk in seven was in a hospital in
southern Canada. Some were away for years; some
never came back, and their families were not told where they were buried. See
[[page:relocations-the-ships-that-took-people-south]].

**Into the settlements.** Families that had lived in ilagiit nunagivaktangit
were drawn into the settlements by the school, the nursing station, the family
allowance and the housing programme, and by the loss of the dogs that made
camp life possible. See [[page:relocations-moving-into-the-settlements]].

## The reckoning

The relocatees pressed their case from the late 1970s. The House of Commons
Standing Committee on Aboriginal Affairs twice found their complaints
substantiated; [[person:John Amagoalik]], then president of the Inuit Tapirisat
of Canada, put it to the committee in March 1990. In 1993 the Royal Commission
on Aboriginal Peoples heard the relocatees themselves, and in 1994
published [[source:rcap1994high|*The High Arctic Relocation*]], which found the
move had not been made with free and informed consent. In 1996 Canada signed a
memorandum of agreement with the Makivik Corporation and paid \$10 million into
a trust for the survivors — and refused to apologise.

The apology came on 18 August 2010, at Inukjuak, and named the failures one by
one: the families separated, the shelter and supplies not provided, the
distance never explained, the promise of return not kept
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-013]]). The QTC's own community histories, written
from testimony given before that day, still record people waiting for it
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-012]]). Two further apologies followed in 2019, for
the handling of the tuberculosis epidemic and for the QTC's findings
themselves.

On 10 September 2010, three weeks after the apology, a boulder with a bronze
plaque was unveiled at Ausuittuq beside a granite carving of a woman and child
by [[person:Looty Pijamini]], one of two monuments Nunavut Tunngavik Inc. had
commissioned in April 2008 ([[artifact:relocations-qtc-041]]); the companion
figure of a man, carved by [[person:Simeonie Amagoalik]], had been unveiled at
Qausuittuq two days earlier ([[artifact:relocations-qtc-051]]). The plaque's
wording is worth reading beside the Commission's: it credits the relocatees
with having "dedicated their lives to Canada's sovereignty in these lands and
waters."

The Commission asked for more than an apology. *Achieving Saimaqatigiingniq*
sets out twenty-five recommendations, and the standard it sets is not that the
record be corrected but that the relationship be repaired. See
[[page:relocations-saimaqatigiingniq]].

## Why it belongs on this ship

Every community the *Amundsen* works out of in Qikiqtaaluk exists in its
present form because of these years. Ausuittuq and Qausuittuq exist *only*
because of them: they were made by an act of government in 1953, and the
families put ashore at Qausuittuq are named one by one in
[[artifact:relocations-qtc-016]]. When the ship takes on a community liaison
at [[place:Mittimatalik]] or lands gear at [[place:Qausuittuq]], it is working
with families who were split by a decision taken in Ottawa within living
memory.

The related topics are [[topic:posts-rcmp-missions]], on the police posts and
missions that were the government's first presence here, and
[[topic:st-roch-modern]], for [[person:Henry Larsen]], who commanded the
St. Roch through the Northwest Passage and then, as Officer Commanding "G"
Division, made the promise of return that Ottawa did not honour
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-011]]).

########## relocations-the-1953-and-1955-high-arctic-relocations
# The High Arctic relocations, 1953 and 1955
[page · relocations-qtc · draft]

In the summer of 1953 the Canadian government moved ten Inuit families — 54
people — from [[place:Inukjuak]] on Hudson Bay and [[place:Mittimatalik]] on
northern Qikiqtaaluk to two places on the High Arctic islands where no Inuit had
lived permanently for centuries. Six more families followed in 1955. The
settlements they were made to build are now [[place:Qausuittuq]] and
[[place:Ausuittuq]], the two northernmost civilian communities in Canada.

## Why the government did it

Ottawa gave several reasons at different times, and the two commissions that
examined the record disagreed with it about which mattered.

The stated reason was hunger. Fur prices had collapsed. At Inukjuak, where the
trading companies had spent decades competing with easy credit and high fox
prices, families had come to depend on store food bought against relief and the
family allowance; officials reported a shortage of game. But the QTC's Grise
Fiord history is careful here: "the problems they faced were more complicated
than that", and the government did not, in fact, choose the most self-reliant
families. "Evidence shows . . . that families who were the most dependent on
government assistance were more likely to be relocated."

The unstated reason was sovereignty. There was precedent: in 1934 the
Department of the Interior authorised the Hudson's Bay Company to open a post
at [[place:Dundas Harbour]] on Devon Island and to move families to it — from
[[place:Kinngait]], and, the QTC adds, from [[place:Panniqtuuq]] and
[[place:Mittimatalik]] as well, fifty-two people in all — and the government's
own press release said why
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-010]]) — "the angle of occupation of the country,
now that aerial routes, mineral developments, and other reasons make possible
the claims of other countries to part of Canada's Arctic". The Dundas Harbour
families were unhappy, were moved on again to [[place:Ikpiarjuk]] and Fort
Ross, and asked for years to be taken home. They never were.

[[person:Alexander Stevenson]], the officer in charge of the 1950 Eastern
Arctic Patrol and later Federal Administrator of the Arctic, recommended in his
patrol report that Inuit be moved from northern Qikiqtaaluk to Devon Island and
settled up the Ellesmere coast, and repeated the suggestion in 1951. In
February 1952 [[person:Henry Larsen]] — the man who had taken the St. Roch
through the Northwest Passage, see [[topic:st-roch-modern]] — recommended to
the Commissioner of the RCMP that the Craig Harbour detachment be moved north
to the Bache Peninsula and that three or four families be recruited from
Mittimatalik to go with it.

## Consent, and the promise

Files call the relocatees "volunteers". The selection was made by individual
officers. The QTC's finding is blunt: "Inuit were never fully informed of
options or potential consequences", and testimony gathered by RCAP, the QIA and
the QTC "show that Inuit were afraid of the RCMP officers and felt pressured to
move." [[person:George Eckalook]] told the QIA that his parents "did not agree
to the move but later on they agreed but in their hearts, they were reluctant to
leave". [[person:Simeonie Amagoalik]] said: "It seemed like I had no other
choice but to say yes." This is the QTC's *ilira*: agreement without consent.

Because officials themselves doubted the plan, two undertakings were given.
Stevenson said that after two years anyone dissatisfied "could return to Port
Harrison". Larsen, remembering Dundas Harbour, wrote on 14 April 1953 that
"families will be brought home at the end of one year if they so desire"
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-011]]). Neither promise was kept.

## The voyage, and the splitting of the families

The relocatees boarded the C. D. Howe at Inukjuak in late July 1953. The ship
called at Mittimatalik in late August and took on three families whose High
Arctic experience was supposed to teach the Itivimiut how to live in the new
country; they expected to be paid for it and never were. At Craig Harbour the
C. D. Howe met the d'Iberville, and the families learned for the first time that
they were to be divided. [[person:John Amagoalik]], five or six years old:

> I remember we were all on the deck of the ship, the C. D. Howe, and all the
> women started to cry. And when women start to cry, the dogs join in. It was
> eerie.

([[artifact:relocations-qtc-001]].) One party went ashore at Craig Harbour on
29 August, with Constable [[person:G. K. Sargent]]. The d'Iberville tried to
reach [[place:Cape Herschel]] on eastern Ellesmere Island, where a third
settlement was planned, could not get through the ice, and returned in early
September to land those families at Craig Harbour too. RCAP notes drily that
this was lucky: game did not appear at Cape Herschel that year and the people
could not have survived. The remainder went ashore at Qausuittuq on 7 September
1953. The two commissions count the Craig Harbour party differently — RCAP
makes it three families in August and three more in September, six in all,
while the QTC's Grise Fiord history says six landed in August and two more the
following month — and this page follows RCAP, which worked from the
departmental file. Who went where, family by family, is in
[[artifact:relocations-qtc-016]].

## What they found

Nothing had been landed for them. Families had been told to bring only tents and
personal possessions. [[person:Samuel Arnakallak]] described the first night
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-002]]): a stone stove, no lamp, and one man's
flashlight passed from shelter to shelter so that people could eat.

The promised game was hedged about with law. [[person:Samwillie Elijasialak]]'s
parents were told they might take one caribou a year for the whole family, and
that killing a muskox meant a \$5,000 fine or arrest
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-003]]). At Qausuittuq the walrus had already
migrated when they landed in September, the caribou were protected, and the fish
in the lakes were full of parasites: "We didn't know any of this"
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-014]]). Fresh water at Ausuittuq had to be got from
sea ice, and it took a long time to learn to tell fresh ice from salt.

Two months after they landed, the sun went out, and stayed out for three
months. The Tununirmiut from Mittimatalik had some experience of a full winter
darkness; the Itivimiut from Inukjuak had none. [[person:Anna Nungaq]], in a
1989 interview: "Because I had never been in a place where there is no daylight
at all, I was so scared and thought there would never be light again"
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-004]]).

The Craig Harbour party was moved almost at once to the
[[place:Lindstrom Peninsula]], 70 km west of the police post — Sargent reported
in December 1953 that the site had been chosen because it reduced the chance of
Inuit becoming dependent on the RCMP while still allowing some contact, and
because it stood a reasonable distance from the caribou herds. The camp had no
Hudson's Bay Company store. A small trading store opened beside the detachment
at Craig Harbour in the summer of 1954, looked after by
[[person:Thomasie Amagoalik]] with the RCMP doing the bookwork, and moved with
the detachment to Grise Fiord in 1956. It was badly supplied. [[person:Rynee Flaherty]], who came
in 1955, told RCAP how she had tried to feed her infant son on flour and water
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-005]]).

## Asking to go home

In the first spring people asked to leave. They were refused
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-008]]), and told instead to bring their relatives
north ([[artifact:relocations-qtc-015]]). [[person:Josephie Flaherty]] asked in
1959-60 and was told a school was coming and he had children to educate.
[[person:Samwillie Elijasialak]] recalled being told that a family wanting to
return would first have to find someone to take its place — "said by people
where no appeal was available to a higher authority"
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-009]]).

The two groups did not mix easily. The Mittimatalik families had been told the
Inukjuak people were poor and lived on relief; the Itivimiut did not think of
themselves that way. By 1958 the Inukjuak families at the Lindstrom Peninsula
had split off to a camp 3 km away. Constable [[person:Terry Jenkin]], posted in
1962, thought outside observers had made too much of the division.

Tuberculosis did the rest. [[person:Markoosie Patsauq]] told RCAP that he was
already infected when he was moved, and that others caught it from him. John
Amagoalik: "The first ten years in Resolute were the most terrible years of our
lives. We spent years without mothers, without fathers, without brothers,
without sisters, who were all sick in the hospital"
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-006]]). See
[[page:relocations-the-ships-that-took-people-south]].

## Redress

Relocatees began returning at their own expense in the 1970s and 1980s; in 1988
the government paid for a number of returns. Two unanimous reports of the
Commons Standing Committee on Aboriginal Affairs found the complaints
substantiated, and in March 1990 John Amagoalik, then president of the Inuit
Tapirisat of Canada, told the committee they had been dealing with the
relocation for more than thirty years. In 1993 RCAP heard the relocatees; in
1994 it published
[[source:rcap1994high|*The High Arctic Relocation*]]. In 1996 Canada signed a
memorandum of agreement with the Makivik Corporation acknowledging both the
relocatees' contribution to "a Canadian presence" in the High Arctic and the
"hardship, suffering and loss" of the early years, and paid \$10 million into a
trust — while refusing to apologise.

The apology was given at Inukjuak on 18 August 2010
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-013]]). It named the separation of families, the
inadequate shelter and supplies, the failure to say how far and how different
the new homes would be, and the broken promise of return.

Nunavut Tunngavik Inc. had commissioned two monuments in April 2008, one for
each community, each carver required to train an apprentice. They were unveiled
three weeks after the apology, on 8 and 10 September 2010: a figure of a man at
Qausuittuq by [[person:Simeonie Amagoalik]], who had been landed there as a boy
in 1953, working with Jeffrey Amagoalik ([[artifact:relocations-qtc-051]]), and
a granite woman and child at Ausuittuq by [[person:Looty Pijamini]], working
with Matt Pijamini, with a bronze plaque set in a boulder beside it
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-041]]). The two figures stand for the families
divided at Craig Harbour. The plaque's wording is the relocatees' own account
of what they did, and it is not the government's: they came "to pursue the
Government's promise of a more prosperous life", they "endured and overcame
great hardship", and they "dedicated their lives to Canada's sovereignty in
these lands and waters."

########## relocations-the-killing-of-the-qimmiit
# The qimmiit: how the dog teams of Qikiqtaaluk were destroyed
[page · relocations-qtc · draft]

Between 1950 and 1975 the number of qimmiit — Inuit sled dogs — in Qikiqtaaluk
fell from three or four thousand to a few dozen. That single figure, from the
Qikiqtani Truth Commission's thematic report
[[source:qtc2013qimmiit|*Qimmiliriniq*]], is the shape of the thing. What is
argued about is how it happened, and whether anyone meant it to.

## What a team was

What a fan-hitch team looked like, and what keeping one running took, is in
Joseph Dewey Soper's 1929 photograph of Shappa clearing tangled traces at
[[place:Kinngait]] ([[artifact:relocations-qtc-062]]).

A qimmiq was not equipment and not a pet. The dogs were the only means of winter
travel; they pulled sleds, carried packs in summer, found seal holes, kept off
the ice cracks in fog and darkness, and held a polar bear for the hunter. They
were the only animals Inuit gave individual names. Children raised puppies, and
being able to handle a team was one of the marks of becoming an adult.
[[person:Pauloosie Veevee]] told QIA researchers that a man was judged by his
dogs' "performance, appearance, health, and endurance"
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-017]]). Losing the team did not mean losing
transport. It meant losing the ability to leave.

## The Dog Ordinance

In 1949-50 Ottawa revised the Northwest Territories *Ordinance Respecting Dogs*.
The QTC's judgement is that the revision "effectively outlawed traditional Inuit
ways of handling dogs, wherever this seemed to conflict with the needs or
practices of a growing qallunaat population"
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-022]]). Under it — and, the QTC found, often beyond
it — police, other government agents, military personnel, Inuit special
constables and hired dogcatchers shot dogs they judged loose, sick, dangerous or
simply surplus.

Enforcement did not need a rifle to bite. At [[place:Panniqtuuq]] the RCMP ran
the post office and handed out the family allowance cheques, and Pauloosie
Veevee remembered that every time, before the cheques came out, the officer
asked whether the family's dogs were tied up
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-019]]). [[person:Levi Evic]], camped at Panniqtuuq
to trade in 1955 or 1956, was warned through the Hudson's Bay Company manager
that the dogs would be shot in the morning; the family harnessed up and left in
the middle of a winter night ([[artifact:relocations-qtc-021]]).

## Two policies at once

People still ask how the same force could vaccinate dogs and shoot them. The
QTC's answer is that the government was pursuing two aims that did not fit
together, and it quotes the government's own words for 1954
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-018]]): leave Inuit in remote areas undisturbed,
integrate those in contact with the "white economy", and "continue to transfer
families from unproductive areas to regions where game is more abundant".

The RCMP, at least until the 1960s, wanted Inuit to stay on the land and away
from the bases — which required healthy dogs, hence publicly funded immunisation
— but also thought Inuit kept more dogs than hunting could support, so officers
"found opportunities to kill qimmiit they considered to be surplus". At the same
time federal policy was drawing people into settlements with schools, housing,
wage work and health care, where a loose team was a hazard among newcomers who
did not know how to be around dogs.

## Three waves

*Qimmiliriniq* separates the killings into episodes rather than one slaughter:

1. **[[place:Iqaluit]], after 1956.** The first systematic killing of loose
   dogs, at the same time as Inuit were drawn in by work at the airport and the
   radar stations, and the town of Apex was built at Niaqunngut to house them.
2. **Cumberland Sound, 1962.** An epidemic of canine distemper or hepatitis
   reached the ilagiit nunagivaktangit around [[place:Panniqtuuq]], and the RCMP
   answered it by systematically killing almost the entire dog population of the
   region. [[person:Mosesee Qiyuakjuk]] testified that the shooting had been
   going on well before the epidemic, and for less reason
   ([[artifact:relocations-qtc-020]]).
3. **After the schools were built.** As almost the whole regional population was
   drawn into thirteen settlements, the dogs went piecemeal — earlier in some
   places, such as [[place:Kinngait]] — shot by many different hands, including
   Inuit who were made to shoot their own teams before moving in. The sharpest
   single episode was at Panniqtuuq in 1966-67, where more than four hundred
   qimmiit were shot around the settlement.

There is also evidence of dogs killed to frighten their owners: Levi Evic's
family, and the day about 1961-62 when [[person:Lucian Ukkalianuk]]'s whole team
was destroyed at [[place:Iglulik]] after the dogs got loose in the settlement,
which [[person:Louis Uttak]] described to the QIA.

## The RCMP's review, and the QTC's answer

Public discussion resumed at a meeting in Iqaluit in February 1999 between
community members and senior RCMP officers. A month later, at the Makivik
Corporation's annual meeting, Nunavimmiut called for an inquiry, an apology and
compensation, and the QIA — whose president had been at that meeting — repeated
the call at once. On 10 March 2005 the House of Commons Standing Committee on
Aboriginal Affairs and Northern Development, having heard Inuit witnesses,
called for a public inquiry by a superior court judge "to get to the bottom of
the matter", a recommendation the Legislature of Nunavut later endorsed. The
government did not hold one. On 28 April 2005 Anne McLellan, Deputy Prime
Minister and Minister of Public Safety, asked the RCMP to review its own
actions instead. The Force's twenty-six-page final report of 2006,
*The RCMP and the Inuit Sled Dogs (Nunavut and Northern Quebec: 1950-1970)*,
accepted that members and other persons in authority had killed hundreds and
perhaps thousands of dogs, and concluded that the killings had never been part
of a conspiracy to force Inuit off the land.

The QTC published a point-by-point answer,
[[source:qtc2013rcmpreview|*Analysis of the RCMP Sled Dog Report*]]. Its
objection is to the method as much as the finding. Because the RCMP could not
conduct an independent inquiry into itself, most Inuit would not give evidence
to it, and the report treated that refusal as proof of political intimidation
rather than as a fact about trust. It analysed the killings as a law-enforcement
question, taking the qallunaat presence and settlement life as given, and — the
QTC's central charge — directed its analysis "toward discrediting Inuit
memories", "denying the validity of most Inuit oral evidence yet presenting the
views of non-Inuit with little negative comment"
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-023]]).

The QTC did not find a conspiracy either. It found something it thought worse
and better documented: "a series of inter-connected government policies and laws
put into effect and enforced by the RCMP, which quickly undermined traditional
Inuit ways of living" ([[artifact:relocations-qtc-022]]). Its legal point is
that Canada failed its obligations to Inuit when it restricted their use of dogs
without involving them in finding workable rules.

## What it left

Snowmobiles came, but not in time: for many years dogs were being shot when
there was no reliable machine to replace them, and after about 1966 the machines
were reliable but expensive to buy, run and maintain. The QTC's conclusion
places the qimmiit inside the larger pattern — "government domination and
interference with Inuit decision-making" — and its Commissioner recommended
that Canada formally acknowledge that today's rates of suicide, substance abuse
and incarceration among Inuit are in part symptoms of intergenerational trauma
caused by historical wrongs. See [[page:relocations-saimaqatigiingniq]].

The related police history is [[topic:posts-rcmp-missions]].

########## relocations-the-ships-that-took-people-south
# Tuberculosis: the ships that took people south
[page · relocations-qtc · draft]

Every summer through the 1950s a ship came round the Qikiqtani coast, and
everybody went aboard to be x-rayed. Anyone whose film showed tuberculosis did
not come back ashore. The ship sailed with them on it.

## How it worked

In 1945, in the middle of a tuberculosis epidemic, responsibility for the
health of First Nations and Inuit was moved to the federal Department of
National Health and Welfare — which had, in the whole of what is now Nunavut,
two hospitals and forty-eight beds for a little under four thousand people. The Eastern Arctic Patrol, which had been
running since 1922 as a sovereignty and supply voyage, became the instrument.
From 1946 the patrol x-rayed at every stop, and, in the QTC's words, "anyone
diagnosed as being tubercular had to immediately board the ship to be sent
south."

Treatment meant a southern sanatorium. That was the policy in Ontario and Quebec
and it was applied unchanged to people who lived two or three thousand
kilometres away. The government's argument was that it was the cheapest
available measure. The QTC's comment: "it was also very likely the least
humane."

The first ships were so crowded with patients, crew, cargo, officials and
traders that the patrol was "almost as much a source of disease as a method of
prevention". After the Nascopie was lost in 1947 the government fitted out the
C. D. Howe ([[artifact:relocations-qtc-042]]): fifty-eight crew, room for thirty
Inuit passengers, a surgery, a dispensary, an x-ray room, a dental office and
six beds, and radar, echo sounding and a helicopter for moving people between
ship and shore. Her route is
[[artifact:relocations-qtc-028]].

Shiptime had been the year's gathering. Now it became the thing to hide from. By
1955 the RCMP were reporting that people around [[place:Kimmirut]] were staying
away from the settlement when the ship was due, having no wish to be "evacuated
to the Land of No Return" ([[artifact:relocations-qtc-027]]). Priests sometimes
hid people. Families in the camps ran when they saw the ship — or heard the
helicopter, which the C. D. Howe carried to fetch in those who had not come.

## What it cost

By 1955 almost a thousand Inuit had been taken south. Treatment averaged twenty
months. In 1956, on the QTC's reckoning, one Inuk in seven was in a hospital in
southern Canada.

The taking was done without ceremony and often without goodbyes. A family would
watch someone go up the side for tests and then watch the ship sail with them
still aboard. [[person:Elijah Padluq]] on his mother:

> [The] patients were not to get off the ship. They stayed on the ship because
> they had TB . . . They would leave in July . . . My mother finally came back a
> year later, in the following spring.

([[artifact:relocations-qtc-025]].) [[person:Walter Rudnicki]], a retired social
worker, told the QTC in 2008 that the policy was applied even to nursing
mothers: "The baby was sent to the person [standing closest] to the mother and
she was sent off," he explained, "no concern was given to who would take care of
the child. I saw this going on" ([[artifact:relocations-qtc-026]]). He also
described patients discharged in winter in the summer clothing they had left in
— "It never occurred to the medical personnel that you don't wear summer
clothes in the dead of winter in Pang."

## The children

Children went south with the adults. [[person:Miluqtituttuq Akesuk]] was ten
when she was sent from [[place:Kinngait]] to Toronto in 1964:

> I thought I was lost. I thought I would never come back here to my parents
> when I first went down. I really thought I was lost.

([[artifact:relocations-qtc-024]].) Many came back having lost Inuktitut and
without the knowledge their age-mates had gained on the land. Record-keeping was
poor enough that patients were returned to the wrong communities — and, the QTC
records plainly, that families "waited for their children and parents to return,
not knowing that they had died at the hospital." Children and adults from
Qikiqtaaluk were sent as far as the Mountain Sanatorium at [[place:Hamilton]],
Ontario, which had a schoolroom for the children who spent years there.

The pattern is why the relocation and the sanatoria cannot be told apart.
[[person:John Amagoalik]] at [[place:Qausuittuq]], on families that were already
2,250 km from home: "We spent years without mothers, without fathers, without
brothers, without sisters, who were all sick in the hospital"
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-006]]). [[person:Markoosie Patsauq]] told RCAP that
he had tuberculosis when he was relocated and should have been in hospital
instead; others in the new settlement caught it from him.

## What was known at the time

The government was told. In 1951 RCMP Inspector [[person:Henry Larsen]] wrote
that the effect of the evacuation policy on Inuit was "not good", and that "the
period of recovery and convalescence might be much shorter if the Eskimos went
under treatment in the environment to which they belong." Anglican Bishop Marsh
wrote to the minister, Jean Lesage, that he had come to the conclusion the
Department of Health and Welfare "are not concerned with the true meaning of the
two words (as far as the Eskimos are concerned) which make up the title of the
Department." The QTC records that many people wanted treatment centres
established in the north, and that proposals for improvement sent to Ottawa were
regularly rejected. The hospital at [[place:Iqaluit]] — later Qikiqtani General
— opened only in November 1964, with twenty adult beds and eight for children.
The Eastern Arctic Patrol's final charter was completed in 1969, and the patrol
was cancelled that year; even before it ended, some communities went a year or
more without seeing a doctor.

## Afterwards

The evacuations also did what nothing else had: they emptied the camps of the
people who ran them, and made the settlement, with its nursing station, the
place a family had to be. That is one of the threads in
[[page:relocations-moving-into-the-settlements]]. And the search for the graves
of those who never came home is unfinished work, taken up by families and by
Inuit organizations long after the QTC reported; see
[[page:relocations-saimaqatigiingniq]].

########## relocations-moving-into-the-settlements
# Nuutauniq: moving into the settlements, and ilira
[page · relocations-qtc · draft]

In 1950 the region's roughly 2,200 Inuit lived mostly on the land, in more than
a hundred *ilagiit nunagivaktangit* — the places where a kin group of five to
thirty people lived and hunted, moving between them with the seasons. By 1975
all but a few families lived in government-created settlements; by 1981 four
times as many people lived in just thirteen of them. The QTC's
thematic report on the moves, [[source:qtc2013nuutauniq|*Nuutauniq*]], is about
how that happened; the Commissioner's own summing-up is
[[artifact:relocations-qtc-033]]: "The decision to give up the traditional way of
life was almost never an easy one, and once made, it proved to be irreversible."

## Four kinds of move

*Nuutauniq* refuses to call every move by one name
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-030]]). A **relocation** is a planned move to a place
chosen by somebody else. A **migration** is a move Inuit made themselves, or
that at least looked voluntary, or that was drawn by enticements. A
**dislocation** is a coerced move by people under pressure from qallunaat. An
**evacuation** is a temporary move by government in a real or supposed
emergency. The moves of 1950-1975 were, in the Commission's words, "a mix of
voluntary, pressured and forced moves . . . usually in response to government
priorities".

The distinction matters because the file and the memory rarely agree. Officials
"often thought a pressured 'dislocation' was a voluntary 'migration'".

## Ilira

The QTC's explanation for that gap is *ilira*: "powerful social fear or
inhibition caused by inequality in power." [[person:Rosemarie Kuptana]], whose
1993 essay the Commission relies on — it prints her name as Rosemary Kuptana —
wrote that a generation or two earlier "a challenge to the authority of the
Qallunaat or defiance of their requests was almost unthinkable."

The clearest illustration in the report is an exchange at a QTC hearing
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-029]]). Commissioner [[person:James Igloliorte]]
asked [[person:Gordon Rennie]], a former Hudson's Bay Company manager, whether
anyone had ever questioned him, or questioned the dog laws. Rennie: "They
wouldn't dare." He went on to explain: "Nobody questioned me . . . I was a
person in authority then."

*Nuutauniq* adds the practical mechanics. Inuit often disagreed by falling
silent or withdrawing, and qallunaat read silence as assent. "To an Inuk,
Qallunaat appeared to be demanding consent, not asking for it." Add language,
distance from anyone who could decide anything, and administrators who "usually
made optimistic forecasts that failed to come true", and real consent — which
in law requires a full understanding of the action and its likely effects — was
rarely possible.

None of which means every move was forced. Many families went in because they
were told, repeatedly, that the settlement held housing, health care, school and
wages, and they believed it. [[person:Gene Rheaume]], active in the North as both civil
servant and politician, put it: "It was [sometimes] a subtle pressure, but it was
coming at you from everywhere."

## What pulled and what pushed

The pulls were real and were promised: a school, a nursing station, a house,
family allowance, wage work at an airstrip or a DEW Line site. The federal
government wanted people in settlements because services were cheaper to deliver
there; it also, at the same time, wanted them self-sufficient on the land. The
QTC's judgement is that these two aims were simply contradictory, and Inuit paid
for the contradiction.

The pushes ran through the same institutions:

- **School.** Children had to be where the school was, so a family's choice
  became a choice between the camp and the children. Those who stayed out saw
  children taken to hostels.
- **Health.** The nursing station was in the settlement, and after the
  tuberculosis years no family took the risk of being far from one. See
  [[page:relocations-the-ships-that-took-people-south]].
- **The dogs.** Without a team a family could not go back out, and the teams
  were being destroyed in exactly these years. See
  [[page:relocations-the-killing-of-the-qimmiit]].
- **Money.** Family allowance and relief were paid where the officials were —
  and, as at [[place:Panniqtuuq]], sometimes handed over only after a question
  about whether the dogs were tied up ([[artifact:relocations-qtc-019]]).

## What it cost

The report is precise about the loss because it is not the loss of a house.
"Qikiqtaalummiut suffered what scholars have called 'domicide' (the killing of
one's home) when they left the land . . . It represents the loss of independence
and replacement of a way of life" ([[artifact:relocations-qtc-031]]).

Transport and communications stayed in qallunaat hands, so families who had
moved in had few ways to move back out; the move was one-way in practice as well
as in law. Settlement life brought its own poverty — cash costs against a diet
that had been free — and cut access to country food. And the separations
compounded: "Every Inuk who appeared before the QTC experienced a long
separation from family members because of relocations, medical evacuations, or
schooling." A generation of young people lost contact with the land and with
the knowledge that goes with it.

The QTC does not present this as a story with no gains. Some people got less
risk in daily life, better health care, and the choice of a wage. The
Commission's point is that they were not given the information or the power to
weigh the trade, and that the trade could not be undone.

The extreme case of a planned move to a place chosen by somebody else is
[[page:relocations-the-1953-and-1955-high-arctic-relocations]]. What the
Commission asked for afterwards is in [[page:relocations-saimaqatigiingniq]].

########## relocations-saimaqatigiingniq
# Saimaqatigiingniq: the Commission, its findings and the apologies
[page · relocations-qtc · draft]

*Saimaqatigiingniq*: "when past opponents get back together, meet in the middle,
are at peace" ([[artifact:relocations-qtc-032]]). It is the word the Qikiqtani
Inuit Association chose for what it wanted out of the Commission, and the title
of the Commissioner's final report. It is not the same as truth-telling, and it
is not the same as compensation.

## How the Commission worked

The QIA established the Qikiqtani Truth Commission in 2007. Between January 2008
and May 2009 Commissioner [[person:James Igloliorte]] — a retired judge from
Labrador — and his staff, under Executive Director
[[person:Madeleine Redfern]], visited all thirteen communities in the region and
also held hearings for Inuit in Ottawa. With interviews the QIA had made in
2004, they had testimony from about 350 people. They also did archival research
and interviewed qallunaat who had worked in the region, including retired RCMP
officers and government officials. In early 2010 they went back to every
community to report the findings and ask whether the proposed recommendations
were right.

Hearings were less formal than a court, but every witness was asked to affirm
they would tell the truth, and people who asked to speak privately were allowed
to. The results were published in 2013: the Commissioner's report and
twenty-two companion volumes — thirteen community histories and nine thematic
reports and special studies. The QIA reissued them in a revised edition in 2024.

**One point of scope has to be stated plainly.** In the Commissioner's own
words, "the QTC's mandate specifically excluded the High Arctic relocations and
residential schools issues": the relocations had been examined by the Royal
Commission on Aboriginal Peoples and the schools were the subject of the
then-ongoing Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Canada. The QTC's community
histories for [[place:Ausuittuq]] and [[place:Qausuittuq]] nevertheless tell the
relocation story at length, because those communities have no other story of
their founding — but the Commission's own investigation did not cover it. Most
of what [[page:relocations-the-1953-and-1955-high-arctic-relocations]] rests on
is therefore RCAP's 1994 report and the testimony given to it in 1993.

## What the Commission found

The findings across the reports run together. Inuit were moved into settlements
by a mixture of promise, pressure and necessity, under conditions where real
consent was rarely possible ([[page:relocations-moving-into-the-settlements]]).
The qimmiit were destroyed, not by a conspiracy but by "a series of
inter-connected government policies and laws"
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-022]], and
[[page:relocations-the-killing-of-the-qimmiit]]). Children were removed from
families for long periods, and families were separated by a medical system that
could only treat people two thousand kilometres from home
([[page:relocations-the-ships-that-took-people-south]]). By 1975 the change was
complete and could not be reversed ([[artifact:relocations-qtc-033]]).

The Commissioner's first recommendation was procedural and pointed: that the QIA
present the full report to the Government of Canada and ask for a formal
acknowledgement of its findings. He wrote that "Healing and reconciliation are
only possible when the party responsible for past wrongs fully accepts its
responsibility and commits to restoring the relationship." Inuit would be
receptive to a sincere apology, he added — "as long as that apology signals a
willingness to work with Inuit in a respectful partnership".

Twenty-five recommendations follow, in four groups: acknowledging and healing
past wrongs; strengthening Inuit governance so that decisions reflect Inuit
culture and needs; strengthening Inuit culture; and creating healthy
communities, against the alcohol and substance abuse, poor diet, unemployment,
low graduation rates, crime and bad housing that the Commission treats as
consequences rather than causes. The recommendation the qimmiit report ends on
asks the Government of Canada to acknowledge formally "that the high rates of
suicide, substance abuse, incarceration, and social dysfunction among Inuit are
in part symptoms of intergenerational trauma caused by historical wrongs."

The Commission also noted why this is not only a northern matter: in the
Canada's North poll conducted by Ipsos Reid for Inuit Tapiriit Kanatami in 2009,
one respondent in three scored a D or failed a simple true-or-false quiz about
Canada's Arctic, and 53 per cent strongly or somewhat disagreed that they were
"generally aware of the realities of life for the Inuit in the Canadian
Arctic."

## The apologies

There have been three, and they are separate acts about separate wrongs.

**Inukjuak, 18 August 2010.** [[person:John Duncan]], Minister of Indian Affairs
and Northern Development, apologised for the High Arctic relocation
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-013]]) — two and a half years after
[[person:John Amagoalik]] told the QTC that the file could not be closed until
it came ([[artifact:relocations-qtc-012]]):

> You can't forgive someone who doesn't acknowledge what happened. So they need
> to acknowledge, they need to apologize so we can at least have the opportunity
> to forgive them.

**Iqaluit, 8 March 2019.** Prime Minister [[person:Justin Trudeau]] apologised
for the federal government's management of the tuberculosis epidemic of the
1940s to the 1960s — the ships, the sanatoria and the graves that are the
subject of [[page:relocations-the-ships-that-took-people-south]]. The statement
is blunt about intent ([[artifact:relocations-qtc-043]]):

> Today, I am here to say sorry. To offer an official apology for the federal
> government's management of the tuberculosis epidemic from the 40s to the 60s.
> This policy wasn't an accident — it was purposeful. It was done even though
> the Government of Canada knew the toll on Inuit families.

It is worth noticing what else the same speech says. It does not treat the
tuberculosis evacuations as a single bad policy but as one strand of a
colonial programme, naming in one breath the forced relocations, the residential
schools, the disc numbers, and the qimmiit: "It happened during the same years
when the government identified Inuit with numbers on disks, and when families
had entire teams of qimmiit, sled dogs, killed by officials." That is close to
the QTC's own finding of "a series of inter-connected government policies"
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-022]]), arrived at from the other side.

The apology came with a programme rather than a payment. The
**Nanilavut Initiative** — *nanilavut*, "let us find them" —
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-044]]) opens the federal tuberculosis patient
records to families, funds travel to a burial place once it is found, and pays
for grave markers, plaques, community commemorations and public education. It
was built with Inuit Tapiriit Kanatami, the Inuvialuit Regional Corporation,
Makivik Corporation, the Nunatsiavut Government and Nunavut Tunngavik
Incorporated. The 2013 health report had made the need exact: patients who died
in the south were buried there, often in unmarked graves, and their families
were frequently never told ([[artifact:relocations-qtc-026]]).
[[person:Natan Obed]], president of ITK, called the apology "a necessary first
step toward closing this dark chapter in Canada's history" and noted that it was
"accompanied by actions". The same day's announcements attached $27.5 million
over five years to Inuit-led tuberculosis elimination — the goal is no
tuberculosis in Inuit Nunangat by 2030 — and, from Budget 2018, more than $640
million for housing in Inuit Nunangat, on the reasoning that tuberculosis is not
cured by medicine alone while overcrowding lasts. The incidence rate for Inuit
in Inuit Nunangat was then more than three hundred times that of Canada's
non-Indigenous population.

**A statement of apology regarding the findings of the QTC** — the thing
Commissioner Igloliorte's first recommendation actually asked for — was made
afterwards by the Government of Canada. It is the least documented of the three:
the Commission's own site carries it as a recorded video with no transcript and
no date, and no departmental news release for it has been traced, so its wording
is not quoted here.

## What it means for people working here now

The QTC's argument is that the years 1950-1975 are not background. They are the
proximate cause of the present shape of every community in Qikiqtaaluk: where it
stands, who lives in it, what languages are spoken in its houses, and how much
its people trust an official who arrives by ship with a plan.

Two of the communities the CCGS *Amundsen* works around exist only because of a
decision taken in Ottawa in 1953. The people who were carried there are named in
[[artifact:relocations-qtc-016]] and some of them are still alive;
[[page:relocations-qausuittuq]] follows one of the two,
[[place:Qausuittuq]], from the beach in September 1953 to the present townsite.
Research relationships in the region are built, or not built, on that history;
the Commission's word for what would make them possible is the one at the top of
this page.

########## relocations-qausuittuq
# Qausuittuq (Resolute Bay): a community made in September 1953
[page · relocations-qtc · draft]

[[place:Qausuittuq]] means "the place with no dawn". The other Inuktitut name
for it is Qarnartakuj, "the place of the ruins", after the whalebone mounds of a
Thule site near the hamlet. Tuniit and Thule people lived on
[[place:Cornwallis Island]] on and off from about 1500 BCE until roughly a
thousand years ago, and then nobody did. No Inuit lived on or near the island
when the Government of Canada put four families ashore there in September 1953.
Everything else in this page follows from that.

The people of Qausuittuq call themselves Qausuitturmiut. This page follows the
Qikiqtani Truth Commission's community history of Resolute Bay
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-054]]), which is the fullest account there is, with
RCAP's 1994 report behind it where the relocation itself is concerned. The
companion community, [[place:Ausuittuq]] (Grise Fiord), gets a paragraph at the
end; the general story of the two relocations together is
[[page:relocations-the-1953-and-1955-high-arctic-relocations]].

## What was already there

An RCAF air photograph of about 1950 shows the bay and Griffith Island as the
government knew the place on paper ([[artifact:relocations-qtc-061]]). A joint
Canadian and American weather station went up on the south shore of Cornwallis
Island in 1947. It had been meant for Winter Harbour on Melville
Island, and ice sent the ship to Resolute Bay instead: the bay usually clears in
summer, so bulk cargo can be landed. An airstrip came with it, and in 1949 an
RCAF base three kilometres from the strip. The field turned out to be one of the
most useful in the North — usable all year, with a big paved apron — and it
supplied Alert, Isachsen and Mould Bay. Atlas Aviation, the first air company
headquartered in the Arctic islands, based itself there in 1962.

So the relocatees were not landed in an empty place. They were landed eight
kilometres from a military base that had not been told they were coming, and
which informed Northern Affairs that it would not be housing, feeding, clothing
or treating them. The base and the weather station were out of bounds to Inuit
unless they worked there; the settlement was out of bounds to Qallunaat; women
and children were in practice not to leave it. The RCMP constable was the
channel between the two. [[person:David Kalluk]] told the Commission what that
looked like from inside: "RCMP were the leaders back then. They seemed to be
higher than any other bosses because they were very scary."

The other thing already there was the game law. Inuit hunting had been
restricted since the Northwest Game Act of 1917 and the Arctic Islands Game
Preserve of 1926, and the relocatees, who had been recruited with promises of
plentiful game, arrived to find they were inside a preserve.

## September 1953

Three families from [[place:Inukjuak]] and one from [[place:Mittimatalik]]
(Pond Inlet) came ashore with their qimmiit and their possessions in early
September 1953, with Constable [[person:Ross Gibson]] and the kit for a
detachment and a small police store. No site had been picked. The only rule was
that they must not settle too near RCAF property, so the village went up on the
beach on the west side of Resolute Bay ([[artifact:relocations-qtc-055]]).

The QTC names them all ([[artifact:relocations-qtc-016]]):
[[person:Jaybeddie Amagoalik]] and Kanoinoo with Ekaksak, Merrari and Sippora,
from Mittimatalik; [[person:Daniel Salluviniq]] and Sarah with
[[person:Allie Salluviniq]], Louisa and Jeannie; [[person:Simeonie Amagoalik]]
and Sarah with [[person:Paul Amagoalik]], who had been born on the ship, his
brother Jaybeddie and his grandmother Nellie; and [[person:Alex Patsauq]] and
[[person:Edith Patsauq]] with [[person:Markoosie Patsauq]], Johnny, Jimmy and
Lizzie. The Mittimatalik family was included because Ottawa thought they could
teach the others a northern country.

Inukjuak is 2,250 km from Resolute — Toronto to Miami. Mittimatalik is 566 km
away. The families had come from a place with wood, a school, a church, a
trading post and a doctor once a year; they arrived in September, when the
walrus had already gone, the few caribou were protected, and the fish in the
lakes were full of parasites ([[artifact:relocations-qtc-014]]).
Then the sun went down. [[person:Dora Pudluk]]: "I found it scary . . . the dark
season frightened me. I came here when it was cold and dark. There wasn't really
a real home and it was starting to get dark at that time."

Nobody had told either group about the other, and their dialects were far apart
enough that the Tununirmiut took the Itivimiut to be speaking English
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-046]]). The Mittimatalik families had been told the
Inukjuak people were poor and living on relief, and expected to be paid for
teaching them; no payment came.

Gibson reported shortages of more than a thousand dollars' worth of goods, tents
in very poor condition, no boat but two one-man kayaks, no storage and no store,
and had to beg accommodation and cartage off the RCAF. Ottawa's answer came from [[person:James Cantley]], an advisor for the
relocation experiment ([[artifact:relocations-qtc-049]]): the families had been
living in those tents before, so conditions could not be worse than what they
had left.

In the first spring the community asked to go home. They were told there was
nothing to be done, and that if they were lonely, perhaps their relatives could
be persuaded to come too ([[artifact:relocations-qtc-015]]).

## 1955, and the years of building

In 1955 thirty-four more people were landed: [[person:Johnnie Echalook]]'s large
household, [[person:Levi Nungak]] and [[person:Alacee Nungaq]] with their four
children, [[person:Joseph Idlout]]'s family from Mittimatalik and
[[person:Horatio Kudloo]]'s. [[person:Markoosie Patsauq]], twelve years old,
described the arrival at the monument unveiling half a century later: "we had
arrived in a dead land . . . We were blind hunters"
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-057]]).

Materials for three houses came in 1955, late and damaged, with most of the food
spoiled. What could be salvaged became a store, a warehouse and a school the
following year. Everything else the community built itself out of the base and
weather-station dumps: a hall in the first autumn that served as workshop, drying
room, church and dark-season classroom; eleven houses along the beach by 1957,
each with a single electric light run from the base; fourteen by 1960; eighteen
by 1964. Gar Lunney photographed one of the 1955 households outside in March 1956 —
Alici Nungak, whom Library and Archives Canada's caption calls
[[person:Alacee Nungaq]], in an amauti with three of her children, the
scrap-built houses behind her ([[artifact:relocations-qtc-045]]).
[[person:Martha Flaherty]] told RCAP what the same years felt like:
"I remember the men being out for months . . . We were completely cut off from
the world for the first three or four years."

Not everyone stayed on the beach. Between 1955 and 1960 three or four families
lived at [[place:Kuvinajuq]], an ilagiit nunagivaktangat on Somerset Island,
some 300 km south across Barrow Strait, in old caribou country
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-056]]). It is the only camp away from the beach the
community history names, and nothing records its closing; it simply falls out
of the record after 1960, once there were houses, wages and a co-operative at
Resolute.

Wage work was the difference between Qausuittuq and every other community in
Qikiqtaaluk. There was no Hudson's Bay post — the police constable was the
trader — but there was a base. Four men had permanent jobs in 1960, nine by
1963; most others got seasonal work loading aircraft, and later with the Polar
Continental Shelf Project. Men were often paid in credits at the police store
rather than in money, and many say they worked without being paid at all. That
ended when the co-op was founded in November 1960. The base also brought
alcohol: [[person:Martha Idlout]] remembered that before the canteen was
restricted in 1961 "the whole town would be drunk for a whole week" when a
shipment came in.

Medicine came once a year on the C. D. Howe, which x-rayed everyone and decided
who would be taken south ([[page:relocations-the-ships-that-took-people-south]]).
In the first two years, [[person:John Amagoalik]] remembered, about five or six
adults and some children were taken aboard and sent south — among them his
mother and an older brother; his younger brother Jimmy was away two years, and
he was himself treated for two years at the Charles Camsell Hospital in Edmonton
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-053]]). The community was then about thirty-five
people. In
1957 the ship arrived with measles aboard, put the infected passengers ashore
near the settlement, and infected it.

## The qimmiit

The count tells it. In 1958 there were more than a hundred qimmiit at Qausuittuq
and four motor boats. By 1964 there were eighty qimmiit — seven of them lost to
rabies — fifteen motor boats and eleven snowmobiles. By 1966 the anthropologist
[[person:David Damas]] counted only one full-time hunter in the community,
though most of the men in wage work still hunted for food when they were not
working.

Resolute was built as an Inuit settlement, and no allowance was made for the fact
that hunting from a settlement means dogs in the settlement. The Ordinance
Respecting Dogs required qimmiit to be tied at all times. In 1959 the RCMP
reported that many were loose and that the Ordinance would have to be enforced,
without saying how; as the QTC observes, there was only one way to enforce it,
since a working team cannot be chained or muzzled. [[person:Dora Pudluk]] told
the Commission what enforcement was ([[artifact:relocations-qtc-047]]): her
husband's team shot because some were loose, and her own puppies, tied up, shot
with them and hung in the garbage while the men laughed. The wider pattern is
[[page:relocations-the-killing-of-the-qimmiit]].

## 1975, and after

By the early 1970s Resolute was three places: the Inuit settlement, the airport,
and South Camp between them, where the contractors, the Bay store and the school
were. Drifting snow, the airport approach and a wish to join the three led to a
plan for a new town; [[person:Ralph Erskine]] designed a community for seven
hundred behind a wall of apartment blocks, of which about one building was ever
built. In the summer of 1975 every useful building except those in the airport area
was hauled roughly three kilometres from the west shore of the bay to the east. The old site was left with
the cemetery. [[person:Saroomie Manik]] told the Commission she was sorry they
moved: the new town sits back from the water, so you cannot watch for animals
from the houses and gear has to be dragged overland to the boats
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-052]]).

The community that the government made turned out to be a political one. It sent
[[person:Ludy Pudluk]] to the Legislative Assembly of the Northwest Territories
for nearly twenty years; [[person:John Amagoalik]] went from here into the work
that produced Nunavut; [[person:Simeonie Amagoalik]] spoke for the community against the seismic
testing and mining near Resolute, and the political engagement of Qausuitturmiut
and of Inuit organizations delayed the opening of Cominco's Avarik (Polaris)
mine until 1980. In 1974
[[person:Simeonie Amarualik]], [[person:Walter Audla]] and
[[person:Peter Paniloo]] wrote to the minister, pointing out that a government
which had moved them here to hunt was now approving work that would drive the
caribou away, and setting down their own account of themselves
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-048]]): "we became a part of the community there.
Now Resolute Bay is our home and we do not want to leave it."

Canada paid for those who wanted to go back in 1988, and signed the $10 million
Memorandum of Agreement with Makivik in 1996 while refusing to apologise. The
apology came at Inukjuak on 18 August 2010 ([[artifact:relocations-qtc-013]]).
Three weeks later, on 8 September, [[person:Simeonie Amagoalik]] — landed here as
a boy in 1953 — unveiled the stone man he had carved for the community
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-051]]). Its plaque reads: "They came to these
desolate shores to pursue the government promise of a more prosperous life. They
endured and overcame great hardship, and dedicated their lives to Canada's
sovereignty in these lands and waters."

## Ausuittuq, the other half

The families who were not landed here were put ashore at
[[place:Craig Harbour]] on 29 August 1953 and moved shortly after to the
[[place:Lindstrom Peninsula]], and from there in 1961 to
[[place:Ausuittuq]] (Grise Fiord), Canada's northernmost civilian community. The
separation itself — the announcement on the deck of the C. D. Howe that half of
them would get off here — is [[artifact:relocations-qtc-001]]. Their first years
are in the same register as these: the flashlight passed from shelter to shelter
so people could eat ([[artifact:relocations-qtc-002]]), one caribou a year and a
$5,000 fine for a muskox ([[artifact:relocations-qtc-003]]), the dark
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-004]]), flour and water for a baby
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-005]]). [[person:Larry Audlaluk]], taken north as an
infant, has been the community's voice on it for decades
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-007]]). [[person:Looty Pijamini]]'s granite woman and
child stands there ([[artifact:relocations-qtc-041]]), facing the man at
Qausuittuq across a thousand kilometres.

## For the ship

The *Amundsen* works out of and around this place. Resolute is the transport and
logistics centre for the High Arctic and the home of the Polar Continental Shelf
Program base that outfits much of the field science done in these islands —
which is to say that the scientific station and the relocated community are the
same settlement, and have been since 1953. The base at Resolute was one reason
the site was chosen, and PCSP work was already one of the community's employers
by 1963. What that relationship looks like from the station's side is
[[page:the-station-and-the-settlement]] in [[topic:science-stations]].

Two practical points. First, the people named in this page are not distant: the
1953 and 1955 relocatees include people still living, and their children run the
hamlet, the co-op and the outfitting. Second, the old village site and the
cemetery on the west shore of the bay are three kilometres from the present
town and are not marked on most charts.
