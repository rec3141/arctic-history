In the summer of 1953 the Canadian government moved ten Inuit families — 54
people — from [[place:Inukjuak]] on Hudson Bay and [[place:Mittimatalik]] on
northern Qikiqtaaluk to two places on the High Arctic islands where no Inuit had
lived permanently for centuries. Six more families followed in 1955. The
settlements they were made to build are now [[place:Qausuittuq]] and
[[place:Ausuittuq]], the two northernmost civilian communities in Canada.

## Why the government did it

Ottawa gave several reasons at different times, and the two commissions that
examined the record disagreed with it about which mattered.

The stated reason was hunger. Fur prices had collapsed. At Inukjuak, where the
trading companies had spent decades competing with easy credit and high fox
prices, families had come to depend on store food bought against relief and the
family allowance; officials reported a shortage of game. But the QTC's Grise
Fiord history is careful here: "the problems they faced were more complicated
than that", and the government did not, in fact, choose the most self-reliant
families. "Evidence shows . . . that families who were the most dependent on
government assistance were more likely to be relocated."

The unstated reason was sovereignty. There was precedent: in 1934 the Hudson's
Bay Company, with the Department of the Interior's approval, had moved families
from [[place:Kinngait]] to a new post at [[place:Dundas Harbour]] on Devon
Island, and the government's own press release said why
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-010]]) — "the angle of occupation of the country,
now that aerial routes, mineral developments, and other reasons make possible
the claims of other countries to part of Canada's Arctic". The Dundas Harbour
families were unhappy, were moved on again to [[place:Ikpiarjuk]] and Fort
Ross, and asked for years to be taken home. They never were.

[[person:Alexander Stevenson]], who commanded the Eastern Arctic Patrol,
recommended a High Arctic move in 1950 and again in 1951.
[[person:Henry Larsen]] — the man who had taken the St. Roch through the
Northwest Passage, see [[topic:st-roch-modern]] — put a plan to the RCMP
Commissioner in February 1952.

## Consent, and the promise

Files call the relocatees "volunteers". The selection was made by individual
officers. The QTC's finding is blunt: "Inuit were never fully informed of
options or potential consequences", and testimony gathered by RCAP, the QIA and
the QTC "show that Inuit were afraid of the RCMP officers and felt pressured to
move." [[person:George Eckalook]] told the QIA that his parents "did not agree
to the move but later on they agreed but in their hearts, they were reluctant to
leave". [[person:Simeonie Amagoalik]] said: "It seemed like I had no other
choice but to say yes." This is the QTC's *ilira*: agreement without consent.

Because officials themselves doubted the plan, two undertakings were given.
Stevenson said that after two years anyone dissatisfied "could return to Port
Harrison". Larsen, remembering Dundas Harbour, wrote on 14 April 1953 that
"families will be brought home at the end of one year if they so desire"
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-011]]). Neither promise was kept.

## The voyage, and the splitting of the families

The relocatees boarded the C. D. Howe at Inukjuak in late July 1953. The ship
called at Mittimatalik in late August and took on three families whose High
Arctic experience was supposed to teach the Itivimiut how to live in the new
country; they expected to be paid for it and never were. At Craig Harbour the
C. D. Howe met the d'Iberville, and the families learned for the first time that
they were to be divided. [[person:John Amagoalik]], five or six years old:

> I remember we were all on the deck of the ship, the C. D. Howe, and all the
> women started to cry. And when women start to cry, the dogs join in. It was
> eerie.

([[artifact:relocations-qtc-001]].) One party went ashore at Craig Harbour at
the end of August. The d'Iberville tried to reach [[place:Cape Herschel]] on
eastern Ellesmere Island, where a third settlement was planned, could not get
through the ice, and returned in early September to land those families at Craig
Harbour too. RCAP notes drily that this was lucky: game did not appear at Cape
Herschel that year and the people could not have survived. The remainder went
ashore at Qausuittuq on 7 September 1953. Who went where, family by family, is
in [[artifact:relocations-qtc-016]].

## What they found

Nothing had been landed for them. Families had been told to bring only tents and
personal possessions. [[person:Samuel Arnakallak]] described the first night
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-002]]): a stone stove, no lamp, and one man's
flashlight passed from shelter to shelter so that people could eat.

The promised game was hedged about with law. [[person:Samwillie Elijasialak]]'s
parents were told they might take one caribou a year for the whole family, and
that killing a muskox meant a \$5,000 fine or arrest
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-003]]). At Qausuittuq the walrus had already
migrated when they landed in September, the caribou were protected, and the fish
in the lakes were full of parasites: "We didn't know any of this"
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-014]]). Fresh water at Ausuittuq had to be got from
sea ice, and it took a long time to learn to tell fresh ice from salt.

Two months after they landed, the sun went out. Neither group from Inukjuak had
seen three months of total darkness. [[person:Anna Nungaq]]: "I was so scared
and thought there would never be light again" ([[artifact:relocations-qtc-004]]).

The Craig Harbour party was moved almost at once to the
[[place:Lindstrom Peninsula]], 70 km from the police post — Constable
[[person:G. K. Sargent]] reported in December 1953 that the distance would keep
Inuit from becoming dependent on the RCMP. The camp had no Hudson's Bay Company
store. A small police-run store opened beside the detachment in 1954, kept by
[[person:Thomasie Amagoalik]] with the police doing the books, and moved to
Grise Fiord in 1956. It was badly supplied. [[person:Rynee Flaherty]], who came
in 1955, told RCAP how she had tried to feed her infant son on flour and water
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-005]]).

## Asking to go home

In the first spring people asked to leave. They were refused
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-008]]), and told instead to bring their relatives
north ([[artifact:relocations-qtc-015]]). [[person:Josephie Flaherty]] asked in
1959-60 and was told a school was coming and he had children to educate.
[[person:Samwillie Elijasialak]] recalled being told that a family wanting to
return would first have to find someone to take its place — "said by people
where no appeal was available to a higher authority"
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-009]]).

The two groups did not mix easily. The Mittimatalik families had been told the
Inukjuak people were poor and lived on relief; the Itivimiut did not think of
themselves that way. By 1958 the Inukjuak families at the Lindstrom Peninsula
had split off to a camp 3 km away. Constable [[person:Terry Jenkin]], posted in
1962, thought outside observers had made too much of the division.

Tuberculosis did the rest. [[person:Markoosie Patsauq]] told RCAP that he was
already infected when he was moved, and that others caught it from him. John
Amagoalik: "The first ten years in Resolute were the most terrible years of our
lives. We spent years without mothers, without fathers, without brothers,
without sisters, who were all sick in the hospital"
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-006]]). See
[[page:relocations-the-ships-that-took-people-south]].

## Redress

Relocatees began returning at their own expense in the 1970s and 1980s; in 1988
the government paid for a number of returns. Two unanimous reports of the
Commons Standing Committee on Aboriginal Affairs found the complaints
substantiated, and in March 1990 John Amagoalik, then president of the Inuit
Tapirisat of Canada, told the committee they had been at it for thirty years. In
1993 RCAP heard the relocatees; in July 1994 it published
[[source:rcap1994high|*The High Arctic Relocation*]]. In 1996 Canada signed a
memorandum of agreement with the Makivik Corporation acknowledging both the
relocatees' contribution to "a Canadian presence" in the High Arctic and the
"hardship, suffering and loss" of the early years, and paid \$10 million into a
trust — while refusing to apologise.

The apology was given at Inukjuak on 18 August 2010
([[artifact:relocations-qtc-013]]). It named the separation of families, the
inadequate shelter and supplies, the failure to say how far and how different
the new homes would be, and the broken promise of return.
