# The Isabella, and after
[page · ross-victory · draft]

At four in the morning on 26 August 1833, on a beach six or seven miles west of Navy Board
Inlet, the look-out [[person:David Wood]] thought he saw a sail. [[person:James Clark Ross]]
put a glass on it and confirmed it. Some of the party maintained it was an iceberg.

## The chase

They launched at six and burned wet powder for a signal. The wind was against them: the ship
made sail to the south-east and drew away, and a second sail to the north bore up under all
sail and left them too. Ross calls this the most anxious moment of the whole four years — to
be in sight of two ships, either of which would end everything, and to reach neither. Then it
fell calm. At eleven she hove to with all sails aback and lowered a boat.

The mate who came alongside opened by presuming they had lost their ship, which was true.
Ross asked her name.

> [[artifact:ross-victory-032|I was answered that it was "the Isabella of Hull, once commanded
> by Captain Ross;" on which I stated that I was the identical man in question, and my people
> the crew of the Victory... with the usual blunderheadedness of men on such occasions, he
> assured me that I had been dead two years.]]

The *Isabella* was the ship [[person:John Ross]] had commanded into Lancaster Sound in 1818,
on the voyage that ruined his reputation. She was now a Hull whaler under
[[person:Richard Humphreys]], who had taken her up Prince Regent Inlet as far as Mount Sherrar
looking for Ross rather than for whales, been stopped two-thirds of the way across by a field
of ice, and run along its edge the day before Ross crossed it. He had seen the three boats and
taken them for the *William Lee*'s. The rigging was manned and they were given three cheers as
they came within a cable's length.

## What they looked like

[[artifact:ross-victory-033|"never was seen a more miserable-looking set of wretches"]]. Ross's
description of coming aboard is worth reading whole: unshaven, filthy, in the rags of wild
beasts, starved to the bones, and made suddenly aware of it by the well-fed men around them.
Everything had to happen at once — washing, shaving, dressing, feeding, and four years of news
in both directions. That night, long used to a cold bed on snow or rock, most of them could
not sleep; Ross gave up the bed he had been given and passed the night in a chair.

Humphreys was two-thirds of a cargo short and stayed out. He put part of the crew aboard the
*William Lee*, and afterwards landed Ross at Possession Bay and on the west coast of Baffin
Bay so that he could finish his survey and check the chart he had made in 1818. They quitted
Davis Strait on 30 September, landed at [[place:Stromness]] on 12 October, reached the Humber
on the 18th and went up to Hull in the Rotterdam steam-boat. The news had got there first: the
Mayor and Corporation, Trinity House and the Philosophical Society all came to the inn, and
Ross was given the freedom of the town. He reached London on the 19th and Windsor on the 20th,
where he laid the magnetic pole flag before William IV.

## The reckoning

Twenty-three men went out and twenty came back. Three died:
[[person:James Marslin]] the armourer of consumption he had brought from England in January
1830; [[person:Chimham Thomas]] the carpenter of scurvy at Fury Beach in February 1833; and
the cook, Henry Ayre, soon after his return. The seaman Anthony Buck lost his sight and another
man a foot. Ross put it to the Admiralty in the arithmetic of the day:
[[artifact:ross-victory-039|"only one man can be said to have perished"]].

For four winters beset and a fifth ashore — the longest any beset crew has survived in the
Arctic — that is an extraordinary outcome, and the causes are not mysterious. Fresh country
food while they had access to it, a strict and fair ration under [[person:William Thom]], hard
work, a school, no spirits, dry and ventilated quarters, a competent surgeon in
[[person:George Macdiarmid]], and three years of trade with the Netsilingmiut. The one winter
without the last of these is the winter that killed a man.

Ross was knighted in 1834; Parliament voted him £5,000 and the crew were paid by Admiralty
order on 28 October 1833 — sums from £818 for the surgeon down to £36 for the dead armourer,
each entry annotated with what became of the man. [[person:Felix Booth]], who had paid for
everything and asked for nothing, was made a baronet.

## Huish, the hostile witness

Before Ross's own subscription volume could appear, [[person:Robert Huish]] rushed out
*The Last Voyage of Capt. Sir John Ross* (1835), compiled "from authentic information and
original documents, transmitted by an officer attached to the last expedition". Huish had
never been north of the Thames. The officer is generally taken to be
[[person:William Light]], the *Victory*'s steward, and the Admiralty's payment list marks
Light alone of the whole crew as "Not recommended".

The book is unreliable and it is not therefore useless. Read as an interested witness it is
the only source in the record that is not Ross:

- On the engine it is simply right, and says what Ross would not:
  [[artifact:ross-victory-037|the machine was untried and should never have gone]].
- On the ration it makes an accusation Ross's own narrative does not mention at all — that the
  cabin ate well while the men were put on a contracted allowance for no reason the stores
  required, and that the men made a formal complaint which was met with anger and then
  conceded ([[artifact:ross-victory-036]]).
- On the command it reprints the 1834 Commons committee evidence in which John Ross and James
  Clark Ross disagreed in public about whether the younger man had been second in command at
  all, the nephew saying he had "in a great measure" shared the chief command and had entire
  direction of the navigation.

None of that can be taken as fact on Huish's word. All of it marks places where a reader
should be careful with Ross's, and the ration charge in particular is the kind of thing a
steward would know and a captain would not write down. The full text of both books is on
disk: [[artifact:ross-victory-010]] and [[artifact:ross-victory-012]].

## Afterwards

James Clark Ross went on to command the *Erebus* and *Terror* in the Antarctic from 1839 to
1843, and led the first search for Franklin in 1848-49. [[person:Thomas Abernethy]], who was
at the magnetic pole, went south with him. [[person:Thomas Blanky]], the *Victory*'s first
mate, became ice master of HMS *Terror* and died with the Franklin expedition
([[topic:franklin-1845]]) — one of the very few men to have survived a four-year besetment and
then gone back.

The coast the *Victory* charted is why Franklin's ships went where they went. Ross and his
Netsilingmiut informants had shown that there was no way through Boothia below 71 degrees,
which closed the Admiralty's favourite route and pointed the 1845 expedition down the west
side of King William Land instead. Twenty-one years later, the people of that same coast were
the ones who told [[person:John Rae]] what had become of them ([[topic:franklin-search]]).
