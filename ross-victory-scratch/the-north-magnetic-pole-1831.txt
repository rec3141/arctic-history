# The North Magnetic Pole, 1 June 1831
[page · ross-victory · draft]

At eight in the morning on 1 June 1831, six men walked onto a low limestone shore on the west
side of [[place:Boothia Peninsula]] where the dipping needle stood at 89 degrees 59 minutes —
one minute from vertical — and no horizontal compass would move at all. It is the first time
anyone stood on a magnetic pole of the Earth. Of everything in this topic, it is the piece
that still bears directly on work being done from a ship today.

## Why anyone cared

A compass does not point north. It points along the horizontal component of the Earth's
magnetic field, and the difference between that and true north — the variation — changes with
place and with time. For a navigator in high latitudes it changes fast and becomes enormous,
and near the magnetic pole the horizontal component vanishes and the compass stops working
altogether. The *Victory*'s own compasses "ceased to traverse" whenever the ship had way on
her, from Prince Regent Inlet onward.

By the 1820s the variation had been mapped across the world's oceans well enough for
Professor Peter Barlow to draw the lines of equal variation converging on a point in the
Canadian Arctic that nobody had visited. Fixing that point by observation was a first-rank
scientific object, and it is why the Admiralty's own committee later concluded that this
expedition, whatever else, had performed a great public service.

## The journey

[[person:James Clark Ross]] had come within about ten miles of the calculated place in 1830
while surveying west, without the instruments to do anything about it, and spent the winter of
1830-31 at [[place:Sheriff's Harbour]] taking magnetic observations that gave him a better
position and a dip already above 89 degrees.

He left the ship at [[place:Victoria Harbour]] on 27 May 1831 with a party of six, John Ross
in company as far as the western sea. The
[[artifact:ross-victory-002|track of that journey]] is unusually well fixed, because he
printed his observed latitude and longitude at every halt:

| date | position | dip |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 28 May, 8 a.m. | 69° 34′ 45″ N, 94° 54′ 23″ W | 89° 41′ |
| 29 May | 69° 40′ 27″ N, 95° 22′ 35″ W | |
| 30 May, 8 a.m. | 69° 46′ 25″ N, 95° 49′ 11″ W | |
| 1 June, 8 a.m. | 70° 05′ 17″ N, 96° 46′ 45″ W | 89° 59′ |

Four of the party were snow-blind on the second day. On 31 May, fourteen miles short, they
left most of their baggage and provisions behind and made a rapid march.

## The place

[[artifact:ross-victory-029|"Nature had here erected no monument to denote the spot"]]. The
land was low near the coast and rose to ridges of fifty or sixty feet about a mile inland.
There were Inuit snow houses on it, not long abandoned; the party moved into them, and camped
half a mile west. Ross was drily aware of the comedy: nobody had expected a mountain of iron,
but they had hoped for something.

The observations ran through that day and most of the next. Then
[[artifact:ross-victory-027|"we fixed the British flag on the spot, and took possession of the
North Magnetic Pole and its adjoining territory, in the name of Great Britain and King William
the Fourth"]], and built a limestone cairn over a canister holding a record. Neither cairn nor
canister has ever been found. The flag was laid before the King at Windsor on 20 October 1833,
and the pole was named after him.

The scientific paper went to the Royal Society; the details are in the
[[artifact:ross-victory-011|appendix volume]], not the narrative. Gratifyingly, the place
Ross had reached was within a few miles of where Barlow's curves said it should be.

## The honest sentence

The part of this that deserves to be read out in a wardroom is what James Clark Ross wrote
next. He had every incentive to leave the flag-planting as the last word. Instead:

> as far as our instruments can be trusted, we had placed ourselves within one minute of the
> magnetic pole, but had not fixed on the precise spot... if popular conversation gives to
> this voyage the credit of having placed its flag on the very point, on the summit of that
> mysterious pole which it perhaps views as a visible and tangible reality, it can now correct
> itself as it may please.

He goes on to say what a real determination would need: several observers, at different
distances and in different directions from the calculated place, working together. That is
precisely the method by which the magnetic pole is tracked now.
([[artifact:ross-victory-028|the passage]])

## Where the pole is now

It has not stayed. From James Clark Ross's 1831 position on the west coast of Boothia the
north magnetic pole has travelled well over a thousand kilometres to the north-north-west,
out of the Canadian Arctic islands and across the Arctic Ocean toward Siberia, and it has
been moving faster over the last few decades than at any time in the record. The 1831 fix is
the anchor point of that whole track: everything since is measured back to a party of six men
with a dip circle and a limestone cairn.

That is also why the position matters to a ship working here. The magnetic declination in the
Canadian Arctic Archipelago is large, changes quickly, and is the reason no one navigates
these channels by magnetic compass. The World Magnetic Model that a modern instrument corrects
against is the descendant of the observations James Clark Ross made in a snow house on
1 June 1831.

## A note on the other Ross

John Ross published the discovery in his own book and, in a passage that has been held against
him ever since, took some pains to say that it belonged to the ship and to the man who paid
for her: "it was the ship Victory, under the command of Captain John Ross, which assigned the
north-west Magnetic Pole, in the year 1831, and that this vessel was fitted out by him whom I
can now call Sir [[person:Felix Booth]]". Before the Commons committee in 1834 the two Rosses
disagreed openly about who had commanded the expedition at all —
[[person:Robert Huish]] reprints the exchange with relish
([[page:the-isabella-and-after]]). The needle was James Clark Ross's; the four winters that
put it there were both of theirs.
