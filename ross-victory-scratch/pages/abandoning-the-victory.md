# Abandoning the Victory: the retreat to Fury Beach

By the autumn of 1831 the arithmetic was finished. The *Victory* had gained four miles in
1830 and fourteen in 1831; she had not doubled the cape that would let her out; the
provisions ran to June 1832. Nobody was going to sail her home. What follows is the part of
the story that is a survival problem rather than an exploring one.

## Carrying the road forward

You cannot walk 200 miles of broken Arctic coast in one lift with two boats, six weeks of
food, sick men and a lame mate. You do it in relays: carry a load forward, walk back empty,
carry the next. April and May 1832 went in this. By 21 May, [[person:John Ross]] calculated,
they had walked 329 miles to gain about thirty in a straight line, and used up a month doing
it. Two men were snow-blind; from 15 May they turned day into night to spare their eyes.

## 29 May 1832

The instruments and powder that could not be carried were cached ashore, for a possible return
and otherwise for the Netsilingmiut. The masts, sails and rigging were left with the boat
*Krusenstern*. Then:

> [[artifact:ross-victory-030|The colours were therefore hoisted and nailed to the mast, we
> drank a parting glass to our poor ship, and having seen every man out, in the evening, I
> took my own adieu of the Victory, which had deserved a better fate.]]

Ross had served in thirty-six ships in forty-two years and never abandoned one. He stopped at
the point where she went out of sight to make a sketch. The *Victory* has never been found;
she lies, if anything of her is left, in or near [[place:Victoria Harbour]] on the east coast
of Boothia, and Ross's own surveyed plan of that harbour marks her berth in about ten fathoms
([[artifact:ross-victory-080]]). The same plan letters the grave of
[[person:James Dixon]], the stoker who had died there in January. She would be one of the more
findable wrecks in the Arctic, and nobody has looked hard
([[artifact:ross-victory-054]]).

## To Fury Beach

A month and three days on the ice and the shore, round the head of every bay, the direct 200
miles becoming nearly 300. They reached [[place:Fury Beach]] on 1 July 1832 — Ross's word is
"completely exhausted by hunger and fatigue". Three of the *Fury*'s boats had been washed off
the beach and driven ashore again; they were repaired that month. Out of her spars and canvas
the party built a hut 32 feet by 16 and, with the flat naval humour of the day, called it
[[place:Somerset House]] after the Admiralty's own building in London
([[artifact:ross-victory-050]]).

This is the second time [[topic:ross-parry]]'s wreck saved them, and the reason the loss of
the *Fury* in 1825 is one of the most consequential accidents in Arctic history. Parry landed
those stores expecting to come back for them; instead they kept twenty-one men alive for a
year, and they were still there for later searchers.

## The escape that failed

On 1 August 1832 they got away in three boats up the east coast of Somerset Island. On
1 September they were at 73° 56′ N and 90° W, on what Ross insisted was the north-east point
of the American continent and not, as others called it, one of Leopold's islands. He climbed
the promontory. Prince Regent Inlet, Barrow Strait and Lancaster Sound were one unbroken sheet
— "just as I had seen it in 1818". They waited. Nothing opened. With the provisions going and
the winter coming, they left the boats at [[place:Batty Bay]] and walked back to Fury Beach,
arriving 7 October.

## The winter in the iceberg

In November they banked the hut to the roof with four to seven feet of snow and poured water
over it at fifteen degrees below zero, so that the whole thing set solid:
[[artifact:ross-victory-031|"we actually became the inhabitants of an iceberg"]]. There was
not enough bedding, not enough clothing, and almost no fresh meat — this was the one winter
of the four with no Netsilingmiut trade, and it is the one winter with scurvy in it.

Christmas 1832 was the first the party had spent without wine or spirits; a half dozen foxes
made the meal. By then the carpenter [[person:Chimham Thomas]] was the only man on the sick
list, and the lime juice was not touching his scurvy. A sermon was read for him on Sunday
10 February 1833; he died on the Saturday morning, 16 February, and was buried on the 22nd,
the ground so hard that the grave was made with great difficulty and the thermometer at 45
below. Ross: "It was the first of our losses, however, which could, in any fairness, be
attributed to the climate and our peculiar situation."

Three others were reduced, in Ross's phrase, to the last stage of debility, and one man had
lost a foot. Twenty men were now left of the twenty-three who had sailed, and only thirteen of
them were fit enough to carry provisions forward, which they did in seven journeys of
sixty-two miles each to Batty Bay.

## Out

They left Somerset House on 8 July 1833, carrying three men who could not walk, and reached
the boats in six days. Then a month of watching ice from a beach. On 14 August a lane of water
opened north for the first time and, Ross wrote, few slept. At four the next morning they were
cutting the shore ice; at eight they were under way.

What had defeated them for four summers took eleven days. On 17 August a southerly breeze let
them round the pack and cross Prince Regent Inlet in a single day — seventy-two miles — and
Ross found the sentence for it: "it was a change like that of magic, to find that solid mass
of ocean... suddenly converted into water". Then Admiralty Inlet, six days pinned down by a
north-easter on two-thirds allowance, Navy Board Inlet on the 25th, and on the morning of the
26th a sail.

The rest is [[page:the-isabella-and-after]].
