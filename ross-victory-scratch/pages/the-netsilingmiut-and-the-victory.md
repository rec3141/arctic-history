# The Netsilingmiut and the Victory

The people who lived on the Boothia coast when the *Victory* arrived were Netsilingmiut,
the people of the seal, whose descendants live at Taloyoak (Spence Bay), Kugaaruk (Pelly
Bay) and [[place:Uqsuqtuuq]] (Gjoa Haven). In 1830 they had never met Europeans.
[[person:John Ross]] put their number in the district at about a hundred and ten. Everything
the expedition achieved after January 1830 — the geography, the magnetic pole, and most of the
survival — came through them.

## 9 January 1830

A seaman saw strangers from the observatory at [[place:Felix Harbour]]. Ross walked out, saw
ten men in a line three deep with one more sitting on a sledge, and went forward alone. He
knew one word of the language and used it:
[[artifact:ross-victory-023|"Knowing that the word of salutation between meeting tribes was
Tima tima, I hailed them in their own language, and was answered by a general shout of the
same kind."]] Both sides threw their weapons on the snow. Within two days the ship was a
place people came to. The plate Ross engraved of that morning is
[[artifact:ross-victory-066]].

## The chart

On 11 January [[person:Tulluahiu]] took a pencil and drew the route his family had travelled,
marking the places they had slept and counting on his fingers that there had been nine of
them. [[person:Tiagashu]] then drew the coast running west, with its capes, bays and rivers.
The next day the man Ross had been promised as a hydrographer came:
[[person:Ikmallik]], with [[person:Apelagliu]]. Shown a European sketch of the coast between
Repulse Bay and Prince Regent Inlet, he recognised the named places at once, took the pencil,
and carried the line on westward into country no chart had.

Ross engraved the result in his book under the flat title *Chart drawn by the natives*
([[artifact:ross-victory-069]]), with the scene of its making on the facing plate:
Ikmallik and Apelagliu at the cabin table among the officers
([[artifact:ross-victory-068]]). The chart is also entered as
[[artifact:inuit-oral-history-002]], and the fuller account of it, with Iligliuk's chart of
1822 and Boas's cartographers of the 1880s, is at
[[page:inuit-drawn-maps]] under [[topic:inuit-oral-history]].

What the chart told Ross was the thing he least wanted to hear and most needed to know: that
the land he was on ran on to the south and joined the continent, and that the two seas were
divided by a neck of land. He reported it to the Admiralty in exactly those terms —
[[artifact:ross-victory-038|"from them we gradually obtained the important information"]] —
and then sent his nephew to check it. [[person:Awack]] and [[person:Ooblooria]] guided that
journey. It settled that there was no passage below 71 degrees, which is a real geographical
result, arrived at by asking people who knew.

## The wooden leg

[[person:Tulluahiu]] had lost a leg to a bear. On 11 January 1830 [[person:Tiagashu]] hauled
him to the ship on a sledge; the surgeon [[person:George Macdiarmid]] found the stump long
healed and the knee sound, and the carpenter [[person:Chimham Thomas]] measured him. The leg
was fitted on 14 January at minus 38 degrees. Thomas then made a second, broader foot for
walking on snow. Within a fortnight Ross could record the only fact of the month he thought
worth stating plainly:
[[artifact:ross-victory-024|"the wooden leg had enabled our friend to attend the seal hunt"]].
At [[place:Sheriff's Harbour]] in 1831 the leg came back for repair; it was banded with
copper and spare legs were sent away with him.

The leg can still be seen. Ross's plate of Tulluahiu standing between his daughter
[[person:Shullanina]] and his wife [[person:Tirikshiu]] — the plate the binder's list calls
*Tulluachiu and Family* — shows the shaft lettered VICTORY:
[[artifact:ross-victory-070]]. Where the leg itself went, nobody knows; nothing of it has
been reported from Boothia.

It is a good story and it is usually told as the whole of the exchange, which it is not. The
leg was made by a man who would die of scurvy on that coast three years later, in a ship kept
in fresh meat by the people he made it for. And the appendix records, as a curiosity, that
Shullanina gave proof of her obedience "by consenting to be exchanged for a wooden leg" —
a bargain struck over her between her father and a suitor. The record preserves that from the
ship's side only.

## The partnership, plainly

Through 1830 and 1831 the Netsilingmiut sold and gave the ship seal, salmon, caribou and musk
ox, and sold skin clothing and boots that were better than anything the expedition had
brought. They sold dogs and sold food for dogs. They built the sledge parties' snow houses —
[[person:James Clark Ross]], when they were no longer available in April 1831, wrote that he
regretted losing "the aid of the Esquimaux, who had formerly been of such essential service to
us, by means of their sledges and dogs, and by the great ease and expedition with which they
raised our temporary encampments". They guided every journey that mattered, including the ones
that found the isthmus and the ones that led toward the magnetic pole. When they promised to
wait at a place in summer with salmon and reindeer, they waited; it was the ship that could
not come.

The names the record preserves, besides those above:
Shullanina and her mother Tirikshiu, [[person:Neweetioke]] who was
Ikmallik's brother, [[person:Poo-yet-tah]] who hunted with the sledge parties, and
[[person:Pow-weet-yah]]. The appendix to Ross's narrative carries a *Sketch of the Esquimaux
found in the Territory of Boothia Felix* with individual descriptions and portraits and an
English-Danish-Esquimaux vocabulary: [[artifact:ross-victory-011]]. It is one of the earliest
ethnographic records of the Netsilingmiut, made a century before
[[source:rasmussen1931netsilik|Rasmussen's Fifth Thule report]], and it should be read
alongside it.

## The quarrel

Once, in April 1830, it nearly went wrong. An adopted son of Pow-weet-yah, a boy of
seven or eight the crew knew, was killed in the night by a falling stone. The death was put
down to the ship's people, who were understood to have powers. Pow-weet-yah raised his weapon
at James Clark Ross and the surgeon from a few yards; his own two sons pinned his arms. The
party was nearly surrounded, and Ross had the gun up when
[[artifact:ross-victory-040|a woman walked out of a hut unarmed and called to him not to fire]].
She explained what had happened, carried Ross's answer back, and it was settled the same day
with a line drawn in the snow and the knives put away.

Nobody was hurt. Both sides understood at once what a killing would cost them: Ross reasoned
that one quarrel would make "the whole land our enemies", and the Netsilingmiut offered to
guide him again three days later. It is the only violence in four years of contact, and the
person who prevented it is not named in the book.

## What Ross wrote about them

Ross is not a reliable narrator of his own dependence. In August 1831, in the middle of the
third summer, he wrote a passage about Netsilingmiut visits as a relief from boredom that
calls them "greasy gormandizing specimens of humanity" and asks whether it is any wonder
"that even the visits of barbarians were welcome". It is entered here in full and unsoftened:
[[artifact:ross-victory-035]].

The same book contains the sentence about the wooden leg and the seal hunt, and the paragraph
in which Ross works out from his own observation that the reason the people of these regions
survive the cold is that they eat enormously and well — a conclusion he then failed to act
on, with a carpenter dead of scurvy at the end of it. Both are Ross. The record is the
record; readers in Nunavut have long been used to reading past this sort of thing to the
facts underneath, and the facts underneath are that a British crew survived four winters in
the ice on a coast whose people chose, for three years, to keep feeding them, clothing them,
and telling them where they were.
