# The North Magnetic Pole, 1 June 1831

At eight in the morning on 1 June 1831 a sledge party walked onto a low limestone shore on
the west side of [[place:Boothia Peninsula]] where the dipping needle stood at 89° 59′ —
one minute from vertical — and no horizontal compass would move at all. It is the first time
a magnetic pole of the Earth was fixed by observation. Of everything in this topic, it is the
piece that still bears directly on work being done from a ship today.

## Why anyone cared

A compass does not point north. It points along the horizontal component of the Earth's
magnetic field, and the difference between that and true north — the variation — changes with
place and with time. For a navigator in high latitudes it changes fast and becomes enormous,
and near the magnetic pole the horizontal component vanishes and the compass stops working
altogether. The *Victory*'s own compasses "ceased to traverse" whenever the ship had way on
her, from Prince Regent Inlet onward.

When the expedition left England the pole was reckoned to lie in latitude 70° N and longitude
98° 30′ W — a figure derived from the variation observed on earlier voyages, and the target
the *Victory* sailed with. Fixing that point by observation was a first-rank scientific object,
and it is why the Admiralty's own committee later concluded that this expedition, whatever
else, had performed a great public service.

## The journey

[[person:James Clark Ross]] had come within about ten miles of the calculated place in 1830
while surveying west near Cape Felix, without the instruments to do anything about it. He
spent the winter of 1830-31 at [[place:Sheriff's Harbour]] taking magnetic observations that
gave him a better position and a dip already above 89 degrees; his mean dip there was
88° 57′·04, at 70° 01′ N, 91° 54′ W, with the variation 96° 12′·3 W.

He left the ship at Sheriff's Harbour on 27 May 1831 — not at [[place:Victoria Harbour]],
which the *Victory* did not reach until the end of that August — with a sledge party under
the mates [[person:Thomas Blanky]] and [[person:Thomas Abernethy]], John Ross in company as
far as the western sea. The [[artifact:ross-victory-002|track of that journey]] is unusually
well fixed, because he printed his observed latitude and longitude at every halt:

| date | observed position | dip |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 28 May, 8 a.m. | 69° 34′ 45″ N, 94° 54′ 23″ W | 89° 41′; needle N 57° W |
| 29 May | 69° 40′ 27″ N, 95° 22′ 35″ W | |
| 30 May, 8 a.m. | 69° 46′ 25″ N, 95° 49′ 11″ W | |
| 1 June, 8 a.m. | 70° 05′ 17″ N, 96° 46′ 45″ W | 89° 59′ |

Those are the figures in James Clark Ross's own narrative of the journey, and they are the
ones used for every position in this topic. The summary magnetic table printed a few pages
later in the same volume gives the pole's longitude as 96° 45′ 48″ W and the 28 May longitude
as 94° 53′ W. The differences are small — about half a mile at the pole — but they are real,
and anyone recomputing from the printed record should know that the book disagrees with
itself by that much.

Four of the party were blinded by snow on the second day, which is why they halted after only
ten miles. On 31 May, encamped fourteen miles short of the calculated position, they left the
greater part of their baggage and provisions behind and made a rapid march.

## The place

[[artifact:ross-victory-029|"Nature had here erected no monument to denote the spot"]]. The
land was low near the coast and rose to ridges of fifty or sixty feet about a mile inland.
There were Inuit snow houses on it, not long abandoned; the party took possession of them and
set up the observations there, and encamped on a point of land about half a mile to the
westward. Ross was drily aware of the comedy: nobody had seriously expected a mountain of
iron, but they had hoped for something.

The observations ran through that day and most of the next. Then
[[artifact:ross-victory-027|"we fixed the British flag on the spot, and took possession of the
North Magnetic Pole and its adjoining territory, in the name of Great Britain and King William
the Fourth"]], and they built a cairn of the limestone fragments that covered the beach over a
canister holding a record ([[artifact:ross-victory-053]]). Neither cairn nor canister has ever
been reported found. The flag was laid before the King at Windsor on 20 October 1833, and the
pole was named after him.

The full observations went to the Royal Society and into the
[[artifact:ross-victory-011|appendix volume]], not the narrative. When the party got home they
learned that during their absence Professor Peter Barlow had laid down the curves of equal
variation to within a few degrees of the point where they converge, leaving that point to be
determined by observation if any navigator ever could; and the place James Clark Ross had
examined was, in his own words, precisely the one where those curves met.

## The honest sentence

The part of this that deserves to be read out in a wardroom is what James Clark Ross wrote
next. He had every incentive to leave the flag-planting as the last word. Instead:

> as far as our instruments can be trusted, we had placed ourselves within one minute of the
> magnetic pole, but had not fixed on the precise spot... if popular conversation gives to
> this voyage the credit of having placed its flag on the very point, on the summit of that
> mysterious pole which it perhaps views as a visible and tangible reality, it can now correct
> itself as it may please.

He goes on to say what a real determination would need: several observers, at different
distances and in different directions from the calculated place, working together. That is
precisely the method by which the magnetic pole is tracked now.
([[artifact:ross-victory-028|the passage]])

## Where the pole is now

It has not stayed. From James Clark Ross's 1831 position on the west coast of Boothia the
north magnetic pole has travelled well over a thousand kilometres to the north-north-west,
out of the Canadian Arctic islands and across the Arctic Ocean toward Siberia, and it has
been moving faster over the last few decades than at any time in the record. The 1831 fix is
the anchor point of that whole track: everything since is measured back to a sledge party
with a dip circle and a limestone cairn.

That is also why the position matters to a ship working here. The magnetic declination in the
Canadian Arctic Archipelago is large, changes quickly, and is the reason no one navigates
these channels by magnetic compass. The World Magnetic Model that a modern instrument corrects
against is the descendant of the observations James Clark Ross made in a Netsilingmiut snow
house on 1 June 1831.

## A note on the other Ross

John Ross published the result in his own book and, in a passage that has been held against
him ever since, took some pains to say that the credit belonged to the ship and to the man who
paid for her: "it was the ship Victory, under the command of Captain John Ross, which assigned
the north-west Magnetic Pole, in the year 1831, and that this vessel was fitted out by him
whom I can now call Sir [[person:Felix Booth]]". Before the Commons committee in 1834 the two
Rosses disagreed openly about who had commanded the expedition at all: asked whether he had
gone out as second in command, the nephew replied that he had not gone out precisely as
second in command, but "in a great measure sharing with Capt. Ross the chief command, that is
to say, he had the entire direction of the navigation of the ship".
[[person:Robert Huish]] reprints the exchange with relish
([[page:the-isabella-and-after]]). The needle was James Clark Ross's; the winters that put it
there were both of theirs.
