# Four winters, three of them in the ship

Parry had wintered twice in a row, at Winter Island and Iglulik ([[topic:ross-parry]]).
[[person:John Ross]] wintered four times: three of them in the ship on the east coast of
Boothia, and a fourth in a hut of salvaged spars at [[place:Fury Beach]], on a ration meant
for a thousand days. The ship winters are the part of the story with the least happening in
it and the most to learn from.

## The three berths

**[[place:Felix Harbour]], 1 October 1829 to 17 September 1830.** Eleven and a half months
without the ship moving. The barrier of ice that stopped her on 1 October never opened; she
got out on 17 September 1830 and made three miles.
[[artifact:ross-victory-025|Ross on the moment she moved]] is the best thing in the book about
what a ship is to a seaman. Ross settled the harbour's position at 69° 58′ 42″ N,
92° 01′ 06″ W, and set it in a small table beside the four earlier British winterings —
Melville Island 1819, Winter Island 1821, Iglulik 1822, Port Bowen 1824 — to show that his was
the coldest October of the five ([[artifact:ross-victory-063]]).

**[[place:Sheriff's Harbour]], 31 October 1830 to 29 August 1831.** The summer of 1830 did not
happen. Through September and October the crew cut, warped and hove the ship a few dozen feet
a day toward a berth she never reached: 850 feet for the whole of October, with two hundred
yards still to go and the ice sixteen feet thick. On 31 October Ross stopped it. Four miles
was the entire gain of 1830. (His dispatch to the Admiralty of 1833 says the ship was cut in
"about the middle of November"; the journal, followed here, says the cutting was given up on
31 October. The two have never been reconciled.) James Clark Ross's mean magnetic position for
the berth is 70° 01′ N, 91° 54′ W, and it was from here that he went to the magnetic pole.

**[[place:Victoria Harbour]], reached 29 August 1831, abandoned 29 May 1832.** Fourteen miles
in 1831, and the last four of them the last the ship ever sailed. By 15 and 16 October the
anchors were ashore and the boats upside down on the ice, and, Ross wrote, "it was very
certain that we had now settled ourselves for the winter". They knew by then that the
provisions ran to June 1832, and that they were going to have to walk. Ross's surveyed plan of
the harbour ([[artifact:ross-victory-080]]) is the most exact record of the place there is;
it marks the ship's berth, where the stores were landed, and a grave.

## Making a ship into a house

The routine Ross set was the standard of the day, done thoroughly. The ship was unrigged and
roofed over, the guns and the engine landed to give room, a powder magazine built on shore.
The lower deck was warmed and, more importantly, ventilated — the enemy in a wintering ship
is not cold but the condensation that soaks bedding and clothing. Snow was banked round the
hull each November.

The day: cocoa or tea at breakfast, dinner at noon, work outside until three or four when the
weather allowed and walking under the roof when it did not, tea at five, evening school from
six to nine, hammocks and bed at ten. On Sunday, muster in best clothes, prayers and a sermon,
a collection of tracts presented by a Mrs Enderby of Blackheath, and a Sunday school at six.

The school was real. From 19 November 1829 it ran classes in reading, writing, arithmetic,
mathematics and navigation. Out of the eighteen men, three had not learned to read and write;
the want of arithmetic was general; the three mates were started on astronomy and navigation.
Attendance was voluntary and everybody came.

Spirits were first restricted and then abolished altogether. Ross thought this as important
as anything he did, and connected it directly to scurvy. Christmas 1832 at
[[place:Somerset House]] was the first the party had spent with neither wine nor spirits, by
which time there were none left.

## What kept them alive, and what did not

Ross's own account of it is worth reading against what actually happened. He believed the
crucial things were fresh food, exercise, occupation, ventilation, no spirits, and never
letting men brood. He was right, and he says so in a passage that is well ahead of the naval
medicine of his day: he had noticed that men differ in the rate at which they make heat, that
the ones with the largest appetites stand the cold best, and that "he who is well-fed resists
cold better than the man who is stinted; while the starvation from cold follows but too soon
a starvation in food". He drew the conclusion out loud —

> This, doubtless, explains in a great measure, the resisting powers of the natives of these
> frozen climates: their consumption of food, it is familiar, being enormous, and often
> incredible.

The ration itself is printed in the book, item by item, for eighteen men over a fortnight
([[artifact:ross-victory-064]]): bread, flour, suet, raisins, sugar, cocoa, tea, rice,
preserved meats, salt beef and salt pork, pickled cabbage and onions, spirits twice a
fortnight — and lemon juice every single day, a pound and an eighth for the whole mess. It did
not prevent the scurvy when it came.

The fresh food came from the country and mostly from the Netsilingmiut: seal, salmon, caribou,
musk ox, hares, ptarmigan, foxes. Ross's own fishing expedition of June 1830 was mounted for
the purpose. As long as they were on the Boothia coast with people trading with them, there
was no scurvy: at the end of August 1831, after two winters, Ross could write that although
"we had no men absolutely sick, and there had been no scurvy", the general health of the crew
was not what it had been, and they had proved incapable of hard travelling.

Scurvy arrived in the fourth winter at Fury Beach, when they were living out of the *Fury*'s
tinned and salted stores with almost no fresh meat, and it killed the carpenter
[[person:Chimham Thomas]]. Lime juice did not save him. The difference between the winters
that worked and the winter that did not is the difference between a coast with people on it
and a beach with a store cupboard.

## The cost of nothing happening

The other thing these winters record is boredom, described more honestly than most explorers
allow themselves. Ross fills pages with it: the sameness, the want of books, the want of
society, the wearisome iteration of thermometers and winds and ice. In September 1831 he wrote
the long tirade against snow that ends
[[artifact:ross-victory-026|"our coffins and our graves were to be graves and coffins of
snow"]]. A few weeks earlier, in August 1831, he wrote a passage about the visits of the
Netsilingmiut as a relief from his own company that is one of the ugliest things in the book,
and it is entered here unedited: [[artifact:ross-victory-035]]. Both passages are the same man
in the same summer. It is useful to see what four years of this does to a person's judgement,
including his judgement of other people.

## The two men who died aboard

[[person:James Marslin]], the armourer, died at Felix Harbour on 20 January 1830 of a
tuberculosis he had brought from England; he had been in hospital for it before the ship
sailed, and Ross wrote that the long certainty of it had limited their regrets. His grave was
dug on the day the carpenter was finishing the snow foot for [[person:Tulluahiu]]'s wooden leg.

[[person:James Dixon]], the stoker, died at Victoria Harbour on 10 January 1832. He had shipped
to feed an engine that was landed on a beach within five months of sailing and had been rated
landman ever since; he was failing through December with what Ross calls a complication of
disorders. The men who had been driving a tunnel through the snow cut his grave, the weather
put the funeral off, a sermon was read for him on Sunday the 15th and he was buried on the
morning of the 16th. His is the only grave of the expedition that anyone can point to: Ross
lettered *J. Dixon's grave* on the west shore of his own chart of the harbour, a few hundred
yards from where the *Victory* was left.

Two deaths in three ship winters, one of them a man who was already ill when he sailed, is a
remarkable record for the period, and Ross gave the credit for it to [[person:William Thom]]'s
management of the provisions.
