# John Ross's Victory, 1829-1833

John Ross went north in 1829 in a paddle steamer bought with a gin distiller's money, because
the Admiralty would not send him. He came back in 1833, four years and five months later,
having lost his ship, three of his twenty-three men, and every possession any of them had.
Four winters in the ice is among the longest any expedition has endured and brought its people
home. The reason they survived is not in the ship's fittings or the stores, though both
mattered. It is that a few dozen
Netsilingmiut families on the Boothia coast decided to keep talking to them.

## The plan and the money

Ross had been in disgrace since 1818, when he turned the Isabella back out of Lancaster
Sound because he had seen mountains across it that were not there (see
[[topic:ross-parry]]). Two proposals to the Admiralty, in 1827 and 1828, were refused. While
the parliamentary reward of £20,000 for a north-west passage still stood, [[person:Felix Booth]],
distiller and Sheriff of London, would not touch the scheme, because backing it would look
like "a mere mercantile speculation"; once the reward was repealed that objection was gone,
and he agreed to pay for the whole thing on condition that his name be kept out of it. The
Commons committee that afterwards examined the accounts put his share at between seventeen
and eighteen thousand pounds, with about two thousand more spent by Ross himself. Booth's
name is now on half the map of that coast: [[place:Boothia Peninsula]], the Gulf of Boothia,
[[place:Felix Harbour]], [[place:Sheriff's Harbour]].

Ross bought the *Victory*, an 85-ton Liverpool-to-Isle-of-Man packet, had her raised five
and a half feet, and put in a high-pressure engine by Braithwaite and Ericsson with paddle
wheels that could be hoisted clear of the water in a minute. It was the first steam vessel
taken into the Arctic. It was a disaster, and a page here is given to it:
[[page:ross-victory-the-machinery]].

## What happened, in order

She left Woolwich on 23 May 1829 with twenty paid hands and three officers serving without
pay — Ross, his nephew [[person:James Clark Ross]], and the purser [[person:William Thom]] —
twenty-three in all. On 12 August she moored at [[place:Fury Beach]], where the stores of
Parry's wrecked *Fury* had lain since 1825 (see [[page:the-loss-of-the-fury]]). Ross's own
dispatch to the Admiralty dates that week a day later throughout than his journal does, and
the disagreement has never been resolved; the journal is followed here. Four years of Arctic
weather had not touched the tinned meat — [[artifact:ross-victory-034|"not our luxury but
our very existence"]] — and the *Victory* took away enough for two years and three months.

South of Cape Garry she was in water no European had charted. On 1 October 1829 the ice
stopped her at the head of the Gulf of Boothia and she never sailed free of that coast again.
The [[artifact:ross-victory-001|track]] tells the story better than a sentence can: in 1830
the ship moved four miles, in 1831 fourteen, in 1832 not at all. Three winters aboard, at
Felix Harbour, Sheriff's Harbour and [[place:Victoria Harbour]]; then, on 29 May 1832, the
colours nailed to the mast and everyone walking north.

They dragged boats 200 miles of coast that became nearly 300 and reached Fury Beach on
1 July 1832. A boat escape in August got as far as 73° 56′ N at the mouth of Prince Regent Inlet,
where Prince Regent Inlet, Barrow Strait and Lancaster Sound lay before them as one
unbroken sheet of ice; they walked back and spent a fourth winter in a hut whose roof
they flooded so that it froze solid — [[artifact:ross-victory-031|"we actually became the
inhabitants of an iceberg"]]. The carpenter [[person:Chimham Thomas]] died there of scurvy in
February 1833. On 26 August 1833, off Navy Board Inlet, they were picked up by a whaler that
turned out to be the *Isabella* of Hull — the ship Ross himself had commanded in 1818. The
mate told him he had been dead two years.

## The magnetic pole

On 1 June 1831 James Clark Ross, with a sledge party and a dipping needle, stood on a low
limestone shore on the west side of Boothia where the needle read 89 degrees 59 minutes and
not one horizontal compass would move at all. It is the first time a magnetic pole of the
Earth was fixed by observation. It has its own page:
[[page:the-north-magnetic-pole-1831]]. It is also the reason this expedition still matters to
people doing science in these waters, and [[artifact:ross-victory-028|his own careful refusal
to overclaim it]] is worth reading by anybody who writes up an observation.

## The Netsilingmiut

On 9 January 1830 four people were seen from the observatory at Felix Harbour. Ross walked
out alone and greeted them with the one word he knew, and they answered it
([[artifact:ross-victory-023|the passage]]). Over the next three years the Netsilingmiut of
Boothia sold and gave the ship seal and salmon and caribou, sold and made clothing, taught
the crew how to travel, guided the sledge journeys that fixed the shape of the country, and
drew its chart. [[person:Ikmallik]] drew the coast west; [[person:Tulluahiu]], who had lost
a leg to a bear, was given a wooden one by the ship's carpenter and was back at the seal
holes within a fortnight. [[page:the-netsilingmiut-and-the-victory]] is about that
relationship, including the parts of it Ross wrote down in terms that are hard to read now.

The charts themselves are entered under [[topic:inuit-oral-history]], where they belong with
[[person:Iligliuk]]'s of 1822 and the four that [[person:Franz Boas]] collected in the 1880s:
[[artifact:inuit-oral-history-002|the Boothia chart of January 1830]]. The engraved plate as
Ross printed it is [[artifact:ross-victory-069]], and the scene of its making is
[[artifact:ross-victory-068]].

## The dead

Three men did not come home. [[person:James Marslin]], the armourer, died at Felix Harbour on
20 January 1830 of a consumption he had brought from England. [[person:James Dixon]], the
stoker, died at Victoria Harbour on 10 January 1832 after a long illness, and is the only man
of the expedition whose grave is marked on a chart — Ross lettered it on his own survey of the
harbour, [[artifact:ross-victory-080]]. Chimham Thomas, the carpenter, died of scurvy at Fury
Beach on 16 February 1833. The cook [[person:Henry Ayre]] reached England and died soon after.
Ross's arithmetic to the Admiralty was that, two of the three having been "cut off early in
the voyage by diseases not peculiar to the climate", [[artifact:ross-victory-039|"only one man
can be said to have perished"]].

## Why it matters here

Three things, for a ship working these waters now.

The magnetic pole James Clark Ross fixed in 1831 has since travelled well over a thousand
kilometres north-west and is out over the Arctic Ocean, moving faster than it has in
recorded history. His position is the first fixed point in that record.

The coast the *Victory* charted with Netsilingmiut help — Boothia joined to the continent,
no strait below 71 degrees — closed off the route the Admiralty most expected and pushed the
search west, which is how Franklin's ships came to be where they were found
([[topic:franklin-1845]]).

And the survival is a lesson in what actually keeps people alive up here, which is not
tinned meat and not steam. It is fresh food, movement, occupation, and local knowledge
freely given. Ross knew it: he wrote that the resisting powers of "the natives of these
frozen climates" came from what and how much they ate, and then went on eating salt pork
until his carpenter died of scurvy.
