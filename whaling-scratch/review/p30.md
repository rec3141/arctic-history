The Baffin Bay fishery had two kinds of catastrophe. One was fast and
spectacular and cost almost nobody their life: nineteen or twenty ships crushed
in [[place:Melville Bay]] from 19 June 1830, the loss the fleet called the
Baffin's Bay Fair. The other was slow. It happened in the autumn of 1835, when
the fleet stayed too long on the west side and the winter came a month early,
and it killed more men than any other event in the history of the trade.

This page is about the second, and about what the loss of the ships did to the
industry. The wreck list itself — which ship, where, how — is on
[[page:the-whalers-bad-years]].

## The autumn of 1835

The season had already been bad before the ice closed. The *Isabella* of Hull
had been wrecked off the Whalefish Islands on 18 May, though Captain Carlill and
her crew were saved; the *William Lee* was lost on 4 July abreast of Dark Head,
and the *Mary Francis* was crushed on 26 August.

On 11 October the *Alfred* of Hull, working south to get clear, left eleven
ships fast in the pack between 69 and 70 degrees north and 60 and 65 degrees
west, with upwards of six hundred men aboard: *Jane*, *William Torr*, *Abram*,
*Dordon*, *Duncombe* and *Harmony* of Hull, *Viewforth* of Kirkcaldy,
*Middleton* of Aberdeen, *Lady Jane* and *Grenville Bay* of Newcastle, and
*Norfolk* of Berwick. A. G. E. Jones, who reconstructed the whole affair from
Admiralty papers, gives the shipowners' estimate of the vessels at no less than
£59,000, and their oil and whalebone at £13,500
([[source:jones1950voyage]], p. 543).

None of them was fitted for a winter. That is the whole of the disaster in one
sentence. A Davis Strait whaler carried provisions for a season, not a year, and
carried lime juice on the same reckoning. The ships were not crushed — most of
them were not crushed. They drifted south in the pack for three or four months
while the people aboard ate short and got scurvy.

- The **Middleton** was lost on 15 November on the west coast of Davis Strait.
- The **Grenville Bay** was beset in 69°30' north, carried south, driven into
  Hudson Strait by the current along the north shore and out again by the
  current along the south shore, round Button's Islands, and released on the
  Labrador coast on 16 December. She arrived off Peterhead on 3 January with
  sixteen men of the *Dordon*, the *Mary Francis* and the *William Lee* aboard
  as well as her own people.
- The **Abram** got free on 30 January in 54 north and reached Hull on 15
  February with ninety-eight hands aboard, including part of the crews of three
  lost ships; the **Jane** was released the next day, in 58 north, and reached
  Stromness on 13 February.
- The **Viewforth** came out at the end of January in 59°40' north with seven of
  her whole complement fit for duty. Ross hired an empty house at Stromness and
  fitted it up as a hospital; she left sixteen of her crew in it.
- The **Lady Jane** lost twenty-two men to the cold and the want of provisions.
  Of her survivors and the *Mary Francis*'s, only ten or twelve could move. She
  reached Stromness after the *Cove* had sailed again.

## The William Torr

She was last seen on 16 October, forty-five miles southeast of
[[place:Cape Searle]], drifting south. Nothing more was ever seen of her. On 17
June 1836 the *Antilles*, on passage from Venice to Hull, picked up a large oil
cask branded *William Torr* in 46°11' north; two other ships found blubber casks
further north, but those bore no names and Jones says they may have come from
other wrecked whalers. Lloyd's reported her lost in October 1836.

What had happened came out four years later, and it came from Inuit. Jones:

> It appeared from information given by the Eskimo to Captain Harrison of the
> *Norfolk* in Northumberland Inlet in 1840 that the *William Torr* was crushed
> off Cape Fry in December 1835. Twenty-two sick men were left with the Eskimo,
> but all died, despite their help. A party led by Captain Smith set off across
> the ice for the *Jane* which they assumed to be frozen up farther to the
> south. They were never heard of again.

([[source:jones1950voyage]], p. 552.) It is worth being plain about what that
paragraph contains. The only witnesses to the largest loss of life in the
Baffin Bay whale fishery were Inuit; the twenty-two men who were put ashore were
taken in and fed by people who had no surplus and no cure for scurvy either; and
the record exists at all because five years later somebody in
[[place:Cumberland Sound]] told a whaling captain what had happened and the
captain wrote it down. That is the shape of a great deal of the Arctic record.

## HMS Cove

The public reaction was loud enough to make the Admiralty do something it had
never done for merchant seamen. Petitions came in from all over the country.
Trinity House of Hull subscribed £1,000 and the mayor and corporation £100; a
further £4,000 was wanted, and a meeting of bankers, merchants and shipowners at
the Mansion House in London on 5 January 1836 collected subscriptions. The
government's offer was that if the owners and underwriters would fit out and man
a ship, the Admiralty would commission her, store her and pay the crew.

[[person:James Clark Ross]] — the most experienced Arctic officer then living,
with fourteen summers and eight winters to his credit, and at that moment in the
middle of the first systematic magnetic survey of the British Isles — offered
his services, arrived in Hull on 14 December, examined the ships in the harbour
and picked the *Cove*, a ship of 374 tons built at Whitby in 1798. She was
commissioned on 18 December and at sea within a fortnight, two or three hundred
shipwrights, caulkers and artificers having worked on her night and day.

Her master was [[person:Richard Humphreys]], who had fitted her out. Three years
earlier, commanding the whaler *Isabella*, he had taken [[person:John Ross]] and
the crew of the *Victory* off the beach near Navy Board Inlet after four winters
([[page:the-isabella-and-after]]); it was Humphreys who had written to James
Clark Ross in the first place, urging that two ships be sent. His first
lieutenant was [[person:Francis Crozier]], then on half pay; the third was
[[person:Erasmus Ommanney]], made lieutenant on 10 December and commissioned to
the *Cove* a fortnight later. Both would spend the next twenty years of their
lives in these waters, Crozier in one direction and Ommanney searching for him.
She sailed with a complement of sixty-four.

The *Cove* sailed from Hull on 6 January 1836 and left Stromness on the 11th —
into the North Atlantic, in January. On the 28th, in 59°37' north and 31° west,
a hurricane from the northwest carried away her bowsprit. An officer wrote home
from off Lewis a week later:

> ...on the 13th we had heavy gales from the N. & W. with hard squalls of snow
> and hail, which weather continued without intermission till the 28th when it
> was such as neither I nor the oldest seaman on board ever saw equalled,
> blowing a perfect hurricane from the N.W. with a most tremendous sea and
> heavier falls of hail and snow than I ever supposed could come down. At about
> half past four... a most awful sea broke over our starboard bow.

([[source:jones1950voyage]], p. 547.) She came back into Stromness under a jury
bowsprit at eleven in the morning of 5 February and lay there while the
survivors came in around her — the *Jane* on the 13th, the *Viewforth* with her
sixteen sick. Ross sailed again on 24 February, reached the pack edge in 54
north on 7 April, spent the spring and early summer working the ice and the
Greenland settlements, was at the Whalefish Islands in June, searched into July
for the *William Torr* without finding a trace of her, and anchored at Junction
Dock, Hull, on 31 August 1836 with all hands in perfect health.

He rescued nobody. Every ship that was going to get out had got out before he
reached the ice, and the rest were gone. The second relief force never sailed at
all: HMS *Terror* was commissioned on 14 January under Commander Edward Belcher,
the Commons was told on 29 February that the last accounts made further steps
unnecessary, and she was paid off on 24 March. HMS *Erebus* stayed at Portsmouth
and was never commissioned. Twelve years later those same two ships would be
abandoned off King William Island ([[topic:franklin-1845]]).

## The following winter, and the argument that went nowhere

The winter of 1836-37 caught six more whalers with about three hundred men. This
time the Admiralty declined to fit out anything; the Treasury offered a bounty
to whoever performed the relief and undertook to pay for the provisions and the
wages of bringing the ships home.

In January 1837 a discussion began in the *Aberdeen Herald* and the *Nautical
Magazine* about establishing a settlement in Davis Strait as a depot for whale
oil and a refuge for beset crews. Captain Beaufort, the Admiralty's
Hydrographer, asked Ross for his views; in a long letter Ross said he favoured
the shore between Cumberland Strait and Cape Walsingham over Pond's Bay or
Coutts Inlet. The discussion ran three months and then died out, and nothing was
built. Whalers went on wintering unprovisioned for another two decades until
they started doing it deliberately, with shore stations and Inuit hunters
([[page:whaling-kekerten-and-the-shore-stations]]) — which is, in the end, the
answer the fishery found to the problem of 1835, and it was an Inuit answer.

## What it did to the trade

Vaughan's summary is one sentence: after the disastrous years of 1830, 1835 and
1836 the Davis Strait whaling fleet fell from sixty to ninety vessels to twenty
or fewer ([[source:vaughan1986bowhead]], p. 295). Hull, which had been the
greatest whaling port in Britain, was down to five whalers by 1857 and sent its
last whaling voyage in 1869.

The Baffin Bay Circuit itself did not survive the crash in its old form. Captain
William Barron made six voyages in the *Truelove* of Hull between 1849 and 1854.
In 1849 he ran the full circuit successfully — five whales off the West
Greenland coast before Disko and six off Pond Inlet after the North Water
crossing. In every one of the five seasons after that, the circuit was either
attempted and abandoned or not attempted at all: the *Truelove* fished around
Disko, took a whale or two, and turned back south round the bottom of the Middle
Ice to try the Southwest Fishing again. Ships stopped crossing Melville Bay not
because it grew more dangerous but because there were no longer enough of them,
and no longer enough whales on the West Side, to make it worth the three weeks.
