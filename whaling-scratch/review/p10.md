For nearly two centuries the busiest shipping in the eastern Arctic was not
exploration. It was a hunt. Between about 1719 and 1915, Dutch, German, Danish,
British and American ships worked north through Davis Strait and around
[[place:Melville Bay|Baffin Bay]] after one animal: the bowhead, *Balaena
mysticetus*, which whalers called the Greenland whale or simply "the fish". At
the height of it, in the 1820s, sixty to ninety British ships alone were in the
strait in a season, and a single ship might see forty sail around her off
Mittimatalik (Pond Inlet). Almost every European name on the chart of Baffin
Bay was put there by a naval expedition; almost every ship that used those
waters was a whaler.

## Why the bowhead

A bowhead is a slow, fat, surface-swimming whale that floats when it is dead —
which is why it was called the "right" whale to kill. It carries up to half a
metre of blubber and, in its mouth, plates of baleen ("whalebone") that can be
several metres long. The blubber was boiled for oil: lamp oil, and the lubricant
and leather-dressing oil of an industrialising Britain. The baleen was the
springy, splittable plastic of the nineteenth century — corset stays, umbrella
ribs, carriage springs, buggy whips, brushes.

The two products traded places. Early on the oil was the prize. After mineral
oil arrived in the 1860s the oil price collapsed and the baleen price climbed,
until in 1904 whalebone was worth about $15,000 a ton and one large whale was
worth $15,000 to $20,000 — enough, A. P. Low wrote, to repay the whole cost of
fitting out a steam whaler ([[source:low1906report]], pp. 248-249). That is why
the fishery kept going for fifty years after it had stopped making sense as an
oil business — and why it did not stop until the last whales were too scarce to
find.

## The shape of the trade

**Beginnings, 1719-1770s.** Twenty-nine Dutch whalers are recorded in Davis
Strait in 1719, the year usually taken as the opening of the fishery, though
Richard Vaughan thinks earlier Dutch voyages will turn up; by the 1720s an
average of 67 Dutch whalers a year were sailing there, and in 1732 and 1733 the
fleets were 137 and 118 ships. Hamburg and Bremen ships followed the same
pattern. Denmark ran its own small land-based fishery on the West Greenland
coast, with Greenlandic crews, from the late 1750s to about 1850 — lookout posts
on headlands, English harpoons and English boats bought from passing whalers, 20
to 30 whales a year in the 1790s, one whale a year by the 1850s. English-speaking
ships came in regularly only in the 1770s, out of Hull and Whitby
([[source:vaughan1986bowhead]], pp. 292-293, 297).

**The Baffin Bay Circuit, 1817-1850s.** In 1817 the *Larkins* of Leith and the
*Elizabeth* of Aberdeen crossed the head of Baffin Bay to 77 degrees north, and
the fishery changed completely. From then on the fleet ran an annual
counter-clockwise loop of the whole bay — the Southwest Fishing off Resolution
Island in April and May, the East Side up the Greenland coast, the crossing of
Melville Bay in June and July, [[place:The North Water]], then the West Side and
[[place:Lancaster Sound]]. This is the part of the story that touches the water
this ship is in, and it has its own page: [[page:whaling-the-baffin-bay-circuit]].

**The disasters, 1830-1837.** The circuit was paid for in ships. Nineteen or
twenty were destroyed in [[place:Melville Bay]] on and after 19 June 1830, in
the loss the fleet named the Baffin Fair; eleven were beset in Davis Strait in
October 1835 and men died all winter. Vaughan puts it flatly: after 1830, 1835
and 1836 the Davis Strait fleet fell from 60-90 vessels to twenty or fewer. See
[[page:whaling-the-disaster-years]] and, for the wrecks themselves,
[[page:the-whalers-bad-years]].

**Wintering and the shore stations, 1840-1915.** Two things saved the trade
after the crash. One was going where the fleet had not been: in 1840
[[person:William Penny]], piloted by the young Inuk [[person:Eenoolooapik]],
took the *Bon Accord* into a sound the Inuit called Tenudiakbeek and brought
Europeans back into [[place:Cumberland Sound]] for the first time since John
Davis in 1585 ([[page:whaling-eenoolooapik-and-tenudiakbeek]]). The other was
staying the winter, which doubled the working season. From 1851 whalers wintered
in Cumberland Sound; from 1857 there were permanent shore stations at
[[place:Kekerten]] and later [[place:Uumanarjuaq]] (Blacklead Island), run on
Inuit labour ([[page:whaling-kekerten-and-the-shore-stations]]). Americans
opened Hudson Bay in 1860 and worked it until 1915
([[page:whaling-whalers-and-inuit]]).

**Steam, and the end.** From the 1860s the fleet was Scottish, steam-driven and
sealing off Newfoundland to pay for the whaling
([[page:whaling-the-dundee-steam-fleet]]). Hull's last whaling voyage was in
1869. Peterhead gave up in 1893, Dundee in 1913. By 1904 Low counted five ships
in the whole British fishery, four of them in Baffin Bay and one in Hudson Bay.
The whalers were gone from Cumberland Sound by 1917 and the post at
[[place:Kekerten]] was abandoned by the mid-1920s. What ended it was not
sentiment and not law. It was that there were no whales left to catch.

## How many whales

W. Gillies Ross made the first serious count, working from unpublished logbooks
and journals and from the Dutch and British annual lists. Two numbers come out
of it and they should not be run together.

The first is what the record actually shows:

> the whale fishery in Davis Strait (1719-1911), yielded 28,394 Greenland
> whales, and those in Hudson Bay (1860-1915) and the Beaufort Sea (1899-1908)
> 572 and 794 respectively, making a total catch in the waters adjacent to
> northern Canada of 29,760 whales.

The second is what the kill probably was. Ross adds the whaling his sources do
not cover — Danes, Dutch and Norwegians before 1719, American voyages of the
mid-eighteenth century, more than 400 British voyages before 1814 — at 2,000 or
more whales, which brings the catch to 31,760; and then a further fifth again
for wounded whales that escaped and died. On that reckoning the total killed
"may have exceeded 38,000" ([[source:ross1979annual]], p. 120;
[[artifact:whaling-032|the compilation itself]]). Ross calls his own figures
preliminary and lists five ways they are incomplete.

The Davis Strait and Baffin Bay stock has not recovered on any human timescale.
Bowheads are among the longest-lived mammals known, and the published age
estimates run past two hundred years; if those estimates hold, a whale alive off
Qikiqtaaluk (Baffin Island) today could have been born while the Dundee steamers
were still working. [[place:Niginganiq]] (Isabella Bay), on the stretch of the
Baffin coast the whalers worked hardest, is now a National Wildlife Area
established for bowheads at the initiative of the community of Kangiqtugaapik
(Clyde River) — protected for the same reason the whalers went there.

## Who was there

The record names very few of the thousands of Inuit who made the later fishery
work, and quite a lot of the Scotsmen. It preserves [[person:Eenoolooapik]], who
drew the chart that opened Cumberland Sound; his sister [[person:Tookoolito]],
who went on to Charles Francis Hall; [[person:Angmarlik]], a whaleboat captain
and camp leader at [[place:Kekerten]]; [[person:Niviatsinaq]] (Shoofly), the
leading Inuk figure at Qatiktalik (Cape Fullerton) and [[person:George Comer]]'s
companion; [[person:Enu]], who taught the *Aurora*'s surgeon Inuktitut in 1884.
It preserves the masters — Penny, [[person:James Mutch]], Comer,
[[person:James Fairweather]], William Adams — and the owners in Aberdeen and
Dundee and New London who never came north at all.

It matters which of these the sources are. Nearly everything written down was
written by whalers, for whalers or for their public at home. When Inuit
testimony survives it is usually because a captain wrote down what he was told,
as Captain Harrison of the *Norfolk* did in 1840 when Inuit in Cumberland Sound
told him how the *William Torr*'s men had died — the only account there is of
the loss of that ship and her whole company.

## Why it still matters here

The whalers are part of the reason there are Inuit communities where there are.
Kekerten and Uumanarjuaq drew families off the land into permanent camps; when
the stations closed those families moved again, and the descendants of the
Kekerten people are largely in Panniqtuuq (Pangnirtung) today. The whalers
brought firearms, whaleboats, tobacco, tea, wooden houses and debt, and they
brought epidemic disease into populations with no exposure to it. They also left
the first substantial written and photographic record of Inuit life in the
region — Comer's wax cylinders and plaster casts, Mutch's notes for Franz Boas —
a record Inuit organisations now use, on their own terms, as evidence about
their own past.

And for the science this ship does: the whalers' logbooks are the longest
systematic observation series that exists for sea ice in Baffin Bay. Every
logbook records the ice edge, the state of the pack, the date the North Water
opened and the date Melville Bay let a ship through, from 1817 onward. They were
written by people whose lives depended on getting it right.
