# The disaster years, 1835-1837
[page · whaling · draft]

The Baffin Bay fishery had two kinds of catastrophe. One was fast and
spectacular and cost almost nobody their life: nineteen or twenty ships crushed
in a week of June 1830 in [[place:Melville Bay]], the loss the fleet called the
Baffin's Bay Fair. The other was slow. It happened in the autumn of 1835, when
the fleet stayed too long on the west side and the winter came a month early,
and it killed more men than any other event in the history of the trade.

This page is about the second, and about what the loss of the ships did to the
industry. The wreck list itself — which ship, where, how — is on
[[page:the-whalers-bad-years]].

## The autumn of 1835

The season had already been bad before the ice closed. The *Isabella* of Hull
had been wrecked off the Whalefish Islands on 18 May, the *William Lee* lost on
4 July abreast of Dark Head, and the *Mary Francis* crushed on 26 August.

On 11 October the *Alfred* of Hull, working south to get clear, left eleven
ships fast in the pack between 69 and 70 degrees north and 60 and 65 degrees
west, with upwards of six hundred men aboard: *Jane*, *William Torr*, *Abram*,
*Dordon*, *Duncombe* and *Harmony* of Hull, *Viewforth* of Kirkcaldy,
*Middleton* of Aberdeen, *Lady Jane* and *Grenville Bay* of Newcastle, and
*Norfolk* of Berwick. A. G. E. Jones, who reconstructed the whole affair from
Admiralty papers, puts the value of the ships at £59,000 and their oil and bone
at £13,500 ([[source:jones1950voyage]], p. 543).

None of them was fitted for a winter. That is the whole of the disaster in one
sentence. A Davis Strait whaler carried provisions for a season, not a year, and
carried lime juice on the same reckoning. The ships were not crushed — most of
them were not crushed. They drifted south in the pack for three or four months
while the people aboard ate short and got scurvy.

- The **Middleton** was lost on 15 November on the west side of the strait.
- The **Grenville Bay** was beset in 69°30' north, carried south, driven into
  Hudson Strait by the current along the north shore and out again by the
  current along the south shore, round the Button Islands, and released on the
  Labrador coast on 16 December. She reached Peterhead on 3 January with
  sixteen survivors of the *Dordon*, the *Mary Francis* and the *William Lee*
  aboard as well as her own people.
- The **Jane** got free on 31 January in 58 north and reached Stromness on 13
  February; the **Abram** was released the next day and made Hull on 15
  February.
- The **Viewforth** came out at the end of January in 59°40' north with seven
  men fit for duty. Sixteen of her crew went straight to hospital at Stromness.
- The **Lady Jane** lost twenty-two men to scurvy and want of provisions. When
  she came in, ten or twelve of her people could still walk.

## The William Torr

She was last seen on 16 October, forty-five miles southeast of
[[place:Cape Searle]], drifting south. Nothing more was ever seen of her. In
June 1836 the *Antilles* picked up a large oil cask branded *William Torr* in
mid-Atlantic, in 46°11' north; two other ships found casks of her blubber
further north. Lloyd's posted her lost in October 1836.

What had happened came out four years later, and it came from Inuit. Jones:

> It appeared from information given by the Eskimo to Captain Harrison of the
> *Norfolk* in Northumberland Inlet in 1840 that the *William Torr* was crushed
> off Cape Fry in December 1835. Twenty-two sick men were left with the Eskimo,
> but all died, despite their help. A party led by Captain Smith set off across
> the ice for the *Jane* which they assumed to be frozen up farther to the
> south. They were never heard of again.

([[source:jones1950voyage]], pp. 549-550.) It is worth being plain about what
that paragraph contains. The only witnesses to the largest loss of life in the
Baffin Bay whale fishery were Inuit; the twenty-two men who were put ashore were
taken in and fed by people who had no surplus and no cure for scurvy either; and
the record exists at all because five years later somebody in
[[place:Cumberland Sound]] told a whaling captain what had happened and the
captain wrote it down. That is the shape of a great deal of the Arctic record.

## HMS Cove

The public reaction was loud enough to make the Admiralty do something it had
never done for merchant seamen. Petitions came in from all over the country.
Trinity House of Hull put up £1,000 and the corporation £100; a meeting of
bankers and shipowners at the Mansion House on 5 January 1836 raised £4,000
more. The government's offer was that if the owners and underwriters would fit
out and man a ship, the Admiralty would commission her, store her and pay the
crew.

[[person:James Clark Ross]] — fourteen summers and eight winters in the Arctic,
and at that moment in the middle of the first magnetic survey of the British
Isles — offered his services, went to Hull on 14 December, walked the harbour
and picked the *Cove*, a 374-ton Whitby-built ship of 1798. She was commissioned
on 18 December and at sea within a fortnight, two or three hundred shipwrights
having worked on her night and day.

Her master was **Richard Humphreys**. Three years earlier, commanding the whaler
*Isabella*, he had taken [[person:John Ross]] and the crew of the *Victory* off
the beach near Navy Board Inlet after four winters
([[page:the-isabella-and-after]]). Eight months before this, he had lost the
*Isabella* in the ice off the Whalefish Islands. He fitted the *Cove* out and
went north again as her master. Her first lieutenant was
[[person:Francis Crozier]], then on half pay; her third was
[[person:Erasmus Ommanney]], promoted ten days before. Both would spend the next
twenty years of their lives in these waters, Crozier in one direction and
Ommanney searching for him.

She sailed from Hull on 6 January 1836 — into the North Atlantic, in January.
On the 28th, in 59°37' north and 31° west, a hurricane from the northwest took
away her bowsprit. An officer wrote home from off Lewis a week later:

> on the 28th when it was such as neither I nor the oldest seaman on board ever
> saw equalled, blowing a perfect hurricane from the N.W. with a most tremendous
> sea and heavier falls of hail and snow than I ever supposed could come down.
> At about half past four... a most awful sea broke over our starboard bow.

She limped to Stromness under a jury bowsprit on 5 February and lay there while
the survivors came in around her — the *Jane* on the 13th, the *Viewforth* the
same day with her sixteen sick. Ross sailed again on 24 February, reached the
pack edge in 54 north on 7 April, spent the spring and early summer working the
ice and the Greenland settlements, was at the Whalefish Islands in June, and
searched into July for the *William Torr* without finding a trace of her. He came
into Hull on 31 August 1836.

He rescued nobody. Every ship that was going to get out had got out before he
reached the ice, and the rest were gone. The second relief force never sailed at
all: HMS *Terror* was commissioned on 14 January under Commander Edward Belcher,
the Commons was told on 29 February that relief was no longer necessary, and she
was paid off on 24 March. HMS *Erebus* was never commissioned. Twelve years later
those same two ships would be abandoned off King William Island
([[topic:franklin-1845]]).

## The following winter, and the argument that went nowhere

The winter of 1836-37 caught six more whalers with about three hundred men. This
time the Admiralty declined to fit out anything and offered a bounty to whoever
performed the relief.

There was a proposal, argued in the *Aberdeen Herald* and the *Nautical
Magazine* through the early months of 1837, to establish a depot of provisions
on the Baffin coast where beset crews could go — Ross favoured the coast between
Cumberland Strait and Cape Walsingham over Pond's Bay or Coutts Inlet. The
argument ran for three months and then stopped, and nothing was built. Whalers
went on wintering unprovisioned for another two decades until they started doing
it deliberately, with shore stations and Inuit hunters
([[page:whaling-kekerten-and-the-shore-stations]]) — which is, in the end, the
answer the fishery found to the problem of 1835, and it was an Inuit answer.

## What it did to the trade

Vaughan's summary is one sentence: after the disastrous years of 1830, 1835 and
1836 the Davis Strait whaling fleet fell from sixty to ninety vessels to twenty
or fewer ([[source:vaughan1986bowhead]], p. 295). Insurance rates in Hull never
recovered; Hull, which had been the greatest whaling port in Britain, sent one
steamer in 1868 and its last whaling voyage in 1869.

The Baffin Bay Circuit itself did not survive the crash in its old form. Captain
William Barron made six voyages in the *Truelove* of Hull between 1849 and 1854.
In 1849 he ran the full circuit successfully. In every one of the five seasons
after that, the circuit was either attempted and abandoned or not attempted at
all: the *Truelove* fished around Disko, took a whale or two, and turned back
south round the bottom of the Middle Ice to try the Southwest Fishing again.
Ships stopped crossing Melville Bay not because it grew more dangerous but
because there were no longer enough of them, and no longer enough whales on the
West Side, to make it worth the three weeks.
