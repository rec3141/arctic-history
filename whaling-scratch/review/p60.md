By the last decades of the fishery the relationship between whalers and Inuit
was not contact. It was employment, and in places it was the whole local
economy. This page is about what that was actually like, mostly in the words of
the whaling masters themselves, because they are the ones who wrote it down.

## Crews, and moving them

In June 1903 the Dundee ship *Albert*, under [[person:James Mutch]], came into
[[place:Cumberland Sound]], could not get through the heavy pack to
[[place:Kekerten]], anchored instead at [[place:Uumanarjuaq]] (Blacklead
Island) on the morning of 25 July, and did this:

> After we had shipped two boats' crews, and gotten all their beds, tents, dogs,
> and other baggage aboard, we left, Aug. 2, for Ponds Bay.

([[source:mutch1906whaling]], p. 485.) Two whaleboat crews with their families,
their dogs and their household goods, carried nine hundred kilometres up the
Baffin coast to work a season at [[place:Mittimatalik]] (Pond Inlet). This was
normal. The whalers moved Inuit labour along the coast the way they moved
barrels, and some of those families stayed. The population of the Pond Inlet
region today includes descendants of people brought north from Cumberland Sound
by ships.

Mutch's coastal run is worth reading for its own sake, because it is the last
good primary description of this shore under sail: Cape Mercy and Saunderson's
Tower, Exeter Sound, calms and fog, Qivitung and Cape Hooper where a boat came
off with news and a list of relatives' names — Kiuktan, Kingudling, Kepignang,
Tudjeguaping — Cape Kater and the Isabella Bank bergs, Cape Aston, Eglinton
Fiord, Dexterity Harbour with the wrecks of the *Eagle*, the *Nova Zembla* and
the *Dexterity* on its beach. He met the *Diana*, the *Eclipse* and the *Balaena*
on the way, all of them working the same coast. They reached Pond's Bay on 6
September 1903 and landed everything — people, dogs, furniture, station timber,
house iron, provisions — because it was late and the year was closing. Erik
Harbour froze on 24 October. The sun went on the 29th and did not come back
until 9 February.

## The Tununirmiut knew the London price

Mutch is irritated, and it is the most useful thing in his article:

> The real Ponds Bay Eskimo had been coming and going all winter, trading a
> fox-skin when they had one, but always wanting nearly the home value for it...
> When a bear-skin was brought, though it was small, a telescope or a gun was
> asked for it. They are much like those who said, "If one never asks, one never
> gets."

(p. 488.) He adds that they believed sealskins were worth more than ten times
what they fetched in the London market, and that they charged well for
everything, being used to getting full value from the whalers. A Scottish station
manager complaining that his suppliers had worked out what their goods were worth
in London and were pricing accordingly is a better description of the trade than
any amount of sympathy would be. This was a negotiation between parties who both
understood it, conducted from very unequal positions.

## Fullerton Harbour

On the Hudson Bay side the arrangement was more total. [[person:George Comer]],
master of the New Bedford schooner *Era*, made six Hudson Bay voyages between
1895 and 1912, wintering two years at a time. His 1903-05 voyage — the one A. P.
Low photographed ([[artifact:whaling-012]], [[artifact:whaling-014]]) — went like
this. The *Era* left New Bedford on 30 June 1903, ran up outside Labrador,
through Hudson Strait, and came to Qatiktalik (Cape Fullerton) at one in the
afternoon of 8 August:

> Here we found our natives waiting for the vessel. They had already taken two
> whales for us.

([[source:comer1906whaling]], p. 476.) Half the Aivilingmiut were engaged for
the time the ship was in the bay. About seventy people of that tribe were at
Fullerton through the winter of 1903-04 and had a meal of biscuit and coffee on
the ship twice a day ([[source:low1906report]], p. 268). The men hunted caribou,
which is what kept the crew from scurvy; the women made the fur clothing, without
which nobody could work outdoors. The ship's own whaleboats went out on sledges
in May, dragged six miles to open water by all the Inuit and all the dogs
together, and the families moved their tents out to be near the boats.

What they were paid, in Low's words: "No regular wages are paid to the natives
either during the winter when hunting, or when in the boats in the summer, but
they are given such articles as the captain thinks they should have or deserve,
everything being left to his judgment or caprice" (p. 268).

Comer's own summary is the opposite and equally sincere: "Whatever may be said
for or against the whaling-ships, they have certainly been of great benefit to
the Eskimo" (p. 483). Both sentences are about the same arrangement. He was, by
the standards of the trade, a good employer: his people wintered in better health
than most, because he kept close to the Inuit and took their advice about food
and clothing, and he ran dances, concerts and sports through the dark to keep
everyone sane.

[[person:Niviatsinaq]] — Shoofly to the whalemen — was the central Inuk figure at
Fullerton and Comer's companion, and much of what he collected and recorded went
through her. Her beaded amauti, photographed by Low's expedition, is one of the
best-known Inuit garments of the period.

## What Comer took, and what he was told about it

Comer collected for the American Museum of Natural History: clothing, implements
and weapons, hundreds of plaster casts of Inuit faces and hands, photographs,
and wax-cylinder recordings of songs, dances and stories made during the 1903-05
voyage — the first sound recordings ever made among Canadian Inuit
([[source:ross1983comer]]). Much of his written material went to Franz Boas and
appears in *The Central Eskimo* and in Boas's later reports; so did
[[person:James Mutch]]'s.

He also, as he says himself, took skulls out of graves. Then a whale destroyed
his boat and nearly killed him, and he was told why:

> Among other things I had taken several skulls from graves of the natives. This
> the natives thought was wrong. After our encounter with the whale, they claimed
> that the spirits of those whose skulls I had taken had gone into the whale, and
> punished me, but that, if I did not take any more, it would not happen to me
> again. It is needless to say that I did not tamper with skulls any more.

(p. 483.) Read it plainly: people told a collector that removing their dead
was wrong; he did not stop because of the argument; he stopped because he was
frightened. The objection was made at the time, by the people concerned, and it
is preserved in his own printed words. That is worth knowing when the question
of returning museum collections comes up, as it does.

Another passage from the same voyage, of a different kind. In the summer of 1904
at Duke of York Bay, at the north end of Salliq (Southampton Island), the *Era*'s
boats came on fourteen Netsilingmiut who had been camped for sealing on the
mainland shore in the early spring of 1903 and carried away when the ice broke
up. A girl of about fourteen died adrift. The rest reached Southampton, managed
to live, and ran out of ammunition. They asked to be taken back; Comer took them
aboard and landed them at Gore Bay, from which, after eighteen months away, they
could travel home (p. 479).

## The Sadlermiut

The people of Salliq, the Sadlermiut, were an isolated population on Southampton
Island, distinct in language and material culture from their neighbours — Comer
noted that they had hunted bowheads from an island with no wood for bows or
harpoons except what came from wrecks.

**What happened to them is not told properly on this page, and the gap is left
visible on purpose.** They came to an end as a people early in the twentieth
century, while American whalers were wintering across Roes Welcome Sound at
Fullerton. But the only statement about it in the sources gathered here is a
single clause in W. Gillies Ross's biographical sketch of George Comer, which
calls them "the isolated inhabitants of Southampton Island, the Sadlermiut, who
were doomed to extinction in 1902" ([[source:ross1983comer]]) and says nothing
about how, or why, or on what evidence. A. P. Low, who was at Fullerton that same
winter, never uses the name. Comer's own 1906 article describes Southampton
Island and its people but not their end. So nothing here carries a cause, a
sequence of events or a count of the dead, and this page will not supply what it
cannot stand behind. The standard modern account is in W. Gillies Ross,
*Whaling and Eskimos: Hudson Bay 1860-1915* (1975); until that is in hand, the
honest thing is to name what is missing rather than repeat a summary of one of
the largest recorded mortality events in this region.

## The government arrives

While Comer was frozen in at Fullerton in the winter of 1903-04, the Canadian
government steamer *Neptune* was frozen in beside him with A. P. Low aboard and
a detachment of the North-West Mounted Police under Major Moodie. Comer's account
of this is very dry:

> During the winter the major had a house erected on the island, near the ship,
> and sent me word that thereafter the place would be a port of entry, and that
> duty would be collected on all goods to be landed. This duty I paid, and I
> suffered no further interference in our whaling.

(p. 477.) That is Canadian sovereignty in the eastern Arctic arriving, in
person, as a customs bill delivered to an American whaling captain across the
ice ([[topic:sverdrup-bernier-peary]], [[topic:posts-rcmp-missions]]). The next
autumn the *Arctic* came in under Bernier with Moodie in charge. The whalers had
been there for forty years; the state arrived at the very end, in time to tax the
last two ships.

Christmas 1903 at Fullerton, with both vessels in and dances every week, was by
Comer's account a grand time, "one which has probably never been equalled in
those parts before or since" (p. 477). Within fifteen years the whaling was over.

## After

When the ships stopped coming, the people who had organised their lives around
them had to organise them around something else, and what arrived was the trading
post, the mission and the police ([[topic:posts-rcmp-missions]]). The knowledge
did not go anywhere: [[person:Angmarlik]] ran a whaleboat at Kekerten in the
station's last years and led the camp for decades afterwards, into the 1940s, and
the visitor centre at [[place:Panniqtuuq]] carries his name and holds the
community's own account of this history.

Two things are worth holding together at the end. The whaling era is remembered
in Cumberland Sound and northwestern Hudson Bay as a time of skill, work, and
real capability — boats commanded, whales taken, wages of a kind, families that
prospered. It is also the era in which introduced disease killed an unknown but
large number of people, in which a whole population on Southampton Island came
to an end, and in which the independence of a hunting economy was traded away
for equipment. Both are true, and Inuit accounts have generally been better than
outsiders' at holding them together without flinching from either.
