The last fifty years of the fishery were run from one Scottish city, in ships
with engines, by men who spent half of each year killing seals off Newfoundland
to pay for the half they spent looking for whales.

## How the fleet shrank

A. P. Low set the figures out in one paragraph ([[source:low1906report]],
p. 250). In **1868** the Arctic fleet totalled thirty steam and sailing vessels:
Dundee thirteen steam and one sail, Peterhead four steam and eight sail,
Fraserburgh two sail, Aberdeen one sail, Hull one steamer. That was the last
year a ship sailed from Hull. By **1877** steam had replaced sail altogether and
the fleet was down to thirteen vessels, all of them sailing from Dundee. By
**1904** the Dundee fleet was five, four of them whaling in Baffin Bay and the
fifth in Hudson Bay.

Vaughan's figures agree and add the tail: about eight Dundee ships a year to
Davis Strait through the 1880s, three to five in the 1890s and early 1900s, plus
two or three a year east of Greenland. Peterhead's last Arctic whaling voyage
was in 1893, Dundee's in 1913 ([[source:vaughan1986bowhead]], pp. 296-297).

Low's note on why nothing replaced them is the sort of thing that only a
practical man writes down: "No new ships have been built during the past
twenty-five years, and the construction of these strong oak vessels, sheathed
with greenheart or ironbark, is fast becoming a lost art in these days of steel
and iron ships" (p. 250). A steel hull is no good in ice that will squeeze it;
the trade needed wooden ships and the wooden shipyards had gone.

## What steam actually bought

Two things, and neither of them was speed for its own sake.

**Independence of the wind in the ice.** A sailing ship in a lead is helpless in
a calm; her crew get out and pull. Compare [[artifact:whaling-021|Markham's
crossing of Melville Bay in 1873]], done in a night, with the three weeks it had
cost a sailing ship and with the [[page:whaling-the-disaster-years|1830 and 1835
disasters]] that ended the sailing fleet. [[artifact:whaling-039|The capstan]] did
not disappear — ships were still warped and still nipped
([[artifact:whaling-038]]) — but a steamer could usually get out of the way.

**A second industry.** From the 1860s the Scottish ships sealed before they
whaled. [[person:David Moore Lindsay]], surgeon of the Dundee steam whaler
*Aurora*, describes the pattern for 1884: the ship
[[artifact:whaling-042|left the Dundee docks on 31 January]] under
[[person:James Fairweather]], sealed out of St John's, Newfoundland, took 28,153
seals over two cruises, refitted, and sailed north for the whaling on 8 May. In
1874 the eleven-ship Dundee fleet's seals were worth about £30,600 and its whales
about £87,500 ([[source:vaughan1986bowhead]], p. 297). The whales were still the
prize, but the seals paid the wages.

And when the whales failed, the ships took whatever else was moveable. Captain
Milne of the SS *Esquimaux* kept a sort of game list in his annual log. In 1885
his crew killed 57 narwhals, 18 walrus, one bear and 800 salmon, nearly all off
the West Side. In 1887: 14 bears, 12 reindeer, 11 foxes, eight ptarmigan, 58
belugas — and one bowhead. In 1888, besides 22,824 seals off Newfoundland: 12
bottlenose whales, 24 narwhals, 100 walrus, eight bears, 19 reindeer, and again a
single bowhead (p. 297). Read downward, that is a whale fishery turning itself
into a general Arctic hunt because its own animal has run out.

## How a whale was taken

Lindsay's account is the clearest there is of the late method.

A ship the *Aurora*'s size fished ten boats: four down each side and two upper
quarter boats. A boat's crew was six — a harpooner, a boat-steerer and four
pulling. In the bow, on a swivel, the harpoon gun. Bent to the harpoon was the
*foregoer*, eighteen fathoms of very pliable untarred rope about two and a half
inches round, coiled in a tub on the port bow; behind it five tarred lines of 120
fathoms each. Six hundred fathoms of line in an open boat, and a struck
bowhead, Lindsay says, "generally starts at about seven or eight miles an
hour".

When the whale tired, the harpooners went in with six-foot lances and bled it to
death. Then the *kant*: a ribbon of blubber about two feet wide cut round the
neck, which when hauled on turns the carcass, and from which the long strips are
cut and hauled aboard by tackle on the *blubber guy*, a heavy wire between the
masts. Boat-steerers cut the blubber small on deck and threw it below.
[[artifact:whaling-040|Then the bone came aboard]] — three hundred blades from
each side of the upper jaw, the longest of them twelve feet in a large animal, a
quarter of an inch thick, tearing easily into long pieces, and weighing, Low
reckoned, close to a ton for an average whale. A whale was measured, always, by
the length of its longest blade and not by its body: Lindsay watched a ten-foot
fish come aboard that would probably yield thirteen tons of oil.

Comer's boats in Hudson Bay did the same work off sledges and floe edges rather
than from a ship: out in early May, six boats dragged by everyone and every dog
to open water six miles away, three weeks' provisions, caribou-skin sleeping
bags, families camped alongside ([[source:comer1906whaling]]).

## What the men got

A whaleman's cash wage was small; the money was in the whales. Lindsay gives the
*Aurora*'s rates. The boat that first strikes is the *fast boat*, and if the
whale is killed everyone in it gets **striking money**: the harpooner ten
shillings for putting in the gun harpoon and ten and sixpence for the hand
harpoon, or a guinea for both, and every other man in the boat half a crown in
either case.

The American side was worse. Low's description of the *Era*'s crew in 1904 is
one of the bleakest pages in his report: twenty men, none of the officers
certificated, the seamen "all landsmen without any knowledge of the sea", got
through crimps, some signed on drunk or under false statements, some signing the
articles after the ship was at sea. No wages at all — everything on shares, with
the advances the crimps had taken and the slop-chest debts set against the share,
"so that it is the usual thing for each man to find himself in debt to the owners
on his return from no matter how successful a voyage" (p. 267). Her forecastle
held twelve men with several inches of water on the floor, every bunk soaking,
and no antiscorbutics. Low lists the crew's former trades: a gunsmith, a clerk in
a wholesale drug business, an iron moulder, a mechanic, an ex-soldier, a railway
brakesman, an Armenian and several nondescripts about town. One of them had been
to sea before.

## The end

Low, writing in 1904 with the price of bone still climbing:

> The future of the whaling industry appears to be very gloomy. The annual catch
> is decreasing regularly, and only the high price of whalebone makes it at all
> profitable. No certainty of a single whale can be had, and the enterprise is
> reduced to almost a gambling chance.

(p. 272.) His year-by-year table of the American catch in Hudson Bay says it
plainly: three ships took 19 whales in 1897; two ships took 2 whales in 1903; one
ship took 1 whale in 1904 (p. 278, from figures Comer gave him). In Cumberland
Sound the bowhead population was below the economic threshold by 1912 and the
whalers were gone by 1917.

What finished it off came from outside the Arctic. Baleen was a material with
substitutes, and once spring steel and the early plastics could do the work of
whalebone in corsets and umbrella ribs the price that had kept the fishery alive
collapsed; then the war took the ships and the men. Dundee's last Arctic whaler
sailed in 1913 and the trade stopped without a closing act, having taken 29,760
bowheads north of Canada by W. Gillies Ross's count of the record, and probably
something over 38,000 once the undocumented early voyages and the whales struck
and lost are added ([[artifact:whaling-032]];
[[page:whaling-the-bowhead-fishery]]).

## What is left of it

The Eastern Canada-West Greenland bowhead population survived — barely — and is
slowly recovering. Bowheads are among the longest-lived mammals known, and the
published age estimates run past two hundred years, which, if they hold, means
whales now alive off Qikiqtaaluk were calves while the Dundee steamers were still
working. That is a claim from the biological literature rather than from the
whaling record, and it is worth checking against the current science.

Inuit in Nunavut hold a legal bowhead harvest again, small, community-run and on
a managed quota, resumed in the 1990s. It is the resumption of something the
commercial fishery interrupted rather than a new thing: Inuit were whaling in
these waters for many centuries before any ship came from Europe
([[topic:thule-dorset]]).

And the animals are still where the whalers said they were.
[[place:Niginganiq]] (Isabella Bay), on the stretch of coast the whalers worked
between Cape Kater and Cape Aston, is now a National Wildlife Area established
for bowheads at the initiative of the community of Kangiqtugaapik (Clyde River).
The whalers' knowledge of where the whales were was extremely good. It is now a
conservation map.
