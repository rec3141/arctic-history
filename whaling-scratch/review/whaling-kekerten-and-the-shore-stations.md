# Kekerten, Uumanarjuaq and the shore-station economy
[page · whaling · draft]

A Davis Strait whaler had about ten weeks of working season. She left Scotland
in March, spent June in the ice, and had to be out of the west side by
September. Everything on [[page:whaling-the-baffin-bay-circuit]] is an attempt
to squeeze a hunt into that window.

The answer, found the hard way after [[page:whaling-the-disaster-years|1835]],
was to stop leaving. If a ship froze in deliberately, provisioned and with
people who knew the country, she could be at the floe edge in April when the
whales came in, and take a second season before the fleet from home had cleared
the Pentland Firth. That single change — wintering on purpose — created the
shore stations, and the shore stations created the modern map of
[[place:Cumberland Sound]].

## How it started

The first wintering in the sound was not planned as an experiment in efficiency.
In 1851 the American barque *MacLellan* of New London left a party there for the
winter: her mate [[person:Sidney O. Buddington]] and thirteen men. The following
July the *MacLellan* was nipped in a crack at the north end of
[[place:Melville Bay]] while leading a file of whalers and naval ships north,
stove in, and given up; Sir Edward Belcher bought the wreck for £100 and his men
salvaged her. Her captain got a passage south in the *Truelove*, worrying the
whole way about the fourteen men he had left. They were found safe, dressed in
Inuit clothing, which is the entire explanation of how they survived
([[source:vaughan1986bowhead]], p. 296).

[[person:William Penny]] wintered his own ships in the sound in 1853-54 and
turned the trick into a method. In 1857 he came back with his wife Margaret and
their son, and with the Moravian missionary [[person:Matthias Warmow]], who
preached at Kekerten and elsewhere through the winter; the Moravian church then
decided a permanent mission was not feasible and withdrew. Buildings went up at
[[place:Kekerten]] in the same year, and the station was worked continuously
from then until the 1920s.

Penny had wanted much more than a station. In 1852 he asked the Colonial Office
for a large land grant on Baffin Island, arguing that a permanent British
presence would keep the Americans out. The application was killed by rival
whaling interests, but it prompted the government to instruct its ambassador in
Washington to inform the American Secretary of State that Cumberland Island was
British territory and American whalemen should not trespass. W. Gillies Ross
counts that as a step on the road to the transfer of the Arctic islands to
Canada in 1880 ([[source:ross1983penny]]). A whaling master's business plan is
part of the sovereignty story ([[topic:sverdrup-bernier-peary]]).

## What a station was

By the 1860s ships from Britain and the United States wintered routinely at
Kekerten, at [[place:Uumanarjuaq]] (Blacklead Island), at Niantilik and at Cape
Haven. Kekerten was run for the Aberdeen firm of [[person:Crawford Noble]];
[[person:James Mutch]] managed it for years and is the source of much of what
Franz Boas published about Cumberland Sound. The Anglican mission of
[[person:Edmund James Peck]] arrived at Blacklead in 1894
([[topic:posts-rcmp-missions]]).

Physically a station was small. A. P. Low, who saw them all in 1903-04,
describes Blacklead as about a dozen one-storey wooden buildings — the station
house and stores, and the church, hospital and dwelling of the missionaries —
and Kekerten as "the usual half-dozen small buildings, all nicely painted, with
the whaleboats ranged on skids, and barrels and tanks alongside clean and ready
for use" ([[source:low1906report]], pp. 9-13). Cape Haven belonged to Potter and
Wrightington of Boston. There was a ketch stationed at Pond Inlet and a vessel
serving as a station at Repulse Bay.

The staffing is the point:

> At each, there are one to three white men in charge, but the actual work is
> done by the natives... Each station has from four to six boats; consequently
> nearly every able-bodied native is employed in them during the whaling
> season.

([[artifact:whaling-010|Low, p. 10]].) Not one or two Inuit crewmen on a
European boat — the whaling was Inuit whaling, done from Inuit-crewed boats,
under a manager. [[person:Angmarlik]], who ran a boat at Kekerten in the
station's last years and led the camp afterwards, is the best-remembered of
those boat captains; the visitor centre at [[place:Panniqtuuq]] (Pangnirtung)
carries his name.

## What it paid

Low is worth quoting at length because he is a government scientist writing
without embarrassment about an arrangement he found normal.

The **value of the animal**, in 1904: whalebone about $15,000 a ton, oil about
$100 a ton, an adult female yielding nearly thirty tons of oil and a male about
twenty, an average whale carrying nearly a ton of bone. Total value of a large
whale, $15,000 to $20,000. "The capture of a single whale repays the
expenditure incurred in outfitting a steam whaling ship" (p. 249).

The **payment to the men who caught it**: they and their families were fed
biscuit, coffee and molasses from the station's stores during the season, and
supplied their own meat. "The men are paid irregularly for their work, usually
in tobacco, ammunition and clothing, and they receive extra pay when a whale is
captured. Of course the pay alone does not at all represent the value of the
whale" (p. 10).

On the American ships in Hudson Bay it was less formal still: "No regular wages
are paid to the natives either during the winter when hunting, or when in the
boats in the summer, but they are given such articles as the captain thinks they
should have or deserve, everything being left to his judgment or caprice"
(p. 268). While a family was with a ship, its entire hunt belonged to the ship.

Low also notes, without connecting it to the above, that "None of these stations
are making great profits, and some of them are being maintained at a loss"
(p. 271). Both things were true. The bone was worth a fortune and there were
almost no whales left to take it from.

It is worth saying what the goods were, because they were not trinkets. Rifles,
telescopes, whaleboats, knives, needles, matches, kettles, powder, lead, cloth,
wood. A whaleboat is a serious capital asset; so is a rifle. The Aivilingmiut on
the Hudson Bay side had been given so many boats over the years that Low found
upwards of twenty serviceable ones between Chesterfield Inlet and Repulse Bay —
and that the younger men no longer knew how to build or handle a kayak (p. 268).
That sentence contains the whole bargain: real equipment, acquired at the cost
of the skills it replaced and of any independence from the ship.

## What it cost

Three things happened to the people of Cumberland Sound in seventy years.

**They concentrated.** Families who had lived dispersed around the sound moved to
be near the stations, because that is where the work, the food and the goods
were. Low counted about five hundred Inuit in the Cumberland Gulf in 1904, "all
connected with, and depend[ent] upon, the whaling stations of Blacklead,
Kekerten and Cape Haven" (p. 10).

**They were made sick.** Populations with no previous exposure met crews arriving
annually from Dundee and New Bedford. Low is blunt: "Disease due to sexual
intercourse has been introduced and has, no doubt, led to many deaths. Other
diseases introduced have carried away numbers of these people" (p. 272). The
Nunavut Parks account of Kekerten says the same thing in plainer words: they
"were also exposed to many of the 'white man's' diseases, and epidemics decimated
their numbers" ([[source:nunavutparks2008kekerten]]).

**They were made dependent, and then the ships left.** Low again, in 1904:

> The natives have for years looked for assistance to the whalers both on Baffin
> island and Hudson bay. They have quite given up the use of their primitive
> weapons, and there is no doubt that a withdrawal of the whalers would lead to
> great hardship and many deaths among these people if the Government did not in
> some manner take their place and supply the Eskimos with the necessary guns and
> ammunition.

(p. 272.) He was writing eight years before the withdrawal happened. Nobody took
their place in time.

## The end, and what is there now

By 1912 the bowhead population of the sound was below the level at which hunting
it paid. By 1917 the whalers were gone. Inuit went on whaling from the station's
boats for about five more years, and Kekerten was abandoned by the mid-1920s.
The Hudson's Bay Company took over the trade in the sound, and in 1921 opened a
post at Panniqtuuq — which is why Pangnirtung is where it is, and why the
descendants of the Kekerten families live there.

Kekerten Island is a National Historic Site of Canada (designated 1985) and a
Nunavut territorial park about 50 km south of Panniqtuuq. What is on the ground
is exactly what a station leaves: hundreds of barrel hoops, wooden barrels,
blubber-hauling pins, several iron trypots, fragments of whaleboats and
buildings, the foundations of the forge where harpoons and flensing gear were
made, the slip where boats were hauled out, tent rings, house foundations,
reconstructed whalebone roof frames of Inuit winter houses, and a whalers'
graveyard. There is also a whale-spotting lookout where the first Union Jack in
the eastern Arctic was raised in 1897 as an assertion of sovereignty — a flag
put up on a hill Inuit had been using to spot whales for the station.

Local guides from Panniqtuuq interpret the site. That is the correct ending for
this page: the history of Kekerten is not held mainly in Aberdeen or Ottawa. It
is held by the families whose grandparents crewed the boats, and it is told at
[[place:Kekerten]] by their descendants.
