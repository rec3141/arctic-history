# The Baffin Bay Circuit, and the tolls of Melville Bay
[page · whaling · draft]

Before 1817 the Davis Strait whalers worked the southern grounds and the West
Greenland coast, and Baffin Bay north of about 74 degrees was a rumour. William
Baffin had gone round the whole of it in 1616 and reported whales; two hundred
years later his map was printed with the note "According to the relation of W.
Baffin in 1616 but not now believed".

In 1817 the ice was unusually open. The *Larkins* of Leith and the *Elizabeth*
of Aberdeen worked north to 77 degrees and crossed to the western side, and the
fishery was never the same again. Within a few seasons the British fleet had
settled into an annual counter-clockwise loop of the entire bay that Richard
Vaughan calls the Baffin Bay Circuit. [[artifact:whaling-017|The track]] on the
map is that loop, with a waypoint for each stage. It is a composite, not any
one ship's course: every master varied it with the ice.

## The stages

Vaughan sets the whole circuit out in a paragraph ([[source:vaughan1986bowhead]],
p. 294). The fleet sailed in March or early April and passed well south of Kap
Farvel to keep clear of the East Greenland ice sweeping round the cape. Then:

- **The Southwest Fishing**, April and May, off Resolution Island and the
  southeast Baffin coast.
- **The East Side**, May, cruising diagonally northeast across Davis Strait to
  the West Greenland coast, and the **Disko** fishery — some ships came straight
  here and were fishing before the end of April. On 4 May 1825 the *Brunswick*
  of Hull had thirty ships in sight off Disko.
- **North along the fast ice**, June, past [[place:Upernavik]] in the lane
  between the land-fast ice and the drifting Middle Ice that filled the centre
  of the bay between about 65 and 75 degrees north.
- **[[place:Melville Bay]]**, late June, entered past the Devil's Thumb
  (Kullorsuaq).
- **Breaking out at [[place:Cape York]] into [[place:The North Water]]**, about
  1 July.
- **The crossing to the West Side**, early July, making land near Cape Byam
  Martin on Bylot Island, at the mouth of [[place:Lancaster Sound]].
- **Pond's Bay**, July and August, off Mittimatalik (Pond Inlet) — the best of
  it. The *Cumbrian* counted forty-one sail here on 14 July 1823.
- **Home from [[place:Cape Dyer]]** in September, for a full ship; and for one
  not full, on south into [[place:Cumberland Sound]] and Frobisher Bay for a
  last effort, "almost in the Southwest Fishing but further north and closer
  inshore than at the start of the season."

At least half the fleet entering Davis Strait each spring crossed to the west
side by this route. In 1828 the Hull ships were through into the North Water by
26 July with forty or forty-five others; the Davis Strait fleet that year was
seventy-nine sail.

## Melville Bay: what the passage actually was

The crossing of Melville Bay was two to seven weeks of manual labour. There is
no engine in this part of the story until the 1860s. A ship worked the lane
between the fast ice and the pack by four methods, all muscular:

- **Boring** — driving under sail into loose floes, with men on the bows
  fending, sometimes the whole crew running side to side on deck to roll the
  ship and break her way through.
- **Tracking** — the crew out on the floe with a rope from the foremast,
  dragging the ship along like a canal barge.
- **Towing** — every boat in the water ahead, pulling.
- **Warping** — running a warp out to an ice anchor set in the floe ahead and
  heaving the ship up to it on the capstan, over and over.

And when the pack closed, **sawing a dock**: measuring out a berth in the fast
ice with the carpenter, and cutting it with long ice-saws worked from tripods.
Tripods twelve feet high, saws fourteen feet long, and a shanty to keep the
stroke: [[artifact:whaling-022|Goodsir watched it done]] and noted that "it is
astonishing how rapidly a well-drilled ship's company will cut through floes,
from six to eight feet thick" ([[source:goodsir1850arctic]], pp. 53-54).

Times varied absurdly. Markham lists Parry taking fifty-four days through
Melville Bay in 1824 and John Ross five days in 1829; Austin took forty-five in
1850, Inglefield in 1852 was not stopped at all. A whaling master reckoned
fifteen to eighteen days' detention a bad year. The dangerous wind was the
southwesterly, which drove the Middle Ice onshore against the fast ice with the
fleet in between.

## What it cost

That is where the fleet was destroyed. Goodsir, who went through in 1849,
gives the roll:

> since 1819, when it became customary for the vessels employed in this trade
> to push thus far north, not a year has passed without being marked by more or
> less damage sustained by the shipping between the 74° and 76° of lat. ... In
> 1819, fourteen ships were thus entirely lost; in 1821, eleven; in 1822,
> seven; but the year 1830 was peculiarly disastrous.

([[source:goodsir1850arctic]], pp. 42-43.)

The wrecks themselves, year by year and ship by ship, are on
[[page:the-whalers-bad-years]], and this page does not repeat them. What belongs
here is the economics of it, which is stranger than the wrecks.

**They kept going in.** Nineteen or twenty ships were destroyed in a week of
June 1830 — [[artifact:whaling-019|Lindsay]] and Goodsir say nineteen and put
the loss at about £140,000, [[artifact:whaling-018|Markham]] says twenty and
names more of them; both figures are in the record and neither is provably
wrong. The next season the fleet went to Melville Bay again. It did so because
the alternative was worse. A ship's owners had spent the winter fitting her out;
a season that turned back at Disko came home clean, and a clean ship was a total
loss on the voyage. The whalers were not being reckless in any way they would
have recognised. They were taking the only bet on the table.

**The men were not paid to be careful either.** A whaleman's wage was small and
most of what he earned came in **striking money** and oil money, paid out on
whales actually taken. Lindsay gives the Aurora's rates: the harpooner got ten
shillings for the gun harpoon and ten and sixpence for the hand harpoon, a
guinea for both; every other man in the boat got half a crown either way. A
crew with nothing struck had nothing extra to take home.

**What ended the toll was steam, not caution.** Compare
[[artifact:whaling-021|Markham in 1873]], aboard the Dundee steam whaler
*Arctic*: Melville Bay crossed with "only a few hours' detention", Cape York in
sight at 5 a.m. and Cape Dudley Digges by ten. He was actually disappointed. The
same passage that killed nineteen ships in 1830 had become a night's steaming.
By then there were thirteen ships left in the whole fishery, all from Dundee.

## The North Water, which is the point of the whole thing

Everything above Melville Bay existed for one reason: at the head of Baffin Bay
there is always open water. [[place:The North Water]] — Pikialasorsuaq, "the
great upwelling" — is the largest polynya in the northern hemisphere, kept open
through the winter by wind and current and an ice bridge across Smith Sound. It
is why there are Inughuit at Cape York, why there are millions of nesting
seabirds on the Greenland side, and why a whaler that got through Melville Bay
by the first of July had a clear run southwest to the fishing grounds while ships
that failed spent the season beating about the Middle Ice.

A ship knew she was through before she saw it. [[artifact:whaling-020|Goodsir's
Advice]], 4 July 1849: they struck out west from Cape Dudley Digges and "were
rejoiced to find that we could not be far from the 'north water,' as the ship
began to 'lift'" — the swell of open sea coming under her through the floes.
They crossed south of the Carey Islands in latitude 76°30' and raised the west
land on the 8th.

Vaughan notes something the whalers themselves knew: there were no whales at the
top of the bay. Baffin's Hvalsund, full of bowheads in 1616, had none in the
nineteenth century, and no fishery was ever worked on the east side north of
Cape York. The North Water was not a whaling ground. It was a road.

## The West Side, and how far in the whalers went

Off the mouth of Lancaster Sound the fleet was in the water this ship works.
Bylot Island, Cape Byam Martin, Cape Graham Moore, Navy Board Inlet, Admiralty
Inlet, [[place:Mittimatalik]] (Pond Inlet) — these are whaling names as much as
naval ones, and the whalers fished among them for eighty years. Markham's
*Arctic* in 1873 rock-nosed the whole of it: past Cape Liverpool and Cape Hay on
27 June, finding Admiralty Inlet blocked solid with heavy ice, into
[[place:Prince Regent Inlet]] on the 28th and 29th, past Leopold Island and Port
Leopold where Ross had wintered in 1848, down to a land-ice barrier running
across the inlet from Batty Bay to Port Bowen, and on 2 August past
[[place:Fury Beach]] close enough to count the casks and spars on the beach and
see two boats hauled up and an anchor. He meant to push up the Gulf of Boothia
and out through Fury and Hecla Strait. The ice would not let him.

The whalers went into these inlets because the whales did — bowheads summer deep
in Lancaster Sound and Prince Regent Inlet — and because a full ship early meant
a second half-season. They did it in ships with no charts worth the name, and
they went further west along the Devon coast, past what is now
[[place:Dundas Harbour]] and [[place:Beechey Island]], than most people realise.
William Penny in the *Saint Andrew* got to 78 degrees west in Lancaster Sound in
1847, looking for Franklin; two years later he tried again in the *Advice* with
Goodsir aboard, in company with Captain Parker's *Truelove*, and the ice stopped
them.

The most famous thing a whaler ever did in these waters was not whaling at all.
On 26 August 1833 the *Isabella* of Hull — a whaler John Ross himself had
commanded in 1818 — picked up Ross and the crew of the *Victory* off Navy Board
Inlet after four winters ashore, a rescue told at
[[page:the-isabella-and-after]]. Whalers were the region's ambulance service
because they were the only ships in it — and the service ran both ways. The
*Isabella*'s master that day was Richard Humphreys. Two years later he lost her
off the Whalefish Islands, and that winter he fitted out HMS *Cove* and sailed
in her as master to look for whalers beset in the ice
([[page:whaling-the-disaster-years]]).

## What the logbooks are worth now

A whaling logbook is a daily ice record kept by a professional whose life
depended on being right: the date the North Water opened, the position of the
Middle Ice edge, how many days Melville Bay held a ship, where the fast ice met
the pack. Hundreds of them survive in Hull, Dundee, Peterhead and at the Scott
Polar Research Institute, unpublished. Vaughan's closing complaint in 1986 was
that nobody had systematically worked them, and that until somebody does, no
correlation can be made between whaling, climate and whale numbers. Forty years
later that is still largely true, and the series would run from 1817 to 1913 —
a century of eyewitness sea-ice observations from the exact waters this ship
crosses.
