# The winter at Etah and the Inughuit
[page · oxford-ellesmere-1934 · draft]

The expedition was landed at [[place:Etah]] on 22 August 1934 because a Norwegian
captain would go no further. It built its hut on part of the site of Donald
MacMillan's Borup Lodge of 1913-17 ([[topic:crocker-land-1913]]) and of a
deserted Inughuit village, and stayed eleven months. Almost nothing in the
published papers about that winter is about the six Europeans; nearly everything
that mattered turned on the people of the Thule district.

## Hiring, and who did the hiring

The Danish administration governed the district and controlled access to its
people. Before the Signalhorn left Robertson Bay on 13 August 1934 the expedition
had to telegraph for permission to take on two men and their wives of its own
choosing instead of the men the Danes had detailed to it. They got
[[person:Nukapinguaq]] and [[person:Enilungwa]], and [[person:Inuatuk]] and
[[person:Natuk]]. [[person:Philip Rosendahl]], the Governor at Thule, had to be
asked again in February 1935 before more hunters could be engaged, and again
before the women at [[place:Siorapaluk]] could be allowed to make the sealskin
boots, mitts, kamiks and dog harness on which every sledge journey depended. At
Siorapaluk, [[person:Enoq]], who kept what Humphreys called certainly the most
northerly store in the New World, took the expedition's word that permission had
been given and set the work going anyway.

The women's work is named once and never described. It is worth saying plainly
that the clothing that kept eight men alive at sixty and seventy degrees of frost
was made by Inughuit women at Siorapaluk, Qaanaaq and Neqe, in the weeks when the
expedition was waiting and complaining about the delay. The
[[artifact:oxford-ellesmere-1934-044|photographs the expedition brought home]]
show what the papers do not: women washing clothes in a tin bath on the hillside,
a woman cutting a man's hair outside a house of wood and stone, children and
Oxford undergraduates playing blind man's buff on the grass, a line of women and
children with a baby in an amaut in front of the base hut. The Scott Polar
Research Institute's catalogue names almost none of them either.

## The walrus

In the autumn of 1934 the expedition shot walrus for its dog-food caches without
harpooning them first, which the North Greenland game laws forbade because an
unsecured walrus sinks. The Inughuit hunters offered to harpoon each animal and
were ignored. In February 1935 Humphreys and Haig-Thomas were summoned to Thule
to answer the charge before a Danish official and an Inughuit council; both got
off, one by pleading ignorance and one, according to
[[source:dick2001muskox|Lyle Dick]], by a false claim that the meat was emergency
food. The story is told in full on [[page:oxford-the-policeman]]. It is not in
any of the expedition's own publications.

## The winter

Freeze-up came on 11 October 1934 and the sun set on the 24th. Fifty-five dogs
came over the ice from Dog Island, where several had died of neglect and hunger
because the wind kept the boats ashore; the number fell to forty-seven when
poisonous shark flesh turned up in the sacks of dried fish bought in West
Greenland. February 1935 was the coldest month, with -37 F on the 4th — milder,
in fact, than MacMillan's four years at the same place. The wireless brought
messages relayed from Pittsburgh and Winnipeg and dance tunes from the BBC.

Twice in November Humphreys, Stallworthy and the hunters tried to reach
Robertson Bay over the ice-cap and were beaten by blizzards. The Inughuit did not
want to travel by ice-cap at all and preferred to wait for the sea ice; the Etah
wind kept breaking it up. On 28 January 1935 Humphreys pressed them to try again,
and he, Haig-Thomas, Nukapinguaq and Inuatuk crossed by moonlight to Neqe, on to
[[place:Robertson Fjord]], through Qaanaaq and down to
[[place:Uummannaq (Thule)]], sleeping in Inughuit houses the whole way and
travelling much of it in company with local sledges — one man carrying a small
boy south to the hospital for treatment for tuberculosis. Coming back on 21
February the party was caught in a blizzard so strong that no iglu could be
built; the Inughuit dug a hole in the ice-cap and cut two caves out of it, six
and a half feet square and four feet high, and six people lay in them for three
days.

## The month that was lost

March went in waiting. The hunters would not start north until their dogs were
in condition, and there was no dog food: the caches laid by boat in the autumn
had been raided by foxes and a bear, and part of one had been eaten by
Nukapinguaq, who instead of taking his tubercular wife to the Thule hospital had
camped south of Etah to hunt walrus and failed. The Greenland sledges were not
finished until 17 March. A relay attempt over the land on the 19th ended with
everyone exhausted, the sledges too heavy and the ice impassable.

Then on 30 March, at half past two in the morning, eight sledges came in with
seventeen people and [[person:Dr Holm]] from the Thule hospital, on his round of
the outlying villages. Two more arrived next day. With the families, thirty-two
people were living at Etah. Ten hunters agreed to go north. The expedition
cooked for them, issued them food and nursed the sick, and its three journeys
began within the week.

## Who went

Besides the permanently employed men Inuatuk and [[person:Porsman]] — who had
replaced [[person:Apilaq]] when Apilaq went down with lobular pneumonia — the
hunters named in the papers are [[person:Ahnowka]], [[person:Kuchiketo]],
[[person:Kokochea]], [[person:Utah]], [[person:Kokachingwa]] and
[[person:Sakeus]], Nukapinguaq's son. The police record, which Lyle Dick worked
from, spells several of these differently and adds two men the Geographical
Journal papers do not name at all: [[person:Rasmus]] and [[person:Macheto]], with
Bentham's and Shackleton's party. Whether Rasmus is Porsman under another
rendering is unresolved. Porsman had sledged round North Greenland
with Knud Rasmussen: [[artifact:oxford-ellesmere-1934-022|a starvation trip of
which he had vivid memories]]. Nukapinguaq had driven for Inspector A. H. Joy of
the RCMP, who
[[artifact:oxford-ellesmere-1934-026|travelled as a passenger]], and on the
searches for Krueger.

## Afterwards

On 27 June 1935 Nukapinguaq came back over the ice-cap with his family, seven in
all, and settled at Etah. It was Enilungwa who woke the hut at half past one in
the morning of 22 August to say the relief ship was in sight. The expedition
gave a farewell party, handed over its surviving dogs, and left on the 25th.
Etah was abandoned as a settlement in the following decades; the district's
people were later moved from Uummannaq when the American air base was built at
[[place:Pituffik]] in 1953, which belongs to [[topic:greenland-inughuit]].
