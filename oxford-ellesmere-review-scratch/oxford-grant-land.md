# The sledge journey to Grant Land
[page · oxford-ellesmere-1934 · draft]

At noon on 3 April 1935, in a gale, four men drove north from the top of the
ravine behind [[place:Etah]]: [[person:A. W. Moore]], Sergeant
[[person:Harry Stallworthy]], [[person:Nukapinguaq]] and [[person:Inuatuk]], with
two more Inughuit sledges carrying dog food as far as Cape Calhoun. Their
objective was Cape Columbia on the polar sea, some 500 miles away across country
nobody had crossed. The route and the day-by-day positions are in
[[artifact:oxford-ellesmere-1934-002|the track]].

## North along Greenland

Twelve hours took them to the main depot at [[place:Cache Point]], where the
thermometer read -32 F and Humphreys's party lay asleep. Loads averaged about
700 lb a sledge. They crossed [[place:Peabody Bay]] in front of the Humboldt
Glacier, taking four seals at their breathing holes, and met two Inughuit hunters
coming south with starving dogs — killing some to feed the others, one man's
cheeks stripped raw by frostbite. The open water at [[place:Cape Calhoun]], on
which the Thule hunters count every spring, had not formed that year. The
supporting sledges turned back there.

Moore's account is unusually direct about who was actually navigating:
[[artifact:oxford-ellesmere-1934-052|often Nukapinguaq would gallop ahead to an
iceberg, sit motionless on top with the glasses for what seemed an age, then take
the lead again with the route for the next few hours locked up in his brain]].
Crossing [[place:Kennedy Channel]] in a
snowstorm, Moore wrote,
[[artifact:oxford-ellesmere-1934-020|he could see only a few yards ahead but never faltered]].

## Fort Conger and Lake Hazen

They crawled into [[place:Fort Conger]] with exhausted dogs and rested two days
in [[artifact:oxford-ellesmere-1934-033|Peary's huts]], flying the Union Jack
over them, leaving a record in a cairn on the hills behind, and burning coal from
the seam the Nares expedition had found in 1875. Stallworthy and Moore took the
hut Dedrick had used and Nukapinguaq and Inuatuk the one that had been Henson's,
and dried out clothing and sleeping bags. A cache laid for Roald Amundsen was
mostly ruined, but its pemmican was sound and went on the sledges. (Moore's paper
dates that cache 1917; [[source:dick2001muskox|Lyle Dick]] says Godfred Hansen
left it in 1919, for the airship attempt on the Pole that never came. The two
have not been reconciled here.) No seal showed in the harbour. This is the ground
of [[topic:nares-greely]]: Greely's men had starved on the same coast fifty years
before.

Three days inland brought them to the east end of [[place:Lake Hazen]] and the
one thing the whole plan rested on — fish. The ice was four to five feet thick,
and within minutes of arriving they had holes bored and Arctic char coming up.
But the char ran six inches to a foot, far smaller than Peary's account had
promised, and it took nearly twenty-four hours of fishing a day to feed the dogs
at all. After five days it was clear the party could not all go on.

The new plan was two men, one sledge and the best dogs. Stallworthy — the most
experienced Arctic traveller of the eight, and the man Canada had lent them —
gave up his claim to the final effort and stayed at the lake with Inuatuk to
fish. It was a genuine sacrifice and Moore said so.

## The farthest point

On 29 April Moore and Nukapinguaq crossed to the north side of the lake and
climbed a winding valley behind the [[place:Garfield Range]] in snow that took
them to the thigh, one man breaking trail while the other drove. It was the worst
going of the whole journey. Next day they got up
[[artifact:oxford-ellesmere-1934-034|the Gilman Glacier]] at its western edge,
cutting steps and carrying the load up, and camped at 3,000 feet on the ice-cap,
Nukapinguaq building a wall of snow blocks on the windward side; they slept in
the open in their bags.

On about 1 May they climbed a prominent peak — the last 300 feet along a
razor-edged ridge — and stood at 9,000 feet looking north over
[[place:Grant Land]]. Beyond stretched a range Moore judged at 10,000 feet or
more. They named it the [[place:British Empire Range]] and flew the Duke of
York's Union Jack from the summit. Nukapinguaq pointed out, through the glasses,
the sea ice of Clements Markham Inlet on the north coast.

They had pemmican for one more day out. Moore listened to Nukapinguaq's opinion
that the dogs could not go on, studied the ground ahead through the glasses,
and turned back. The farthest was about
[[artifact:oxford-ellesmere-1934-002|82 degrees 25 minutes north]] — the highest
latitude reached by the expedition, and short of Cape Columbia by the whole width
of an unmapped mountain range. [[place:Mount Oxford]], in the British Empire
Range, carries the university's name; the paper itself does not name the peak
Moore climbed.

## Home on starving dogs

The return was a hungry business. Three caribou shot by Nukapinguaq below the
glacier were the first meat since Peabody Bay. At Fort Conger two dogs had to be
shot; a sledge was abandoned and the party went on with two teams. The oil ran
out and they cooked on seal blubber and on the boards of their own sledges,
Stallworthy coming up black from the smoke. At Cape Calhoun there was open water
at last, and Inuatuk crossed thin ice to save a large seal that gave the dogs
about 600 lb of meat; they ate until they swelled and slept for a day.

They drove into Etah on 26 May 1935 in the worst snowstorm of the journey, after
about 900 miles, having missed by inches a boulder dislodged on to the fjord ice
by the sledge above them. There is
[[artifact:oxford-ellesmere-1934-031|one photograph of the four of them together
afterwards]], in their fur clothing beside a laden sledge: Nukapinguaq,
Stallworthy, Moore, Inuatuk.

## What it settled

Very little was mapped: Moore had no chronometer, the survey instruments having
gone with the geological party. What the journey did establish was that the
interior of northern Ellesmere Island is a mountain country on a scale nobody had
allowed for, and that a sledge party cannot live off Lake Hazen. Both lessons
were still worth having twenty years later, when Canadian and British parties
came back to the same lake for the International Geophysical Year:
[[topic:operation-hazen-1957]].
