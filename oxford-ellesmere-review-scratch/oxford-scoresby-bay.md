# The Scoresby Bay journey
[page · oxford-ellesmere-1934 · draft]

The geological party — [[person:Edward Shackleton]] and [[person:Robert Bentham]],
with [[person:Porsman]] and [[person:Ahnowka]] as the two older men who went the
whole way, and [[person:Sakeus]] and another younger man in support — left
[[place:Etah]] on 4 April 1935 and came back on 24 May after about 800 miles.
[[artifact:oxford-ellesmere-1934-004|The track]] gives the daily positions.

## Across Smith Sound on someone else's trail

They crossed [[place:Refuge Harbour]], MacMillan's old winter quarters, in an
icy wind and drove into camp frost-bitten; at [[place:Cache Point]] they spent a
day polishing scored steel runners and collecting the Bovril dog pemmican. Then
out on to the sea ice in sixty and seventy degrees of frost, running behind the
sledges to keep warm — and largely on the trail Humphreys's party had chopped the
day before, which is why they made [[place:Fram Havn]] in good time.

Next morning Sakeus complained of a pain as if his eye were full of iron filings.
That is exactly how snow-blindness feels, so they assumed snow-blindness; it was
in fact an iron filing, from polishing the runners. They bandaged his eyes, put
him on Porsman's sledge, and made a forced march of over fifty miles through to
the abandoned police post at the mouth of [[place:Flagler Bay]], where
[[person:Noel Humphreys]] anaesthetised the eye and took it out. Everyone fell
asleep on the sledges on the way.

## The Sven Hedin Glacier

On 13 April Shackleton took two sledges up Flagler Fjord and over the neck of the
peninsula — not a mile and a half wide at that point, though the maps made it
more — to look at the glaciers discharging into [[place:Princess Marie Bay]]. The
Sven Hedin Glacier in [[place:Woodward Bay]] was not the easy ramp on to the
ice-cap they had hoped for: a snout at least sixty feet high, scored by huge
crevasses, with talus in front and a badly crevassed terminal moraine — two men stand at the foot of that wall of broken ice
in [[artifact:oxford-ellesmere-1934-042|one of the expedition's photographs]].
Shackleton
read the convexity of the snout, the talus and the moraine as the signs of an
advancing glacier — a small observation, but the kind of thing a glaciologist
working the same fiord today can check against a 1935 baseline. It was a
seventy-mile day and they were back at Bache within twenty-four hours.
Bentham had meanwhile found Cambrian fossils in
[[artifact:oxford-ellesmere-1934-011|the gully behind the post]] — the material
[[person:Christian Poulsen]] described in 1946, founding four new trilobite
genera on it and on what came from further north. Their base was
[[artifact:oxford-ellesmere-1934-032|the empty police detachment itself]], closed
by Ottawa in 1933 and still flying a Union Jack from a cairn at the door.

## North into the worst ice

There is a photograph of this stretch of coast from the sledge:
[[artifact:oxford-ellesmere-1934-041|good going on the sea ice, heading for Cape
Louis Napoleon on the Judge Darling Peninsula]].

On 25 April they emptied the depot at [[place:Cape Albert]] and headed out into
Kane Basin. Three days made eighty miles. The fourth gave them a day of
pressure ridges twenty-five feet high, axes in continuous use, runners burying
themselves in the hollows and dogs struggling on their bellies — and then a huge
palaeocrystic floe, thick and weathered into an undulating country of small hills
and valleys, hard-packed, over which they drove for hours.

Near [[place:Hayes Point]] the ice stopped them altogether and they forced three
miles in to the mainland to find an ice-foot, arriving drenched with sweat at
midnight with their beards and eyelashes white. By chance they had hit a
fossiliferous shore and Bentham took Silurian trilobites, gastropods and
brachiopods. On the [[place:Darling Peninsula]] they crossed a flat, wind-free
shingle plain behind a ten-foot gravel bank and christened it Aerodrome Bay,
thinking it would serve aeroplanes at any season — which, thirteen years later,
was more or less how the archipelago did get surveyed: [[topic:qei-from-the-air-1947]].

South of Cape Fraser the ice-foot disappeared under blocks driven thirty to forty
feet up the hillside. Shackleton wrote that he had never imagined anything so
devastating as the power that could do it, and noted that this was the sea
through which Peary and others had forced their ships:
[[artifact:oxford-ellesmere-1934-010|pressure ridge on the ice-foot]]. They
camped three miles short of [[place:Scoresby Bay]]. In the night a mass of the
ice-foot fell on their floe and shattered it in all directions.

## What was at Scoresby Bay

On about 1 May Shackleton took a light sledge up the bay and found it was not a
bay. It ran further and further south, an inlet rather than a bay, and as he
came round the last corner
[[artifact:oxford-ellesmere-1934-024|the whole dazzling range of the Victoria and Albert Mountains sprang into view]] —
foothills rising straight out of the sea ice, the highest peaks seven or eight
miles off where the map put them at least twenty miles inland. Nares had placed
them from the deck of his ship, and the error had stood since 1876. One peak rose
over 5,000 feet, ringed by ice at about 2,000 feet flowing from the ice-cap; the
plates give it as
[[artifact:oxford-ellesmere-1934-012|approximately 6,000 feet]].

The attempt on that peak failed. Bentham, without snowshoes, floundered four
hours up a foothill through two feet of soft snow under a wind crust that bore
one foot and dropped the other, felt snow-blindness coming on, and struggled
back. Shackleton set up a Stratton two-valve receiver at Cape Norton Shaw, got
time signals and, incidentally, news bulletins from Daventry, Pittsburgh,
Philadelphia, Schenectady and Cincinnati — but it was too cold for observations
that would improve on existing data, and then he fell ill with what he thought
was poisoning. Bentham's last day's work showed the whole of Scoresby Bay to be
Silurian as far north as Cape M'Clintock.

## Back

The dogs had had one Arctic hare between them for the entire journey and the last
pemmican went at [[place:Cape Hawks]]. A fifty-mile forced march without sleep
brought them to Cape Albert on 8 May and to [[place:Bache Peninsula]] on the 9th,
where the hunters went straight out and killed four seal. After five more days of
Bentham's collecting in Princess Marie Bay and a fruitless search by Shackleton
for Inughuit remains in [[place:Alexandra Fiord]], they left for Etah on 18 May.

In mid-[[place:Smith Sound]] on the 20th the blizzard caught them. The land went;
the sledges vanished in a flash; it took five men to raise the tent and a quarter
of an hour later they could not have done it. They lay thirty-six hours and found
afterwards that they had been camped within half a mile of open water. They
reached Etah in the early hours of 24 May. The Inughuit, anxious for their
sealing grounds, left the same day, and Shackleton wrote of Porsman that
[[artifact:oxford-ellesmere-1934-025|it was his energy, endurance and cheerfulness, and that of his companions, that brought them such small success as they attained]].
