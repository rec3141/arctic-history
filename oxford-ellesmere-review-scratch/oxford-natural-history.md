# Birds, plants, the sea and the weather at Etah
[page · oxford-ellesmere-1934 · draft]

The expedition was not, in the end, much of a surveying party — that is on
[[page:oxford-what-they-mapped]]. What it did bring home was a season's worth of
observation of living things at a latitude where almost nobody had spent a whole
year, and eleven months of temperatures at a place where nobody now lives.

## Birds, plants and the sea

[[person:David Haig-Thomas]] found Etah disappointing, since MacMillan had
already described its birds over four years
([[topic:crocker-land-1913]]). His winter notes are still useful: wheatears
still by the lake at the beginning of November, and dead ones later near the hut,
which he took to mean they had left migration too late; ptarmigan, ravens and
Mandt's guillemots seen or heard in every month of the winter; ptarmigan crops
full of willow buds; ravens living on the offal of foxes and dogs; and no
explanation at all for what the guillemots were eating at the ice edge, since he
could find no plankton. On Ellesmere Island in April and May he saw only snow
buntings, a Greenland falcon and ravens. In the summer at
[[place:McCormick Bay]] he added the Lapland bunting and the golden plover to
North Greenland's list — the plover shot at Etah being the most northerly ever
recorded, the bunting the most northerly on the American continent — and found a
colony of thirty to sixty pairs of knot. He hunted, without success, for the nest
of the greater snow goose, which has never been found in northern Greenland,
though a goose shot by an Inughuk had plainly been sitting.

[[person:A. J. Wilmott]] at the British Museum received about three hundred
numbers of dried plants, most of them collected by Humphreys around Etah through
a whole flowering season — the first flower, purple saxifrage, on 9 June 1935 —
with material from Cape Alexander, Hatherton Bay, Robertson Bay and the head of
Foulke Fjord. [[person:A. W. Moore]] dredged in Foulke Fjord at twenty to seventy
feet in July 1935 and brought back crustaceans, echinoderms, small fish and a few
molluscs, reported on by [[person:Charles Elton]] in the *Annals and Magazine of
Natural History* in 1937, in the same issue that carried
[[person:G. D. H. Carpenter]]'s notes on the insects. Neither paper is held here
— Taylor and Francis returns a browser challenge to the library's proxy — and a
request has been filed for both.

The survey work was also, in the expedition's own framing, a stock-take: an
assessment of the musk-ox and caribou populations Peary's parties had depleted.
There are [[artifact:oxford-ellesmere-1934-038|ten photographs of musk-oxen in
western Ellesmere Island]] from Humphreys's and Haig-Thomas's crossing, several
showing the defensive ring, and those 1935 counts are what Haig-Thomas used as
his baseline when he came back to the same fjords in 1938
([[topic:haig-thomas-1937]]). That makes them the earliest usable comparison
point for musk-ox numbers in the interior of the island.

## Climate, and the reason it matters

Shackleton's climate appendix compares the expedition's Etah temperatures month
by month with MacMillan's four years at the same place, and reaches a conclusion
about the region rather than the weather: the open water that stretches from the
edge of the Smith Sound pack south to Cape York holds a constant temperature just
below freezing a little under the surface and warms the whole coast, while the
pack edge — running from about the latitude of [[place:Cairn Point]] across to
[[place:Pim Island]] — marks a real climatic boundary, with much lower
temperatures a few miles north of it, noticed independently by all three sledge
parties. Etah itself he judged a poor base: warmer than
[[place:Bache Peninsula]] by fifteen degrees in the winter mean, but ruined by
katabatic winds off the ice-cap, while Bache is normally windless and its
problem is deep snow on the sea ice. [[place:Robertson Fjord]] he thought the
best of the three.

For a ship in these waters that is the most directly usable thing the expedition
left: a described position for the Smith Sound ice edge in 1934-35, a
wind-and-snow contrast across the sound, and eleven months of temperatures at a
place where nobody now lives.
