# The Oxford University Ellesmere Land Expedition, 1934-35
[page · oxford-ellesmere-1934 · draft]

Six young men from Oxford, an RCMP sergeant lent by Canada, and between two and
twelve Inughuit hunters from the Thule district spent the winter of 1934-35 in a
two-storey hut eighteen feet by twelve at [[place:Etah]] in north-west Greenland,
and in the spring of 1935 sledged some 2,500 miles between them across
[[place:Smith Sound]] and over [[place:Ellesmere Island]]. It was the first
British party to work in the far north of the Canadian archipelago since Nares
in 1875-76 ([[topic:nares-greely]]), and the last expedition of its kind: dogs,
sledges, sealskin lines, a two-valve wireless set, and no aircraft at all.

## How it started, and how it went wrong at once

The expedition came out of the Oxford University Exploration Club.
[[person:Edward Shackleton]], second son of Sir Ernest, and [[person:A. W. Moore]]
had been on the Club's Sarawak expedition in 1932 and wanted an Arctic one;
[[person:Noel Humphreys]], a doctor and surveyor known for two Royal
Geographical Society papers on Ruwenzori, joined them and was made leader while
Shackleton was made organiser. [[person:Robert Bentham]] came as geologist and
wireless operator and [[person:David Haig-Thomas]] as ornithologist. The
Canadian government gave the permits, some money, and the loan of
[[person:Harry Stallworthy]] of the Royal Canadian Mounted Police, who had
thirteen years in the Canadian Arctic, spoke Inuktitut, and in 1932 had led the
search out of Bache Peninsula for the lost German geologist
[[person:Hans Krueger]] ([[topic:kruger-1930]]).

They chartered a hundred-ton Norwegian sealer, the Signalhorn, sailed from St
Katherine's Dock on 17 July 1934, and meant to land at [[place:Fort Conger]] in
Grant Land, or failing that at the abandoned police post on
[[place:Bache Peninsula]]. They reached neither. On 14 August the pack lay solid
across Smith Sound; on the 17th, after a second attempt, the captain told them
that unless they would be put ashore at Etah he must go south at once. They were
landed at Etah, some 250 miles short of Fort Conger, on the wrong side of the
sound, with seventy tons of stores below the high-water mark and a hut in
sections. [[artifact:oxford-ellesmere-1934-006|The Signalhorn among the floes]]
is in the plates.

Everything that followed was shaped by that. Etah proved a bad wintering place:
the walrus no longer came into the fjord, the large land game had long been
hunted out, and the katabatic winds off the ice-cap kept breaking up the young
sea ice so that the party could not sledge north to its own caches. Sixteen of
seventy-one dogs died before the winter was out. The three spring journeys
started a month late.

## The three journeys

In April 1935 the expedition split into three parties, each of two Europeans and
four Inughuit, two of whom went in support and turned back early.

[[page:oxford-grant-land]] — Moore, Stallworthy, [[person:Nukapinguaq]] and
[[person:Inuatuk]] went north up the Greenland ice-foot, across
[[place:Kennedy Channel]] to [[place:Fort Conger]], inland to
[[place:Lake Hazen]], and then, cut down to two men and one sledge for want of
dog food, over the [[place:United States Range]] to about 82 degrees 25 minutes
north. About 900 miles. They named the [[place:British Empire Range]].

[[page:oxford-across-ellesmere]] — Humphreys and Haig-Thomas, with
[[person:Kuchiketo]], [[person:Kokochea]], [[person:Utah]] and
[[person:Kokachingwa]], twice failed to cross the Grinnell Land ice-cap and
instead crossed Ellesmere Island twice by its valleys, reaching
[[place:Bay Fiord]] and a fjord on the south coast, and dug at the old village
on Haa Island. About 800 miles.

[[page:oxford-scoresby-bay]] — Shackleton and Bentham, with [[person:Porsman]],
[[person:Ahnowka]] and [[person:Sakeus]], worked the east coast north to
[[place:Scoresby Bay]], through some of the worst ice on the coast, and found
the Victoria and Albert Mountains standing at the head of the inlet rather than
twenty miles inland where Nares had put them. About 800 miles.

[[page:oxford-etah-winter]] covers the winter itself, and the Inughuit families
without whom none of it happened. [[page:oxford-what-they-mapped]] covers the
survey, the map corrections and the geology, and
[[page:oxford-natural-history]] the birds, the plants, the dredgings and the
weather.
[[page:oxford-the-policeman]] covers Sergeant Stallworthy, the abandoned police
posts the parties sheltered in, and the walrus case the Royal Geographical
Society papers do not mention.

## Two accounts of the same expedition

The three papers read at the Royal Geographical Society on 18 November 1935 are
the expedition's own account, and they are modest and readable. There is a second
account, from the Royal Canadian Mounted Police files, and it is colder.
[[source:dick2001muskox|Lyle Dick]] calls the British university expeditions of
the 1930s "of minor importance to Arctic science" and reads them as romantic
adventure-seeking in the twilight of the empire — quoting the party's own
description of Ellesmere Island as "the most northerly part of the British
Empire". His chapter records what the papers leave out: the walrus shot at Etah
in the autumn of 1934 without being harpooned first, contrary to the North
Greenland game laws, and the summons of Humphreys and Haig-Thomas in February
1935 to answer for it before a Danish official and an Inughuit council at
[[place:Uummannaq (Thule)]]. He also gives the Inughuit names as the police
spelled them — Anauka, Rasmus, Sakius, Macheto, Kuuttiktittoq, Kakutiak, Uutaaq,
Kakachinguaq — beside the spellings the British used, and there are more men in
his list than in theirs.

Both accounts are in this topic, and where they disagree the disagreement is
said.

## What it was worth

Humphreys's own summing-up was modest: they had penetrated unexplored Ellesmere
Island at five points, made collections, and gained a great deal of experience.
Nobody reached Cape Columbia; nobody crossed a peninsula near its centre. The
solid gains were the correction of the Victoria and Albert Mountains, the first
description of the interior valleys of western Ellesmere Island, Bentham's
Cambrian, Ordovician and Silurian collections from a coast almost untouched
since Per Schei in 1899, and about three hundred numbers of North Greenland
plants.

The collection that outlasted everything else was the rock. In 1946 the
Copenhagen palaeontologist [[person:Christian Poulsen]] described Bentham's
Cambrian and Lower Ordovician material from the west side of Kane Basin and
[[artifact:oxford-ellesmere-1934-049|founded four new trilobite genera, ten new
trilobite species and two new brachiopod species on it]], redetermining Per
Schei's collections from the same coast at the same time. Those names are still
in use. Nothing else the expedition did is cited today.

The other lasting thing is the photographs.
[[artifact:oxford-ellesmere-1934-030|One thousand and eighteen of them]] are in
the Scott Polar Research Institute's Picture Library as collection P66/17: the
ship, the ice, the empty police posts, the sledge parties, and several hundred
pictures of the Inughuit families at Etah whom the published papers barely name.

The expedition also left a name on the map — [[place:Mount Oxford]] — and a
generation of travellers: Haig-Thomas went back with Nukapinguaq in 1937-38
([[topic:haig-thomas-1937]]), Bentham returned to southern Ellesmere Island for
two more seasons and published
[[artifact:oxford-ellesmere-1934-048|a structural account of the south of the
island]] in 1941, and Shackleton wrote
[[source:shackleton1937arctic|Arctic Journeys]] and went into politics. In
November 1937 Shackleton put a far larger scheme to the Northwest Territories
Council — a base at [[place:Robertson Fjord]], an advance party at
[[place:Lake Hazen]], four aircraft, flights to the Pole of Inaccessibility,
and a guarantee of a quarter to half a million dollars. He conceded that the
principal object was not scientific investigation but the search for new land,
and argued British sovereignty was paramount. Canada said no: the Deputy
Commissioner's view was that developing social services among the Inuit would do
more for sovereignty than aerial expeditions. Twenty years later the Defence
Research Board did much of what Shackleton had proposed, from Lake Hazen
([[topic:operation-hazen-1957]]).

For a ship working in the Queen Elizabeth Islands today the most useful thing in
the record is probably the ice: where the pack lay in Smith Sound in August 1934
and August 1935, where the ice-foot held and where it collapsed, and where
[[artifact:oxford-ellesmere-1934-010|pressure ridges]] reached forty feet up a
hillside. See [[artifact:oxford-ellesmere-1934-005|the expedition's map]] and the
four track artifacts for the routes.
