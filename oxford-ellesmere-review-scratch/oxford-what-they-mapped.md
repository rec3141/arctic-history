# What the expedition mapped and what it found in the rocks
[page · oxford-ellesmere-1934 · draft]

The expedition's own verdict, in [[person:Noel Humphreys]]'s last paragraph, was
that it had penetrated unexplored Ellesmere Island at five points, made
collections, and gained a great deal of experience. That is a fair summary, and
this page sets out what the five points and the collections actually amounted to.
The whole of it is in [[artifact:oxford-ellesmere-1934-016|the Grant Land paper and its six appendices]]
and on [[artifact:oxford-ellesmere-1934-005|the expedition's map]].

## Survey

Not much, and the papers are honest about why. There were two surveyors,
Humphreys and [[person:Edward Shackleton]], but only one chronometer and one
portable wireless set, and both went with the geological party; Humphreys and
[[person:David Haig-Thomas]] worked west with a single prismatic compass.
Shackleton got good time signals at Cape Norton Shaw on a two-valve receiver but
the days he had were too cold for a series of sun observations that would improve
on existing data, and he was ill for the rest. The positions in the four track
artifacts here are therefore compass-and-clock reckoning, good to perhaps ten
kilometres, not survey.

Three map corrections did come out of it, and they are real:

- The Victoria and Albert Mountains stand at the head of
  [[place:Scoresby Bay]], seven or eight miles from the sea ice, not twenty miles
  inland where Nares had placed them from a ship's deck
  ([[artifact:oxford-ellesmere-1934-024|Shackleton's account]]).
- Scoresby Bay is a long inlet running south, not the bay on the chart.
- At the head of [[place:Franklin Pierce Bay]] there is no glacier, though the
  Admiralty chart showed one coming down from the ice-cap; and a charted island
  off the south-west cape of [[place:Cook Peninsula]] is not there, while an
  uncharted islet stands in Woodward Bay.

Against that, the one fjord identification the expedition tried to make it could
not agree on: Humphreys took the fjord reached on 4 May 1935 for
[[place:Vendom Fiord]], Haig-Thomas and [[person:Nukapinguaq]] for
[[place:Trold Fiord]], and the disagreement is printed as a footnote under
Humphreys's own text. The [[place:British Empire Range]] was named from a
9,000-foot summit north of [[place:Lake Hazen]] and the name stuck.

## Geology

[[person:Robert Bentham]]'s appendix is the substantial scientific result. He
worked two areas.

Around [[place:Etah]] and [[place:Foulke Fjord]] everything is Precambrian, in
the two formations Lauge Koch had named — the older Etah Formation of igneous,
metamorphic and sedimentary rocks, and the younger Thule Formation of shallow
water and desert sandstones, some of them, Bentham thought, strikingly like the
Torridonian of Scotland, with dolerite sills, ripple marks and sun-cracked
shales. He read a series of seven river terraces at the head of Foulke Fjord, at
40, 90, 125, 200, 290, 350 and 400 feet, as evidence that Greenland here is still
rising isostatically after the Quaternary ice load — a total post-Precambrian
uplift of about 2,000 feet, at least 400 of it geologically recent. That is a
measurement a modern GPS or tide-gauge record can be set against directly.

On the Ellesmere side, only Feilden with Nares in 1875-76 and
[[person:Per Schei]] with Sverdrup in 1899 had collected before him. Bentham
found Cambrian trilobites in one horizon in the ravine behind the police post on
[[place:Bache Peninsula]]
([[artifact:oxford-ellesmere-1934-011|the gully]]); cephalopod-rich beds between
Maury Bay and Cape Norton Shaw that he called almost certainly Ordovician, with a
coral fauna of Favosites and Halysites above; and Silurian limestones through the
whole of Scoresby Bay as far north as Cape M'Clintock. He also mapped the
structure: Bache Peninsula an almost undissected plateau of 1,200 to 1,500 feet
little disturbed since the peneplanation of its Archaean basement, with the
biggest fault system in the peninsula having formed Harmsworth Bay, and, north of
Princess Marie Bay, Alpine country in an advanced state of dissection.
Somewhere in this work a photograph was taken of
[[artifact:oxford-ellesmere-1934-043|a hand pointing at a fossil in the rock]];
the catalogue does not say where.

The fossils went to specialists, and this is where the expedition's one lasting
result came from. In 1946 [[person:Christian Poulsen]] of Copenhagen published
[[artifact:oxford-ellesmere-1934-049|his description of the Cambrian and Lower
Ordovician material]] in the *Quarterly Journal of the Geological Society of
London*. Mostly brachiopods and trilobites, collected between Bache Peninsula
and Scoresby Bay, they yielded four new trilobite genera, ten new trilobite
species and two new brachiopod species; Poulsen redetermined and redescribed Per
Schei's collections from the same coast at the same time, so the two expeditions'
material was worked up together, forty-seven years apart. No copy of that paper
is held here: the Lyell Collection refuses an anonymous fetch and the university
has no licence to it, so a request has been filed and the species are not yet
named on this page.

Bentham himself went back. Between September 1936 and August 1938 he worked two
seasons out of the police post at [[place:Craig Harbour]], and his
[[artifact:oxford-ellesmere-1934-048|1941 paper on the structure and glaciers of
southern Ellesmere Island]] carries forward both the Ordovician beds he had seen
near Scoresby Bay in 1935 and Humphreys's coal seams in Bay Fiord, which he
suspected might be older than the Tertiary coals known elsewhere on the island.
Nobody has yet brought a fossil back from them.

Humphreys's party brought back coal from a vertical seam about two yards wide on
[[place:Bay Fiord]], fossil wood, and fossil-bearing rock from the divide of the
Ellesmere Land crossing — the western coal that has been noticed by every party
through that country since.

The birds, the plants, the dredgings and the eleven months of weather are on
[[page:oxford-natural-history]].
