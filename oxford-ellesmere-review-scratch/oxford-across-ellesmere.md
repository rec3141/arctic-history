# Across Ellesmere Island: Humphreys and Haig-Thomas in Grinnell Land and Ellesmere Land
[page · oxford-ellesmere-1934 · draft]

[[person:Noel Humphreys]] and [[person:David Haig-Thomas]] left [[place:Etah]] on
2 April 1935, the first party away, with [[person:Kuchiketo]],
[[person:Kokochea]] and two supporting men, [[person:Utah]] and
[[person:Kokachingwa]]. They had one prismatic compass between them: the
chronometer and the portable wireless had gone to the geological party. Their
plan was to get into the interior of Grinnell Land, which no one had crossed.
[[artifact:oxford-ellesmere-1934-003|The track]] carries the daily positions.

## Two failures on the ice-cap

They crossed [[place:Smith Sound]] by way of [[place:Pim Island]] and
[[place:Fram Havn]], drew stores from the abandoned police depots there and at
[[place:Bache Peninsula]], and worked north along the ice-foot past
[[place:Victoria Head]] to [[place:Franklin Pierce Bay]]. At the head of the bay
the Admiralty chart showed a glacier coming down from the main ice-cap. Driving
up the valley they got into snow that reached the thigh and then the waist, left
the sledges, walked on into the night, climbed a cliff at the head of the valley
to look north — and found no glacier at all. They also found fresh musk ox tracks
and dung, in a part of Ellesmere Island where musk ox were believed exterminated.

The second try was up the [[place:Parrish Glacier]] at the head of Copes Bay. The
glacier itself was easy, with just enough snow for a near-perfect surface, and on
17 April they drove straight up it and camped on the ice-cap. There they were
stopped by weather and by their companions' judgement — the Inughuit would not
travel on days they thought would turn — and then by the snow: two days of four
and five hours' pushing made almost no ground, the runners sank out of sight, and
the dogs could not restart a stopped sledge. They came down in five hours. From
the high camp they had seen, about fifty miles west, a single high conical peak,
and took a bearing on it. Nobody has yet matched that bearing to a named
mountain in this record.

## Across the island by the valleys

What they did instead is the more interesting result. Twice they crossed
Ellesmere Island by its low interior valleys, which is how the Inughuit crossed
it and how the RCMP patrols crossed it.

The first crossing, at the end of April, went up [[place:Flagler Bay]] over glare
ice in a wind that blew the sledges sideways, then along the isthmus to
[[place:Bay Fiord]], past the pillar of [[place:Thumb Mountain]] which guards, as
Humphreys put it, the western portal of the land crossing. A glacier from the
south had pushed into the valley and dammed a lake of ice talus; the party made a
detour, though Haig-Thomas walked the face and judged that a couple of hours'
chopping would let sledges straight through.

The west of the island was another country. The hills are low, the ice-cap comes
nowhere near the sea, and there are wide undulating areas of vegetation. [[artifact:oxford-ellesmere-1934-038|Herds of
musk ox]] stood out intensely black against light brown rock on both sides of Bay
Fiord; there were caribou, very tame; coal outcropped in a vertical seam about
two yards wide, and there was fossil wood. Only one Arctic wolf was seen in the
whole expedition, here, following the hunters back to camp.

The second crossing, on 4 May, took sixteen and a half hours from a camp opposite
the [[place:Gretha Islands]]: inland over a raised beach strewn with clam shells
and tufted with grass, silene, saxifrage and poppy; up a narrowing valley with
sheer and overhanging cliffs; then down an alluvial plain of frozen streams to a
fjord. Whether that fjord was [[place:Vendom Fiord]] or [[place:Trold Fiord]] is
unresolved in the printed paper. Humphreys took it for Vendom and thought the
fjord ran further north than the chart showed. Haig-Thomas put a footnote under
his leader's text saying the distance made it impossible — about 140 miles there
and back with one camp — and that it must have been Trold; and he had
[[person:Nukapinguaq]] on his side, who had made the same journey with Sergeant
Joy of the RCMP. Sources disagreeing in print, in the same paper, is rare enough
to be worth pointing at.

## Haa Island

They gave up the crossing to Canon Fjord — the hunters would not attempt it, and
wanted to be home before the ice broke — and on 12 May went from Bache Peninsula
to Haa Island, where about fifteen Inughuit house sites had never been examined.
The ground was frozen too hard to dig. From the moss packed between the stones of
one wall still standing they took artefacts suggesting the village had stood
empty about a hundred years, and sent them to the Pitt Rivers Museum at Oxford,
where they presumably remain. They opened graves.
[[artifact:oxford-ellesmere-1934-023|Of one the Inughuit said that by the care with which it was built her people must have loved her very much.]]
That sentence is the whole of the interpretation the paper offers, and the
question of what should happen now to human remains and grave goods taken from
Inuit sites in the 1930s is a live one: see [[topic:thule-dorset]] and
[[topic:inuit-oral-history]].

## The way home

They crossed Smith Sound in one camp and reached the Greenland coast north of
[[place:Cache Point]] at half past one in the morning on 19 May. The ice-foot,
undercut at low tide, broke away under Haig-Thomas's sledge; the dogs dropped and
hung by their traces; Kokochea held the sledge, cut the draw-line, and was
lowered on a rope to haul the living dogs out one at a time. Two were crushed
dead and four were never seen again. Shackleton's party, coming down three days
later, had to ferry their sledges on floating pans round the same point.

Humphreys was back at Etah on 23 May, after about 800 miles.
