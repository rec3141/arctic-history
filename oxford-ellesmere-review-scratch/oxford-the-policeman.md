# The policeman: Stallworthy, the empty posts and the game laws
[page · oxford-ellesmere-1934 · draft]

Canada did not send a policeman with the Oxford party to help them explore. The
Commissioner of the Royal Canadian Mounted Police put the reason in one sentence
in his annual report for the year ending 31 March 1935:
[[artifact:oxford-ellesmere-1934-051|it had been the Northwest Territories
Council's policy to have members of the force accompany expeditions to the
Canadian far north to ensure that the laws of Canada respecting game and similar
matters are not violated]]. Acting Sergeant H. W. Stallworthy, regimental number
6316, was lent "in an advisory capacity". He was a game warden as much as a guide.

## The man they were lent

[[person:Harry Stallworthy]] had thirteen years in the Canadian Arctic and spoke
Inuktitut. He had served at [[place:Craig Harbour]] and had been the officer in
charge of the detachment on [[place:Bache Peninsula]], the most northerly police
post in Canada, and he had done the hardest travelling the force did in the
Queen Elizabeth Islands ([[topic:rcmp-high-arctic-patrols]]).

In March 1932, two patrols left Bache Peninsula to look for the German geologist
[[person:Hans Krueger]], missing since 1930 with Åge Rose Bjare and the Inughuk
Akqioq ([[topic:kruger-1930]]). Stallworthy and Constable
[[person:R. W. Hamilton]] took seven Inughuit and 124 dogs; at
[[place:Bay Fiord]] the parties divided, Hamilton west and Stallworthy north.
Stallworthy reached Lands Lokk at the north tip of Axel Heiberg Island and found
the record Krueger had left there, written in German and signed by all three men,
saying they had been there by 24 April 1930 and were leaving for Meighen Island.
He followed south-west, ran short of food, killed six of his own dogs on 6 April
to feed the rest, shot seven Peary caribou on the 26th, and came back to the post
on 23 May after sixty-five days and about 2,250 km. He had found the last trace
of the Krueger party anyone has ever found.
[[artifact:oxford-ellesmere-1934-028|The 1932 patrol is described in the
Commissioner's report for that year]].

He also knew what the country could do to a man. On a patrol north of Makinson
Inlet in 1931 he went down a crevasse and jammed nine metres below the surface;
his Inughuit companions heard him before he lost consciousness, stood astride the
crack and hauled him out on a harpoon line.

## What he was worth to the expedition

Everything practical. [[person:Noel Humphreys]] records that Stallworthy built
the hut at [[place:Etah]], bought the dogs and the equipment in West Greenland,
and advised on all the stores. When the three spring parties were made up, he was
one of only four people in the whole expedition — with
[[person:Nukapinguaq]], [[person:Inuatuk]] and, by then,
[[person:A. W. Moore]] — judged fit to attempt the northern journey; the young
men from Oxford had trouble even with the nine-metre dog whips.

And when the dog food ran out at [[place:Lake Hazen]] and the party had to be cut
in half, it was Stallworthy who gave up the dash north. He stayed on the lake ice
with Inuatuk, jigging for Arctic char through bored holes for a week and a half,
so that Moore and Nukapinguaq could go on to
[[artifact:oxford-ellesmere-1934-034|the Gilman Glacier]] and the ice-cap. He is
in the one photograph of the four of them together after it was over,
[[artifact:oxford-ellesmere-1934-031|second from the left]].

## The posts, empty

The expedition travelled through a police landscape that had just been abandoned.
Ottawa closed the Bache Peninsula detachment in 1933 for economy, and when
[[person:Edward Shackleton]] and [[person:Robert Bentham]] came over
[[place:Smith Sound]] in April 1935 they found
[[artifact:oxford-ellesmere-1934-032|the huts standing empty with a radio mast
beside them and a rock cairn in front carrying a Union Jack]]. They used it as a
depot, and Humphreys operated on [[person:Sakeus]]'s eye there. Shackleton's joke
about Ellesmere Island — [[artifact:oxford-ellesmere-1934-019|the only country in
the world populated solely by policemen]] — was already out of date when he made
it.

Further north the Grant Land party slept in
[[artifact:oxford-ellesmere-1934-033|Peary's huts at Fort Conger]], burnt coal
from a seam the Nares expedition had opened in 1875 ([[topic:nares-greely]]), and
found a cache of pemmican left in 1919 by Godfred Hansen for Roald Amundsen's
airship dash to the Pole, still good enough to feed the dogs.

## The game laws, and what happened at Thule

The irony is exact. Stallworthy was lent to enforce Canadian game law, and the
game law that was broken was Danish, at Etah, in Greenland, where he had no
authority at all.

In the autumn of 1934 the expedition was laying caches of walrus meat for the
spring. The North Greenland game laws required a walrus to be harpooned before it
was shot, because a walrus shot and not secured sinks and is wasted. The Inughuit
hunters offered to harpoon each animal first and were ignored. Stallworthy's
report to headquarters is the source: "since Dr. Humphreys and other members of
the expedition, led by Haig-Thomas, continued to shoot at every opportunity,
three Eskimos joined in with their rifles, more to prevent the wounded ones from
slipping off the ice-pans into the water." Alarmed at the indiscriminate
shooting, he handed Humphreys a handbook of the game laws. It made no difference.

In February 1935 Humphreys and [[person:David Haig-Thomas]] were summoned to
[[place:Uummannaq (Thule)]] to answer before a Danish official and an Inughuit
council for shooting and wounding walrus without harpooning them first. Humphreys
pleaded ignorance and the charge against him was dismissed. Haig-Thomas, in
[[person:Lyle Dick]]'s account, also defeated the charge by claiming the animals
had been shot for emergency food — which Dick calls a false explanation.

None of this appears in the three papers the expedition read to the Royal
Geographical Society in November 1935, or in
[[source:shackleton1937arctic|Arctic Journeys]]. It survives because a policeman
wrote it down and [[source:dick2001muskox|a historian went to the police files]]
sixty-six years later. The council that heard the case was an Inughuit council:
the people whose walrus it was had a say, and used it.

## Afterwards

Stallworthy went back to the force. William Barr's biography of him, *Red Serge
and Polar Bear Pants* (2000), is the fullest account of his Arctic service and is
the obvious next thing to read for this page; the University of Manitoba licenses
it through Scholars Portal.

For the Inughuit side of the same years — the patrols, the guides, and what the
guides thought they were for — see [[topic:rcmp-high-arctic-patrols]] and
[[topic:greenland-inughuit]].
