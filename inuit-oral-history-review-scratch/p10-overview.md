Almost everything Europeans and Americans learned about this coast between 1576 and 1880 they learned from Inuit. The ships arrived with charts that stopped at the edge of what a ship had seen; the people already living there carried in memory, and could draw on paper, the shape of coasts a thousand kilometres beyond it. Where the exploration record is accurate, it is usually accurate because an Inuk told someone, and where it is wrong, it is often wrong because someone decided not to listen.

These pages are about that: Inuit knowledge, testimony and authorship inside a written record that was not made by Inuit, and mostly did not credit them.

## Three kinds of knowledge

**Geography.** Inuit travelled the coasts and the sea ice constantly, and knew them in a form that could be drawn. [[artifact:inuit-oral-history-015|Boas set it down plainly]] in 1888: "As their knowledge of all the directions is very detailed and they are skillful draftsmen they can draw very good charts. If a man intends to visit a country little known to him, he has a map drawn in the snow by some one well acquainted there and these maps are so good that every point can be recognized." Five of the charts he printed carry the name of the man who drew it. Sixty years earlier [[person:Iligliuk]] had drawn [[artifact:inuit-oral-history-001|the coast north of Winter Island]] for [[person:William Edward Parry]], and [[person:Ikmallik]] and his companions had drawn [[artifact:inuit-oral-history-002|the Gulf of Boothia]] for [[person:John Ross]]. See [[page:inuit-drawn-maps]].

**Testimony.** Inuit remembered encounters with ships, and passed the accounts on. [[person:Ookijoxy Ninoo]] told [[person:Charles Francis Hall]] in 1861 what her people remembered of [[topic:frobisher|Frobisher's]] three voyages of 1576-78 — the two ships, then three, then many, and the five white men her people captured, who built a boat and sailed away — nearly three hundred years after the event, and it matched Frobisher's three voyages exactly. The Inuit accounts of the [[topic:franklin-1845|Franklin]] retreat, gathered by [[person:John Rae]] in 1854, by Hall in 1869 and by Schwatka's party in 1879, were the only true information anyone had for a century and a half, and were repeatedly dismissed. See [[page:franklin-testimony-vindicated]].

**Language.** Nothing crossed between the two records without interpreters, and there were very few. [[source:smith1985ooligbuck|The Dictionary of Canadian Biography reckons]] that during the first half of the nineteenth century there were probably fewer than ten Inuit capable of acting as interpreters. [[person:Tookoolito]], [[person:Ebierbing]], [[person:Ooligbuck]], [[person:William Ouligbuck]], [[person:Tattannoeuck]] and [[person:Hans Hendrik]] are most of that list. See [[page:the-interpreters]].

## Whose record is it

The convention of these books is that the explorer sees and the Inuk assists. The books themselves keep contradicting it. Parry laid down the coast he already knew, made Iligliuk box the compass, and then watched — "never were the tracings of a pencil watched with more eager solicitude" — while she drew the rest; and when he tried to lead her past the end of her knowledge she "immediately dropped the pencil, and said she knew no more about it" ([[artifact:inuit-oral-history-016|p. 198]]). That is not an assistant. That is a geographer with standards, being consulted.

Ross's officers spent January 1830 in the Victory's cabin taking dictation from Netsilingmiut cartographers, and Ross calls Ikmallik "the promised hydrographer" without apparent irony. [[person:Franz Boas]], who came in 1883 as a trained geographer, printed the Inuit charts as figures in a scientific report with their authors named — [[person:Itu]], [[person:Sunapignang]], [[person:Angutuqdjuaq]], [[person:Aranin]] — which was more credit than most of his contemporaries gave.

And some of the record is Inuit-authored outright. [[person:Hans Hendrik]] served on four expeditions over twenty-three years and wrote his own account of them in Kalaallisut; [[source:hendrik1878memoirs|it was translated and published in 1878]]. In the same decade [[source:rink1875tales|Henry Rink's collection of Greenlandic tales]] appeared with a title page reading "with numerous illustrations, drawn and engraved by Eskimo" — [[artifact:inuit-oral-history-031|a winter house]], [[artifact:inuit-oral-history-034|a kayak with its harpoon and thrower]], [[artifact:inuit-oral-history-036|a crowd round a bear brought in on a sledge]]. See [[page:inuit-authorship]].

## Why it matters now

The wreck of HMS Erebus was found on 2 September 2014 in [[place:Wilmot and Crampton Bay]], in the water an old Ookjoolik man had described to Schwatka's party in 1879: a big ship frozen in the ice near an island about five miles due west of Grant Point, which his people boarded, cut a hole in, and which filled and sank the following summer ([[artifact:inuit-oral-history-021|Gilder 1881, pp. 78-79]]). [[person:Louie Kamookak]] of [[place:Uqsuqtuuq]] spent more than thirty years collecting that kind of testimony from Nattilik elders and setting it against the printed narratives; his work is part of why anyone was looking there. The wreck sites are now watched each summer by Inuit Guardians from Gjoa Haven. See [[page:knowledge-alive-today]].

For anyone working in these waters today, the practical point is the one Parry found in 1822. The people of the coast hold a description of it that is detailed, testable, and older than any chart aboard.
