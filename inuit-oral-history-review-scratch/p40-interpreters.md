During the first half of the nineteenth century, [[source:smith1985ooligbuck|by the Dictionary of Canadian Biography's reckoning]], there were probably fewer than ten Inuit capable of acting as interpreters. Almost every European account of this coast passed through one of them. They are named in the books, usually once, usually under a nickname given by a ship's crew, and then the narrative goes on as if the explorer had been talking directly to everyone.

## Tattannoeuck and Ooligbuck: the overland expeditions

[[person:Tattannoeuck]] — his name means "it is full"; the books call him Augustus — was born on the west coast of Hudson Bay some two hundred miles north of Fort Churchill and was trained as an interpreter at the Hudson's Bay Company post there, employed from 1812 to 1814 and again in 1815-16. He and another Inuk, Hoeootoerock (Junius), were engaged in 1820 as interpreters for [[person:John Franklin]]'s first overland expedition of 1819-22. Their task on the Coppermine was almost impossible: Samuel Hearne's Dene guides had massacred a band of Inuit at Bloody Falls fifty years earlier, and an armed party coming down that river again was the worst thing the local people could see. Tattannoeuck and Hoeootoerock were sent ahead alone to say otherwise. Twice the people fled anyway; from an old man too frail to escape, Tattannoeuck got an account of the inhabitants, the resources they used and the shape of the coast.

The return to Fort Enterprise killed more than half the party. Hoeootoerock disappeared on a hunting trip and Tattannoeuck searched for him in vain. Starving, he pushed ahead of the struggling column, got lost in country he had never seen, and found Fort Enterprise anyway; his having done so, Franklin wrote, "must be considered a remarkable proof of sagacity."

He interpreted again in 1825-27. On 7 July 1826, after several hundred Inuit at the Mackenzie mouth had pillaged Franklin's two boats, he went alone that night into a camp of forty men and warned them to change their conduct or lose their trade. "My tribe were in the same unhappy state in which you now are, before the white people came to Churchill," he told them, "but at present they are supplied with everything they need, and you see that I am well clothed." It did not work; the Inuit planned to kill the whole party, Tattannoeuck included, the next day, and simply did not get the chance. In 1833 he learned that [[person:George Back]] was mounting an expedition to search for [[person:John Ross]]. He went to Fort Churchill, bought a pound of gunpowder, two pounds of ball shot and half a pound of tobacco, and set out on foot for Fort Resolution with a lame leg. He arrived months later to find Back had gone on to Fort Reliance, pushed on after him, lost his way, and died of exposure on the Rivière à Jean, twenty miles short of Fort Resolution, in late February or early March 1834.

[[person:Ooligbuck]] was engaged at Churchill in May 1824 for the same second expedition, as hunter and as company for Tattannoeuck. He spoke no English then and learned it, and went on to serve for nearly thirty years: he went east with [[person:John Richardson]] and Kendall in 1826 on a journey of 1,455 miles, and his presence was what persuaded the Inuit they met that the party meant no harm; he helped open Fort-Chimo on Ungava Bay; he surveyed the coast east to the Castor and Pollux River with Dease and Simpson in 1839, who called him "a valuable and unhoped for acquisition"; and he went north with his son William on [[person:John Rae]]'s 1846-47 expedition, by which time he could no longer keep the pace of sledge travel. He refused to attend church at Churchill and would not travel without at least one other Inuk in the party. He died late in 1852.

## William Ouligbuck and the 1854 report

Ooligbuck's son [[person:William Ouligbuck]] went north with Rae again in 1853-54. In April 1854 near Pelly Bay, now [[place:Kugaaruk]], he interpreted the conversations that changed what Britain knew about the Franklin expedition. Everything in [[artifact:inuit-oral-history-019|Rae's report to the Admiralty]] — the forty men dragging a boat south over the ice, the bodies near the Great Fish River, the relics with the officers' crests on them — reached English through him. It is worth being exact about this: the most consequential single piece of Arctic testimony of the century was spoken in Inuktitut by Inuit witnesses and rendered into English by a young Inuk employee of the Hudson's Bay Company, and the argument that followed in London was conducted entirely without reference to any of them. Rae's published despatch names none of his informants and does not name his interpreter either. See [[page:franklin-testimony-vindicated]].

## Eenoolooapik and the sound that could not be found

[[person:Eenoolooapik]] was born about 1820 at Qimisuk (Blacklead Island) in Tenudiakbeek — Cumberland Sound. The Aberdeen whaling master [[person:William Penny]] had heard from Inuit of a large bay full of whales and had failed three times to find it. In September 1839, at Durban Island, he met a young man who had been born there, knew its geography in detail, and wanted to see Scotland; he took him home aboard the Neptune, and they reached Aberdeen on the evening of 8 November. Eenoolooapik nearly died of pneumonia after kayaking on the River Dee for the crowds; when he recovered he went to the theatre, to dinner parties and to two balls for the Queen's wedding, and over the winter he and Penny drew a map of the sound. Penny sent it to the Admiralty, which paid £20 towards his keep and was not otherwise interested.

He left Scotland on the Bon Accord on 1 April 1840; she whaled through the early summer, and then, with his help, Penny took her into Tenudiakbeek. Penny, believing no European had been in it, named it Hogarth's Sound after one of his backers; it was afterwards recognised as the Cumberland Gulf [[person:John Davis]] had entered in 1585. Eenoolooapik went back to his family, married Amitak, traded baleen with Penny each year, and died of consumption in the summer of 1847 — five years before the first planned wintering of a whaling crew in the sound, and long before the consequences of what he had shown Penny became clear. His sister was [[person:Tookoolito]].

## Tookoolito and Ebierbing

[[person:Tookoolito]] and her husband [[person:Ebierbing]] worked with [[person:Charles Francis Hall]] from 1860 until his death in 1871, and afterwards on the Polaris and through the six-month ice-floe drift; Ebierbing then guided Schwatka's search on King William Island in 1878-80. Tookoolito had been taken to England in 1853 and presented to Queen Victoria, and read and wrote English. Nearly everything Hall recorded from Inuit informants — [[person:Ookijoxy Ninoo]] on the Frobisher voyages, [[person:In-nook-poo-zhee-jook]] on Franklin — is her translation. She is the reason those testimonies exist in English at all, and she is credited in the books as a servant.

Their story is told at length in [[topic:kane-hall-polaris]], at [[page:hall-among-the-inuit]] and [[page:the-inuit-of-these-expeditions]].

## Hans Hendrik

[[person:Hans Hendrik]] of Fiskernaesset in south Greenland shipped with [[person:Elisha Kent Kane]] at eighteen and served on four expeditions over twenty-three years — Kane, Hayes, Hall's Polaris, Nares — which is more Arctic service than most of the officers who employed him. He is the one member of this group who wrote his own book. See [[page:inuit-authorship]].
