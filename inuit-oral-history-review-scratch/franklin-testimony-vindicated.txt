# The Franklin testimony, and how long it took to be believed
[page · inuit-oral-history · draft]

The Franklin expedition vanished in 1845. Between 1848 and 1880 something like thirty search expeditions went looking for it, at enormous cost, and found very little. Inuit told searchers what had happened, in detail, from 1854 onwards. Almost every element of what they said turned out to be true, and almost none of it was acted on for a century and a half.

## 1854: Rae at Pelly Bay

In April 1854 [[person:John Rae]] was surveying the west shore of Boothia when he met Inuit near Pelly Bay, now [[place:Kugaaruk]]. Through his interpreter [[person:William Ouligbuck]] he learned, over several conversations and from several people, that [[artifact:inuit-oral-history-019|in the spring of 1850 about forty white men had been seen dragging a boat south over the ice]] off the north shore of King William Island, and that later that season some thirty bodies had been found on the mainland and five on an island near a large river. He bought relics from them: silver forks and spoons with the crests and initials of Crozier, Goodsir, Macdonald, Peddie and others, and a plate engraved "Sir John Franklin, K.C.B."

Rae was scrupulous about what kind of evidence this was. [[artifact:inuit-oral-history-020|He told the Admiralty in the same report]]: "None of the Esquimaux with whom I conversed had seen the whites, nor had they ever been at the place where the bodies were found, but had their information from those who had been there, and who had seen the party when travelling." He also reported, because the people had told him, that "from the mutilated state of many of the corpses and the contents of the kettles" the last survivors had been driven to cannibalism.

## The reaction

The report was published in London on Rae's arrival in October 1854. The relics were unarguable. The cannibalism was not accepted, and the way it was rejected mattered: not by arguing about the physical evidence but by arguing that the witnesses were not the sort of people whose word counts. [[person:Charles Dickens]], writing in Household Words at Lady Franklin's request, [[artifact:inuit-oral-history-025|put it in one sentence]]: "We believe every savage to be in his heart covetous, treacherous, and cruel." He had never been to the Arctic and had never met an Inuk.

It worked. Rae, who had located the fate of the expedition at a fraction of the cost of any naval search, was frozen out; he received the Admiralty reward but not the knighthood or the standing given to men who found less. The Inuit account went into the literature as native rumour. The version of the Franklin story that Britain and Canada told for the next hundred years had no Inuit witnesses in it.

## 1869 and 1879: Hall and Schwatka

[[person:Charles Francis Hall]] spent five years at Naujaat with [[person:Tookoolito]] and [[person:Ebierbing]] collecting testimony, and in May 1869 crossed to King William Island with [[person:In-nook-poo-zhee-jook]], the man who had first told Rae of the deaths and who Hall called, in a letter to Grinnell on 20 June 1869, "a walking history of the fate of Sir John Franklin's Expedition." In-nook-poo-zhee-jook drew him a sketch map of the island and showed him a silver spoon with an eel's-head crest. See [[page:hall-at-repulse-bay]].

Ten years later Schwatka's party, with Ebierbing interpreting, took down accounts from the Ukjulingmiut and Netsilingmiut and — this is the important editorial decision — [[source:gilder1881schwatkas|W. H. Gilder printed the interviews as recorded]], "so that each reader may draw his own conclusions." Two of them matter especially.

[[person:Tooktoocheer]]'s son [[person:Ogzeuckjeuwock]] had been about twelve when he went with his parents to the boat place on the south coast of King William Island. [[artifact:inuit-oral-history-022|He described]] a fastened tin case about two feet long, full of written and printed books, which they broke open; a needle that stuck to iron; more than four skulls outside the boat; and arm and leg bones "that appeared to have been sawed off." That last detail was ridiculed. In 1992 and after, forensic work on skeletal remains from King William Island found cut marks consistent with exactly that.

[[person:Ikinnelikpatolok]], an old Ukjulingmiut man, [[artifact:inuit-oral-history-021|described a ship]] frozen in the ice "near an island about five miles due west of Grant Point, on Adelaide Peninsula," with a dead man in a bunk. His people went aboard, could not work the doors, cut a hole in the side at ice level, took knives and forks and pans and left the books, and "when the ice broke up during the following summer the ship filled and sunk."

## 2014

On 2 September 2014 a Parks Canada team found HMS Erebus in [[place:Wilmot and Crampton Bay]], off the Adelaide Peninsula, in shallow water beside a small island. She had a hole in her side. The wreck is where Ikinnelikpatolok said it was, one hundred and thirty-five years after he said it, and about a hundred and sixty-five years after his people watched her sink.

The search area had been narrowed by, among other things, the lifetime of oral-history work done by [[person:Louie Kamookak]] of [[place:Uqsuqtuuq]], who had collected Nattilik accounts of ships and of white men and matched them against the printed narratives and against Inuit place names. See [[page:knowledge-alive-today]] and, in [[topic:franklin-1845]], [[page:franklin-1845-inuit-testimony]] and [[page:franklin-1845-the-wrecks]].

## What the record actually shows

It is worth stating the score plainly, because the habit of treating oral testimony as a soft source dies hard.

- The ships were crushed or abandoned and the men walked south: the Inuit said so in 1854, confirmed by the [[artifact:franklin-1845-012|Victory Point record]] found in 1859.
- Bodies near the Great Fish River: said in 1854, confirmed by McClintock and Hobson in 1859 and by every subsequent survey.
- Cannibalism among the last survivors: said in 1854, denounced as slander, confirmed forensically in the 1990s.
- Books in a tin case at the boat place: said in 1879, and still the best reason to think written material may yet be recoverable.
- A ship sunk off the Adelaide Peninsula with a hole cut in her side: said in 1879, found in 2014.

Where the Inuit accounts were imprecise, they were imprecise in the ways any long-transmitted testimony is — dates unfixed, two events sometimes merged. Kamookak himself pointed out that some elders' accounts of men hauling boats had run [[person:John Ross]]'s people together with Franklin's. That is a reason to work with the testimony carefully. It was never a reason to throw it away, and throwing it away cost the searchers a century and a half.
