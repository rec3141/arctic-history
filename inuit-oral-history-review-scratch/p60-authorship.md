Nearly all of this record was written by people who did not live here, about people who did. There are a small number of exceptions, and they are worth reading closely, because they show what the same events look like from the other side of the interpreter.

## Hans Hendrik's Memoirs

[[person:Hans Hendrik]] — Suersaq — was a Kalaallit hunter from Kekertarsuatsiak (Fiskernaes) in south Greenland. [[person:Elisha Kent Kane]] shipped him in 1853, when he was eighteen, because nobody else would go: the ship's master, Hans writes, "tried to get one assistant more, but did not succeed." He served on four expeditions over twenty-three years: Kane 1853-55, Hayes 1860-61, Hall's Polaris 1871-73, and Nares 1875-76. That is more Arctic service than most of the naval officers who employed him, and on at least two of those expeditions the survival of the party depended on his hunting.

He then wrote it down himself, in Kalaallisut. Henry Rink, director of the Royal Greenland Board of Trade, translated it, and it was published in London in 1878 as [[source:hendrik1878memoirs|Memoirs of Hans Hendrik, the Arctic Traveller, Serving under Kane, Hayes, Hall and Nares, 1853-1876, Written by Himself]]. [[artifact:inuit-oral-history-027|The full text is here]].

[[artifact:inuit-oral-history-026|The first sentence declares the whole project]]: "To relate how the northern part of the big country came to be explored, I write this — I, Hans Hendrik, who first lived at Kekertarsuatsiak [Fiskernaes], belonging to the Germans [Moravians], but have now removed north, to Kangersuatsiak, belonging to Upernivik."

## What is different in it

Three things, mainly.

**Family.** The book opens not with an outfitting list but with his parents: his father Benjamin, who served the priests at the Moravian station and went round the other stations to lead the service on the great holidays; his mother Ernestine, who had charge of the church lamps and had come from Agdluitsok, at the end of the country. It sets out his brothers by name — Simion, Joel, and the youngest Nathaniel — and the reason he went: his father had died, the younger boys had not "grown food-winners as yet", and the ship offered payment to the family of whoever joined. His mother begged him not to go.

**Cost.** "When I left my countrymen and relatives, to be sure it was very disheartening. Still, I thought, if I do not perish I shall return. How strange! This was not to be fulfilled." He never lived in the south again.

**Being doubted.** The most uncomfortable passages are about how he was treated. When Hayes's astronomer August Sonntag died on a sledge journey in 1860 Hans was cross-questioned about it, and he records that the others "conceived suspicions". Those passages are collected in [[topic:kane-hall-polaris]] — see [[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-031]] and [[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-030]]. Rink, translating, added the flat question that no expedition narrative had thought to ask: who caught the seals? ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-034]]).

He was on the ice floe through the whole six-month drift of 1872-73 with his wife Merkut and their four children ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-033]]). Nineteen people came off that floe alive, and the reason was that two Inuit families were on it.

## Pictures drawn by Greenlanders

The other Inuit-authored body of work from the same years is in [[source:rink1875tales|Henry Rink's Tales and Traditions of the Eskimo]] (Edinburgh, 1875). Its title page reads "with numerous illustrations, drawn and engraved by Eskimo", and the preface says that some of the illustrations were "designed as well as drawn and engraved on wood by natives of Greenland", from blocks Rink supplied to the publisher. The book is Greenlandic oral tradition, collected between 1848 and 1868, printed alongside pictures made by Greenlanders — which makes it one of the earliest printed books in which Inuit narrative and Inuit images stand together.

Some are ethnographic plates, captioned by the engraver: [[artifact:inuit-oral-history-031|a winter house]] banked with turf, with its glazed windows and its stovepipe; [[artifact:inuit-oral-history-032|a woman with a child in the amaut]] and [[artifact:inuit-oral-history-033|a girl in holiday dress]] at Nuuk (Godthaab); [[artifact:inuit-oral-history-034|the kayak with its harpoon, thrower and paddle]]; [[artifact:inuit-oral-history-035|the large lance]]. Others are full-page scenes set into the tales themselves, and carry no caption at all: [[artifact:inuit-oral-history-036|a crowd round a bear brought in on a sledge]], [[artifact:inuit-oral-history-037|armed men on a shore beside a boat]], [[artifact:inuit-oral-history-038|people scattering down a slope in falling snow]], [[artifact:inuit-oral-history-039|a congregation at a service in a plank-built mission house]]. The last two sections of the book are tales about Europeans and about the Danish colony, and the pictures go with them: this is a Greenlandic view of the colonists, drawn and cut by Greenlanders and printed in Edinburgh. ## The other Inuit-authored material

- **The charts.** [[person:Iligliuk]]'s [[artifact:inuit-oral-history-001|Eskimaux Chart No. 1]], [[person:Ikmallik]]'s [[artifact:inuit-oral-history-002|chart of the Gulf of Boothia]], and the five charts in [[source:boas1888central|The Central Eskimo]] by [[person:Itu]], [[person:Sunapignang]], [[person:Angutuqdjuaq]] and [[person:Aranin]] are documents made by named Inuit authors, published under their names, and still readable as geography. See [[page:inuit-drawn-maps]].
- **The testimony.** The interviews [[source:gilder1881schwatkas|Gilder printed as he recorded them]] in 1881 are, in effect, dictated first-person narratives by [[person:Ikinnelikpatolok]], [[person:Tooktoocheer]] and [[person:Ogzeuckjeuwock]], with Gilder as stenographer rather than author. He said so himself, printing them "so that each reader may draw his own conclusions." See [[page:franklin-testimony-vindicated]].

## How to read the rest

The practical consequence is a reading rule. When a narrative says "we found", ask who was on the sledge; when it gives a coastline the ship never approached, ask who drew it; and when it reports what the local people said, ask who translated. The answer is usually a named person who is not on the title page.
