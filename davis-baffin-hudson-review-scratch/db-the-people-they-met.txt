# The people they met, and the two men who were taken
[page · davis-baffin-hudson · draft]

Every voyage in this topic met people who already lived in the country it
claimed to be discovering. The meetings are recorded in only one direction — in
English and Danish, by men writing for backers — and what they record includes
music, trade, four killings by Europeans, at least ten deaths of Europeans, and
two men carried off to sea who were never named. This page sets out what the
sources actually say, and what they cannot say.

## What the record contains

**1585, Gilbert Sound.** [[person:John Davis]]'s first meeting with the
Kalaallit at [[place:Nuup Kangerlua]] is the friendliest scene in the whole
topic: "we caused our Musicians to play, our selves dauncing, and making many
signes of friendship" ([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-025]]). A man ashore
pointed at the sun and struck his breast so hard it could be heard across the
water; [[person:John Ellis]] copied the gesture back until both sides were
satisfied, and caps, stockings and gloves were thrown ashore. They traded
sealskins, salmon and birds for iron.

**1586, the same anchorage.** They were recognised on their return, and the
year turned violent. Davis took a man hostage over a stolen anchor, got the
anchor back, and sailed with him anyway when the wind came fair; the printed
margin reads only "One of the people taken, which after dyed"
([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-027]]). On 30 August the Sunneshine's crew
killed three men in a fight over a boat. Two of Davis's sailors were killed by
arrows on the Labrador coast a week later.

**1587.** The master of the Sunneshine seized "one of the people, a very strong
lustie yong fellow" at Gilbert Sound on 19 June. Markham's note is the whole of
his after-history: "What became of him is not related."

**1610-11, the bay.** [[person:Henry Hudson]] and his men saw one person all
winter: an Inuk who came to the ship once in the spring, traded skins, said he
would come back, and did not. In July 1611 the starving survivors traded
peacefully at the [[place:Digges Islands]] on the 28th and were attacked on the
29th; Henry Greene, William Wilson and John Thomas were killed outright and
Michael Perse died of his wounds ([[page:hudsons-fate]]).

**1612.** [[person:Thomas Button]] anchored at the same islands and lost five
men killed — by the surviving second-hand accounts, in reprisal for canoes his
people had taken.

**1615-16.** [[person:William Baffin]] saw people waving from the rocks at the
Digges Islands. In 1616, at the Women Islands off Upernavik, the inhabitants
fled the ship's approach and left behind two young women hidden on a rock, two
old women and a woman with a child. Off Cape Shackleton in mid-June forty-two
people came out in kayaks over several days to trade sealskins and narwhal ivory
for iron and beads, and Baffin named the place Home Sound. At Cockin Sound in
August, six people brought salmon to trade on three separate days. He writes
down dress, women's facial tattooing, kayaks, a cry of "Ilyout" made toward the
sun, and graves covered over with stones.

**1619-20, the Churchill.** [[person:Jens Munk]] traded knives and iron for
birds and seal meat with people in [[place:Hudson Strait]] in July 1619 — the
only meeting of that whole expedition. At [[place:Kuugjuaq]] he sent nineteen men
three miles inland in November to see whether anyone lived there and they found
nobody, though they found summer camp sites, hearths and cut wood. Sixty-one of
his men died there over the winter ([[page:munk-at-the-churchill]]).

## The two men who were taken

The 1586 hostage and the 1587 captive are the two clearest facts in this topic
that the record refuses to complete. Davis describes the first man's companion
following the ship, the captive speaking four or five words and covering his
face with both hands, and the other man doing the same and going away; the
English read it as a sign of death. Davis then gave him an English frieze suit
and called him a pleasant companion. He died at sea. The second man was simply
taken and "what became of him is not related".

Neither man's name is anywhere. Neither is any Kalaaleq's or Inuk's name in the
whole of the Davis, Hudson, Button, Baffin, Munk, Foxe or James record. What
does survive is [[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-031|about forty words of their
language]], written down at Gilbert Sound in 1586 and printed with Hinrich
Rink's identifications in 1880: nerisinait, aiguk, umiamik, pautik, savingmik,
tugto, kuninga, kissuk. The vocabulary and the kidnapping are on facing pages of
the same book.

That absence should be stated rather than filled in. There is no basis for
naming these men, and inventing names or motives for them would be a second
erasure. What can be said is that they were somebody's son, brother or husband
in a community around Nuup Kangerlua that is still there, and that this is what
the written record is like: it preserves the ship's business.

## Reading the encounters honestly

Three things are worth holding onto when the accounts are used.

First, the violence runs both ways and the sources only count one side. Nine
Europeans were killed by Inuit in these voyages (four with Hudson at the Digges
Islands, five with Button at the same place). At least four Kalaallit were
killed by Europeans (three at Gilbert Sound in 1586, one by Abacuk Pricket at
the Digges Islands in 1611), and two men were carried off. The European deaths
are named, dated and mourned in print. The others are a line in a margin.

Second, the attacks are not unmotivated, and the sources contain the motive
without noticing it. Hudson's men fought at the Digges Islands in 1611, ten
months after Hudson's own visit and a year before Button lost five men in the
same place over canoes his people had taken. Theft of boats, of iron, of people
had preceded every one of these fights. Davis, watching the Kalaallit take iron
from his ships, wrote that their simplicity gave him occasion for laughter; when
his own men later killed three of them he was not there.

Third, these encounters were the beginning of a continuous relationship, not
isolated incidents. Baffin's report of whales in Whale Sound
([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-043]]) is the same information that brought the
whaling fleets into Baffin Bay two centuries later, with everything that followed
for the communities of Qikiqtaaluk (Baffin Island) and Kalaallit Nunaat — see
[[topic:whaling]] and [[topic:greenland-inughuit]]. The chart made from these
voyages is the reason later ships knew where to go.

Inuit accounts of this period exist as oral history rather than as documents,
and they are the other half of the record: see [[topic:inuit-oral-history]],
[[topic:thule-dorset]] for who was living along these coasts when the ships
arrived, and [[topic:hudson-strait-south-baffin]] for the communities whose
waters most of these meetings happened in. The Qikiqtani Truth Commission's
principle is the right one to apply to a page like this: the written record of
outsiders is evidence about the outsiders as much as about anyone else.
