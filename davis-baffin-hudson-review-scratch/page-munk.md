On 9 May 1619 two ships of the Danish crown went down the Sound from Copenhagen:
the frigate [[vessel:Enhiorningen]] — the Unicorn — with forty-eight men, and
the sloop [[vessel:Lamprenen]], the Lamprey, with sixteen. Sixty-four men in
all, under [[person:Jens Munk]], sent by Christian IV to find the north-west
passage and claim it for Denmark. Sixteen months later three of them reached
Norway alive. The track is [[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-008]] and the record
is Munk's own book, [[source:munk1624navigatio]], printed at Copenhagen in 1624
and translated in [[source:gosch1897danish]]
([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-024]]).

## Getting there

Munk was a competent seaman and the outward passage shows it. He took his ships
past Shetland and the Faroes, sighted Cape Farewell on 30 June in 60° 30′,
fought through the ice at the mouth of what he called Fretum Christian —
[[place:Hudson Strait]] — in mid-July, and on the 18th anchored in a harbour he
named Rin Sund, for the reindeer his men shot there. It is the only meeting with
anybody in the whole voyage, and it went well: knives, iron goods and a
looking-glass for birds and seal flesh, described on
[[page:the-people-they-met]]. He rounded the
[[place:Digges Islands]] in the first days of September and ran three days
south-west across the bay.

On 7 September 1619 he brought both ships into the mouth of the Churchill River
on the west shore of Hudson Bay — [[place:Kuugjuaq]], where the town of
Churchill, Manitoba stands now — and named it Jens Munk's Winter Harbour, in a
country he called Nova Dania. The woodcut he had made for his book shows it as
it looked then: the two ships hauled in, a shelter ashore, men out hunting
([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-016]]). It is a picture of a place with living
men in it.

He was three weeks getting the big ship up the river and grounded safely against
the drift ice. Then the winter closed.

## The winter

On 21 November a sailor who had long been ill was buried: the first. On
12 December the surgeon of the Lamprenen, [[person:David Velske]], died, and the
frost was so hard that his body could not be got ashore for two days. On
10 January the priest [[person:Rasmus Jensen]] and the second surgeon,
[[person:Casper Caspersen]], took to their beds and the sickness became general.
On 23 January the mate Hans Brock died; that same day Rasmus Jensen sat up in
his berth and preached, "which sermon was the last he delivered in this world".
The priest died on 20 February, Caspersen on 21 March, the skipper Jan Ollufsen
on 25 March, Munk's own nephew Erich Munck on 1 April, the skipper Jens
Hendrichsen on 16 May. By the end of March about half the company were dead, and
there was no one aboard either ship who knew anything about medicine. When Munk
went through the surgeon's chest himself he found medicaments the surgeon he had
did not know; the priest had been reading the labels to the surgeon while both
were still alive.

What killed them is not in doubt. Munk described it exactly:

> all the limbs and joints were so miserably drawn together, with great pains
> in the loins, as if a thousand knives were thrust through them. The body at
> the same time was blue and brown, as when one gets a black eye, and the whole
> body was quite powerless.

([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-032]]; the teeth were all loose, so that they
could not eat.) Gosch's footnote in 1897 is one line: the disease was
undoubtedly scurvy.

By May the survivors could no longer bury anyone. On 19 May Munk wrote of Erich
Hansen Li, who "had dug many graves for others, but now there was nobody that
could dig his, and his body had to remain unburied"
([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-033]]). Munk records the names of nearly every
man as he dies, which is why an almost complete crew list can be reconstructed
from the death entries alone.

On Whit Sunday, 4 June 1620, three men besides Munk were alive, all of them
lying down and unable to help one another, and none with the strength to go into
the hold for a drink of wine. He wrote what he took to be his last entry:

> INASMUCH as I have now no more hope of life in this world, I request, for the
> sake of God, if any Christian men should happen to come here, that they will
> bury in the earth my poor body, together with the others which are found
> here... and, furthermore, that this my journal may be forwarded to my most
> gracious Lord and King.

([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-034]].) The margin of the manuscript at that
entry reads 61 dead.

## The three who lived

He did not die. On 8 June, unable to bear the smell of the bodies any longer, he
got himself out of the cabin; the next day the two men living ashore saw that he
was alive and helped him down off the ship. The sailmaker died aboard while they
were ashore, which left three. They lived under a bush, made a fire in the
daytime, and

> crawled about everywhere near, wherever we saw the least green growing out of
> the ground, which we dug up and sucked the main root thereof

([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-035]]) — and it benefited them. On 18 June they
set a net at low water and God gave them six large trout, which Munk cooked
himself. On 26 June, three men with scurvy, they began the work of hauling the
sloop off the beach, unloading her, floating her on a spring tide and clearing
the decomposed dead out of the Enhiorningen. On 16 July 1620, a Sunday
afternoon, they set sail, leaving the Enhiorningen scuttled where she lay with
the dead aboard and ashore. They were beset in the ice, broke the rudder,
crossed the Atlantic taking daily latitudes, saw Norway on 20 September, got the
sloop into an anchorage south of Alden on the 21st with half an anchor and no
boat, and Munk came into Bergen himself on 25 September 1620 and went straight
to the physicians.

The cure was growing on the shore they had been anchored against for eleven
months. Munk had even gathered tydebaer — lingonberries — from the bare patches
in March, while men were still dying, and handed them round. Neither of the two
professional medical men aboard, both of whom died, appears to have known what
the berries were doing.

The names of the two men who came home with him are nowhere in the book. Gosch
says so plainly: they are "the two who returned with Munk, but whose names are
not mentioned."

## The place, and what came after

Munk met nobody at the Churchill, but the country was not empty and his own
journal shows it. On 7 November 1619 he went ashore with nineteen men and
pushed nearly three miles inland to see whether anyone lived there; they found
no one, though they found summer camp sites, hearths and cut wood chips, and
Munk took some stone structures for altars where Gosch thought they were graves.
A week later, on the night of 14 November, the watch shot what he took for a
black fox and found in the morning that it was a large dog, trained to catch
game, its muzzle worn bare where cords had been tied round it and its right ear
cleft. Somebody's dog. This is the country where Dene, Cree and Inuit
territories meet, and the absence in Munk's journal is a winter absence: the
people who used the river mouth in summer were elsewhere, as they would be.

The Hudson's Bay Company built its first post on the river in 1688 and Prince of
Wales Fort at the harbour mouth from 1717. Gosch, editing in 1897, placed Munk's
wintering ground about four miles up the western shore, where one of his cannon
had been dug up, and the local name for it was Munk's Cove. Churchill today is
the rail head and deep-water port for Hudson Bay and the place most southern
Canadians have heard of because of polar bears.

Denmark did not go back. The voyage cost the crown two ships and sixty-one men,
and the shape of Hudson Bay on the charts still looked open to the west
([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-015]]), so it was the English who tried again in
1631 with Foxe and James ([[page:foxe-and-james-1631]]). Munk himself proposed
another expedition, never got it, and died in Danish service in 1628.

Read beside [[page:hudsons-fate]], the winter at Nova Dania is the plainest
statement in this whole topic of what the search actually cost, and of the one
technical fact that decided who came home: fresh food. Every Arctic expedition
for the next two hundred and fifty years failed or survived on the same point,
until [[topic:amundsen-gjoa]] and others learned to eat what the people of the
country ate.
