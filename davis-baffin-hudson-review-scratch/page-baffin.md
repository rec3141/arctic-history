In two summers, in a 55-ton bark that had already been to the bottom of Hudson
Bay and back, [[person:Robert Bylot]] and [[person:William Baffin]] did the two
most useful things anyone did in this whole search: they showed where the
passage was not, and they drew the outline of Baffin Bay so accurately that
nobody believed it for two hundred years.

The ship was the [[vessel:Discovery]] — the same ship Hudson had been set adrift
from ([[page:hudsons-fate]]), with the same Bylot who had brought her home in
1611 and had never been punished for it. Bylot was master and commander both
times. Baffin was his mate and pilot, and he kept the journals, which is why the
bay is his and not Bylot's. By Baffin's own count they carried "fourteene other
men and 2 boyes" in 1615 and were "in number seuenteene persons" in 1616. The
backers were [[person:Thomas Smith]], [[person:Dudley Digges]],
[[person:John Wolstenholme]] and Alderman Jones, and they promised treble wages
if the passage were found; [[person:Alwin Cary]] came aboard with Wolstenholme
on 15 March 1615 carrying the commission. The record is
[[source:markham1881baffin]] ([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-021]]).

## 1615: settling it by the tides

The 1615 voyage went up [[place:Hudson Strait]] and along the south-east side of
Southampton Island: Resolution Island on 27 May, Salisbury Island, Mill Island
(so named "by reason of the greate extremetye and grindinge of the ice"), Cape
Comfort, Sea Horse Point. The track is [[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-006]].
Baffin's own manuscript chart of the voyage survives and is reproduced in
facsimile ([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-072]]): the coasts he actually saw are
washed in colour, the ship's track is pricked in red, and the shores nobody had
seen are left as faint outline — a map that says plainly what is observation and
what is not. His working table of the voyage is printed too, five ruled pages of
date, latitude, compass variation and remarks
([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-075]], [[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-076]],
[[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-077]], [[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-078]],
[[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-079]]); a second, engraved chart of the strait is
[[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-014]].

What makes this voyage different is how it reached its conclusion. On the
evening of 16 July the Discovery came to the southern point of Southampton
Island, which Baffin named Sea Horse Point, and anchored there in the open sea
on purpose, "the better to proue the sett of the tyde":

> Heare we found, most apparently to all our companies sight, that in this
> place the tyde of floud doth come from the south east, and the ebb from the
> north west, being the certaynest sett of tyde we haue yet made proofe of.

([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-042]].) Bylot went ashore himself to check it,
and they repeated the trial at Nottingham Island and at the
[[place:Digges Islands]]. The reasoning is simple and it is right: if the flood
comes from the south-east, the tide is entering from the Atlantic, and there is
no great ocean pressing in from the west. Every earlier captain had argued from
the look of the water and the run of the coast — that is exactly what misled
[[person:John Davis]] in Cumberland Sound thirty years before
([[page:davis-three-voyages]]). Baffin argued from a measurement, and he was
careful about what it did and did not show: he would not affirm there was no
passage anywhere within the strait, only that no tide he could find came out of
one.

Earlier, in the third week of June, he did something else nobody had done. The
ship lay fast in the ice off a point of broken islands he called Broken Point,
in weather so fair and calm that the men passed the days shooting at butts with
bow and arrows and playing stool-ball and football. On 21 June, seeing sun and
moon both clear, Baffin took the altitude and azimuth of
each; he was not satisfied, took a better set the next day, and worked it out.
His answer for the longitude was 74° 5′ west of London, against the 74° 30′ he
had been carrying by dead reckoning. Markham calls it the first attempt anyone
made to fix a longitude at sea by observing the moon. It was two hundred years
early. Parry, off the same point on 29 July 1821 with chronometers, wrote that
Baffin's longitude was "not a degree to the westward of the truth".

They came into Plymouth Sound on 7 September 1615 without the loss of one man.

## 1616: round the top of the bay

They sailed again on 26 March 1616, seventeen people aboard, and went up the
Greenland coast: past Sukkertoppen, past the Disko coast, past
[[place:Sanderson's Hope]] — Davis's farthest, at 72° 12′ — on 30 May, and on
into water no European had recorded. At the Women Islands off Upernavik the
people fled at their approach, leaving behind two young women hidden on a rock,
two old women and a woman with a child. Off Cape Shackleton in mid-June
forty-two people came out in kayaks over several days and traded sealskins and
narwhal ivory for iron and beads. Baffin writes down the dress, the women's
facial tattooing, a cry of "Ilyout" made toward the sun, the graves covered over
with stones. He writes down no names, and there are none to recover. See
[[page:the-people-they-met]].

Then they got the one thing every ship on this coast needs and few get: an open
year in Melville Bay. On 1 July they "were come into an open sea in the latitude
of 75° 40′, which anew reuiued our hope of a passage". On 2 July they passed
Cape Dudley Digges; on the 3rd they were blown out of Wolstenholme Sound; on the
4th they were in [[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-043|Whale Sound, in the latitude
77° 30′, with more whales in it than they had any means of killing]]; and on
5 July they lay off Hakluyt Island at the mouth of [[place:Smith Sound]], which
Baffin reported running "to the north of 78°". There the compass varied above
five points — fifty-six degrees — which he called a thing almost incredible and
matchlesse in all the world beside. It is, and it is real: they were close to
the magnetic pole's own hemisphere of strangeness.

That was the highest latitude reached, and it stood for about two centuries. It
is worth saying exactly what the text supports, because the books repeat a
number that is not in it: Baffin gives no single farthest-north figure. The
highest latitude he states for a place he actually entered is Whale Sound,
77° 30′. Smith Sound running "north of 78°" is a sighting, not a position sailed
to. The often-quoted 77° 45′ appears nowhere in Markham's edition.

Coming south they passed [[place:Jones Sound]] on 10 July and, on 12 July, "were
open of another great Sound, lying in the latitude of 74° 20′, and we called it
Sir James Lancaster's Sound"
([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-046]]) — [[place:Tallurutiup Imanga]], the actual
Northwest Passage, whose door they looked at and did not enter. In the same
breath the hope of a passage "began to be lesse euery day then other". The cook
[[person:Richard Waynam]] died of scurvy on 26 July; two days later they anchored
in Cockin Sound, ate scurvy grass, sorrel and orpine, and the rest of the sick
were well in nine days. They were at Dover on 30 August. The track is
[[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-007]].

## The letter, and the erasure

Baffin wrote to [[person:John Wolstenholme]] to say what the money had bought:

> there is no passage nor hope of passage in the north of Davis Straights. We
> hauing coasted all, or neere all the circumference thereof, and finde it to
> be no other then a great bay, as the voyage doth truely shew.

([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-044]].) He was right about the bay and wrong
about the passage, and he was wrong for a reason that is written on his own
track: the ice pushed him past the mouth of Lancaster Sound. In the same letter
he defended Davis by name
([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-045|Neither was Master Davis to be blamed in his
report and great hopes]]), on the technical ground that the tides at Hope
Sanderson are small and irregular and would not have told Davis anything.

Then the strangest part. Because Baffin said there was no passage, nobody had a
reason to go back, and because nobody went back, nothing he had drawn could be
confirmed. Over two centuries his names came off the maps. Daines Barrington's
map of 1818 marks the bay "Baffin's Bay according to the relation of W. Baffin in
1616, but not now believed"; Sir John Barrow's map of the same year removed it
altogether and left Davis Strait opening onto a blank space. Markham printed the
whole sequence side by side in 1881 — Baffin's own delineation, the
eighteenth-century blanks, and the nineteenth-century surveys
([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-073]]). [[person:Luke Foxe]] had printed all of
it in 1635 — Smith Sound, Alderman Jones, Lancaster Sound, lettered in at the top
of his card ([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-018]]) — and it was rubbed out anyway.

In that same year, 1818, John Ross sailed north with Parry, went round the bay,
and found every one of Baffin's names where Baffin had put them, his latitudes
good and his coastline right. Ross wrote that he had "derived great additional
pleasure from the reflection that I have placed in a fair light before the
public the merits of a worthy man and able navigator". Markham, editing Baffin
sixty years later, put it harder still: Baffin had lived to see the bay his name
was fairly associated with treated as a phantom of the imagination. The next
year Parry sailed into Lancaster Sound and kept going — see
[[topic:ross-parry]].

Baffin himself never knew any of it. He went east with the East India Company
and was killed at the siege of Qeshm in the Persian Gulf in January 1622, aged
about thirty-eight, hit while going ashore with his mathematical instruments to
take the height and distance of the castle wall so the guns could be laid on it.
Markham printed a chart of that coast in the same volume as the Arctic voyages
([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-074]]).

## Why it matters on this ship

The Amundsen works in the water Baffin drew. Tallurutiup Imanga is now a
national marine conservation area co-managed with the Inuit of Mittimatalik
(Pond Inlet), Ikpiarjuk (Arctic Bay), Qausuittuq (Resolute), Ausuittuq (Grise
Fiord) and Clyde River. The North Water polynya that let Baffin through in 1616
— the Pikialasorsuaq — is the most productive water in the Arctic and is the
subject of an Inuit-led management commission today. And the method matters as
much as the map: Baffin settled a geographical question by measuring the tide,
and reported his numbers so that somebody else could check them. That is the
same argument this ship makes every day.
