[[person:John Davis]] of Sandridge in Devon made three voyages to these waters
in three consecutive summers, in ships that would look small beside the
Amundsen's launches. His backer was the London merchant
[[person:William Sanderson]]; the narrator of the first and third voyages was
Sanderson's nephew [[person:John Janes]], who sailed as merchant and wrote what
we have. The whole record is in [[source:markham1880davis]], with the engraved
portrait of Davis at [[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-011]].

## 1585: Gilbert Sound and Cumberland Sound

The [[vessel:Sunneshine]] (50 tons) and the [[vessel:Moonshine]] (35 tons) left
Dartmouth on 7 June 1585 and were promptly weatherbound at Falmouth and the
Scillies until 28 June. On 20 July they raised the south-east coast of Greenland
in fog and ice and Davis named it the Land of Desolation. Working round the
southern cape he anchored on 29 July in Gilbert Sound — [[place:Nuup
Kangerlua]], the fjord where Nuuk stands now — and there had the first meeting:

> When they came unto us, we caused our Musicians to play, our selves
> dauncing, and making many signes of friendship.

([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-025]].) A man ashore pointed at the sun and
struck his own breast so hard the English could hear the blow;
[[person:John Ellis]], master of the Moonshine, did the same back until the two
parties trusted each other, and caps, stockings and gloves were thrown ashore.
It is one of the few first meetings in the Arctic record that is not about
theft or violence.

Crossing the strait that now carries his name, Davis made the Baffin coast on
6 August, named Mount Raleigh, Totnes Road, [[place:Exeter Sound]], Dyer's Cape
and Cape Walsingham, and then on 11 August came to the mouth of what he called
Cumberland Gulf — [[place:Cumberland Sound]] — and sailed sixty leagues up it.
Everything he saw said "passage":

> the water of the very coulour, nature, and qualitie of the mayne ocean,
> which gave us the greater hope of our passage

([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-026]].) His reasons were good ones: the land
broke up into islands, the water never freshened, whales came from the west, a
counter-tide struck from the south-west, there was no bottom at 330 fathoms, and
the tide rose and fell six or seven fathoms from more than one direction. Every
one of those is a real observation, correctly made. Every one of them was
misread. Cumberland Sound is a dead end. He was home at Dartmouth on
30 September. The track is [[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-001]].

## 1586: the violent year

Four ships this time. Davis left Dartmouth on 7 May 1586 and was back at
Gilbert Sound on 29 June, where the Kalaallit recognised the English from the
year before. Then it went wrong. On 11 July, after an anchor was stolen, Davis
took a man hostage; the anchor came back; and when the wind came fair he sailed
with the man aboard anyway. The margin of the printed narrative reads only
**"One of the people taken, which after died"**
([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-027]]). Davis describes a companion following
the ship, the captive speaking four or five words and covering his face with
both hands, the other doing the same and leaving; the English understood it as a
sign of death. Davis then dressed his prisoner in an English frieze suit, called
him a pleasant companion, and noted that the ship's people used him well "with
Yliaout, which was the common course of curtesie". The man's name is not
recorded — not here, not anywhere.

On 30 August the Sunneshine's crew killed three men at Gilbert Sound in a fight
over a boat. On 3 September the pinnace [[vessel:Northstarre]] was lost with all
hands. On 6 September two of Davis's sailors were killed and two wounded by
arrows on the Labrador coast. Davis reached the mouth of Hudson Strait on
20 August without understanding what it was, and came home in October. From
Exeter on 14 October, counting his dead, he wrote to Sanderson that he had
[[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-028|brought the passage to that certainty, as
that I am sure it must be in one of foure places, or els not at all]], and
offered to spend his whole inheritance on a third attempt. He did. The track is
[[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-002]].

## 1587: Sanderson's Hope and the Furious Overfall

The [[vessel:Elizabeth]], the Sunneshine and the pinnace [[vessel:Ellen]] left
Dartmouth at midnight on 19 May 1587. At Gilbert Sound on 19 June the master of
the Sunneshine seized "one of the people, a very strong lustie yong fellow";
Markham's note is the whole of his after-history — "What became of him is not
related." That is the second man taken in two years, and the second whose name
is lost.

Davis then made his best northing. On 30 June he stood at 72° 12′ of latitude
off the Greenland coast, named the great cliff [[place:Sanderson's Hope]], and
saw open sea to the west and north. It was the highest latitude yet reached in
these waters and it held for twenty-nine years, until [[person:William Baffin]]
passed it in 1616 — see [[page:baffins-farthest-north]]. The two larger ships
had already broken their promise to wait for him; Davis crossed and worked south
along the Baffin coast in the pinnace, and on 30 and 31 July met the tide races
at the eastern mouth of Hudson Strait:

> This day and night we passed by a very great gulfe, the water whirling and
> roring, as it were the meetings of tides.

([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-029]].) The name [[place:The Furious Overfall]]
is not his — it is the legend engraved on the Molyneux globe of 1592 — and this,
the entrance to Hudson Strait and Hudson Bay, is the single most consequential
thing he found and the one he valued least. He landed at Dartmouth on
15 September and wrote to Sanderson the next day:

> I have bene in 73 degrees, finding the Sea all open, and forty leagues
> betweene land and land. The passage is most probable, the execution easie.

([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-030]]; his journal's own figure is 72° 12′, and
73 is the letter's round number.) The track is
[[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-003]].

## The working papers

Davis is one of the very few Elizabethan navigators whose actual method survives
on paper. Five pages of the traverse book of the 1587 voyage are printed in
facsimile: ruled columns for the month, the day, the course steered, the leagues
run, the latitude by observation, the wind and a remarks column
([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-053]], [[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-054]],
[[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-055]], [[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-056]],
[[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-057]]). Read beside the narrative, they show the
same days from the inside: a latitude, a wind, a line about the ice.

He also wrote the manual. *The Seaman's Secrets*, first printed in 1594 and
dedicated to the Lord High Admiral ([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-058]],
[[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-059]]), teaches the three kinds of sailing and
then the instruments: the thirty-two-point compass
([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-061]]), the table of leagues sailed on each
point to raise a degree of latitude ([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-063]]), the
sphere ([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-065]]), and the projection and graduation
of the cross-staff ([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-067]],
[[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-068]]). The figure that mattered most shows a
seaman with his back to the sun ([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-069]]). Davis's
own instruction is on the same page: the centre of the staff "must be turned to
that part of the Horizon which is from the Sunne, and with your backe toward the
Sunne", the brass plate catching "the shadowe of the transuersary" while the eye
sights the horizon through a slit. This is the back-staff — later called the
Davis quadrant — which let a navigator take the sun's altitude without staring
into it, and it stayed in use at sea for the next two hundred years. It is the
thing of Davis's that outlasted everything else he did.

## What came of it

Nobody funded a fourth voyage: the Armada sailed the next summer, and Davis
went east instead, and was killed on 29 or 30 December 1605 in a fight with
Japanese pirates off Bintang, at the eastern entrance of the Strait of Malacca.
But his charts sent [[person:Henry Hudson]] to the overfall in 1610
([[page:hudsons-fate]]) and Bylot and Baffin up Davis Strait in 1616. Baffin,
who got further than anyone, went out of his way to say Davis had been right:
[[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-045|Neither was Master Davis to be blamed in his
report and great hopes, if hee had anchored about Hope Sanderson]] — at 72° 12′,
Baffin wrote, "the sea is open, and of an vnsearchable depth, and of a good
colour", and the tides there are too small and irregular to have told Davis
anything.

The 1586 vocabulary of about forty Kalaallit words, collected at Gilbert Sound
and printed by Markham with Hinrich Rink's identifications
([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-031]]), is the earliest substantial word list of
Greenlandic taken by an English ship, and it sits in the record beside two
unnamed men who were carried away and did not come back. Both facts are the same
source. See [[page:the-people-they-met]], [[topic:inuit-oral-history]] and
[[topic:greenland-inughuit]].
