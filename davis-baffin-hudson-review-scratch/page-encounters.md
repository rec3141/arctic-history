Every voyage in this topic met people who already lived in the country it
claimed to be finding. The meetings are recorded in only one direction — in
English and Danish, by men writing for backers — and what they record includes
music, trade, four killings by Europeans, at least nine deaths of Europeans,
household goods carried off from empty tents, and two men taken to sea who were
never named. This page sets out what the sources actually say, and what they
cannot say.

## What the record contains

**1585, Gilbert Sound.** [[person:John Davis]]'s first meeting with the
Kalaallit at [[place:Nuup Kangerlua]] is the friendliest scene in the whole
topic: "we caused our Musicians to play, our selves dauncing, and making many
signes of friendship" ([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-025]]). A man ashore
pointed at the sun and struck his breast so hard it could be heard across the
water; [[person:John Ellis]] copied the gesture back until both sides were
satisfied, and caps, stockings and gloves were thrown ashore. They traded
sealskins, salmon and birds for iron.

**1586, the same anchorage.** They were recognised on their return, and the
year turned violent. Davis took a man hostage over a stolen anchor, got the
anchor back, and sailed with him anyway when the wind came fair; the printed
margin reads only "One of the people taken, which after died"
([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-027]]). On 30 August the Sunneshine's crew
killed three men in a fight over a boat. Two of Davis's sailors were killed by
arrows on the Labrador coast a week later.

**1587.** The master of the Sunneshine seized "one of the people, a very strong
lustie yong fellow" at Gilbert Sound on 19 June. Markham's note is the whole of
his after-history: "What became of him is not related."

**1610-11, the bay.** [[person:Henry Hudson]] and his men saw one person all
winter: an Inuk who came to the ship once in the spring, traded skins, said he
would come back, and did not. In July 1611 the starving survivors traded
peacefully at the [[place:Digges Islands]] on the 28th and were attacked on the
29th; [[person:Henry Greene]], William Wilson and John Thomas were killed
outright and Michael Perse died of his wounds ([[page:hudsons-fate]]).

**1612.** [[person:Thomas Button]] anchored at the same islands and lost five
men killed — by the surviving second-hand accounts, in reprisal for canoes his
people had taken.

**1615, the Savage Islands.** This is the fullest description in the topic, and
the least comfortable. [[person:William Baffin]] and [[person:Robert Bylot]]
moored the [[vessel:Discovery]] near an island in [[place:Hudson Strait]] where
the men heard "a great houlinge and noyse, as we supposed of doggs". Baffin
landed with seven others and found five sealskin tents, thirty-five or forty
muzzled dogs, and no people; from the hilltop he saw a large boat with about
fourteen people in it, called to them "using some words of Groynlandish
speeche" and made signs of friendship, which they returned, but neither side
would come closer. He then went back down to the tents, took away fourteen or
fifteen whale fins — baleen, the most valuable thing there — and a small leather
bag containing "a company of little images of men, and one the image of a woman
with a child at hir backe", and left knives, beads and counters in their place.
He describes the dogs pulling sledges shod with bone "as the Lappians doe theare
deare", and the people as living much like those of Greenland. Nobody was there
to consent to the exchange or to refuse it.

**1616, the Greenland coast.** At the Women Islands off Upernavik the
inhabitants fled the ship's approach and left behind two young women hidden on a
rock, two old women and a woman with a child. Off Cape Shackleton in mid-June
forty-two people came out in kayaks over several days to trade sealskins and
narwhal ivory for iron and beads, and Baffin named the place Home Sound. At
Cockin Sound in August, six people brought salmon to trade on three separate
days. He writes down dress, women's facial tattooing, kayaks, a cry of "Ilyout"
made toward the sun, and graves covered over with stones.

**1619, Rin Sund.** On 18 July [[person:Jens Munk]] saw people on the south side
of a harbour in Hudson Strait and went ashore to them. They had laid their
weapons and implements down behind some stones and stood between him and the
place where they lay. Munk went over, picked the weapons up and examined them,
and they made him understand "that they would rather lose all their garments and
go naked than lose their arms and implements", pointing to their mouths: this was
how they ate. He put them down again and they clapped their hands and looked up
to heaven. He gave them knives and iron goods, and a looking-glass, which the man
did not recognise until Munk held it up to his face, and then put in his bosom.
They gave him birds and seal flesh in return. One of Munk's men had a swarthy
complexion and black hair, and all of them embraced him, "no doubt thinking that
he was one of their nation and countrymen". Munk stayed four days hoping they
would come back. Nobody came, and they did not even return for their fishing gear
while his ships lay there. It is the only meeting of the whole Danish expedition.

**1619-20, the Churchill.** At [[place:Kuugjuaq]] Munk sent nineteen men three
miles inland on 7 November to see whether anyone lived there and they found
nobody, though they found summer camp sites, hearths and cut wood. On the night
of 14 November his watch shot an animal it took for a black fox and found in the
morning that it was a large dog trained to catch game, its nose rubbed bare
where cords had been tied round it. Sixty-one of his men died there over the
winter ([[page:munk-at-the-churchill]]).

## The two men who were taken

The 1586 hostage and the 1587 captive are the two clearest facts in this topic
that the record refuses to complete. Davis describes the first man's companion
following the ship, the captive speaking four or five words and covering his
face with both hands, and the other man doing the same and going away; the
English read it as a sign of death. Davis then gave him an English frieze suit
and called him a pleasant companion. He died at sea. The second man was simply
taken and "what became of him is not related".

Neither man's name is anywhere. Neither is any Kalaaleq's or Inuk's name in the
whole of the Davis, Hudson, Button, Baffin, Munk, Foxe or James record. What
does survive is [[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-031|about forty words of their
language]], written down at Gilbert Sound in 1586 and printed with Hinrich
Rink's identifications in 1880: nerisinait, aiguk, umiamik, pautik, savingmik,
tugto, kuninga, kissuk. The vocabulary and the kidnapping are on facing pages of
the same book.

That absence should be stated rather than filled in. There is no basis for
naming these men, and inventing names or motives for them would be a second
erasure. What can be said is that they were somebody's son, brother or husband
in a community around Nuup Kangerlua that is still there, and that this is what
the written record is like: it preserves the ship's business.

## Reading the encounters honestly

Three things are worth holding onto when the accounts are used.

First, the violence runs both ways and the sources only count one side. Nine
Europeans were killed by Inuit in these voyages: four with Hudson at the Digges
Islands in 1611 and five with Button at the same place in 1612. At least four
Indigenous men were killed by Europeans — three Kalaallit at Gilbert Sound in
1586, and one Inuk killed by [[person:Abacuk Pricket]] at the Digges Islands in
1611 — and two men were carried away to sea. The European deaths are named,
dated and mourned in print. The others are a line in a margin.

Second, the attacks are not unmotivated, and the sources contain the motive
without noticing it. Hudson's men fought at the Digges Islands in 1611, ten
months after Hudson's own visit and a year before Button lost five men in the
same place over canoes his people had taken. Theft of boats, of iron, of tents'
contents, of people had preceded every one of these fights. Davis, watching the
Kalaallit take iron from his ships, wrote that their simplicity gave him occasion
for laughter; when his own men later killed three of them he was not there.

Third, these encounters were the beginning of a continuous relationship, not
isolated incidents. Baffin's report of whales in Whale Sound
([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-043]]) is the same information that brought the
whaling fleets into Baffin Bay two centuries later, with everything that followed
for the communities of Qikiqtaaluk (Baffin Island) and Kalaallit Nunaat — see
[[topic:whaling]] and [[topic:greenland-inughuit]]. The charts made from these
voyages are the reason later ships knew where to go.

Inuit accounts of this period exist as oral history rather than as documents,
and they are the other half of the record: see [[topic:inuit-oral-history]],
[[topic:thule-dorset]] for who was living along these coasts when the ships
arrived, and [[topic:hudson-strait-south-baffin]] for the communities whose
waters most of these meetings happened in. The Qikiqtani Truth Commission's
principle is the right one to apply to a page like this: the written record of
outsiders is evidence about the outsiders as much as about anyone else.
