[[person:Henry Hudson]] sailed from St Katharine's Pool in London on 17 April
1610 in the [[vessel:Discovery]], a bark of about 55 tons, with some twenty-two
men. He was looking for the tide race [[person:John Davis]] had reported at the
mouth of what is now [[place:Hudson Strait]]. He found it, went through it, and
did not come back. The whole track is [[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-004]].

## What is known, and how

Two kinds of document survive, and the difference between them matters more
than anything else on this page.

The first is Hudson's own abstract journal, printed in [[source:asher1860henry]]
([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-022]]). It is precise, dry and full of observed
latitudes — the Orkneys at 59° 22′, the Faroes at 62° 24′, Cape Farewell at
59° 27′, the Isles of God's Mercies at 62° 9′ — and it stops on 3 August 1610,
the day he named [[place:Cape Wolstenholme]] and Cape Digges for his backers and
entered the bay: "the head of this entrance on the south side I named Cape
Worsenholme; and the head on the north-wester shoare I called Cape Digs." After
that date there is no more of Hudson.

The second is the narrative of [[person:Abacuk Pricket]], a haberdasher and the
servant of [[person:Dudley Digges]], one of the men who paid for the voyage. He
was aboard as his master's eye, not as a seaman. He came home alive. He wrote
the only eyewitness account of the mutiny that exists. Purchas, who printed it
in 1625, distrusted it and put Thomas Woodhouse's note beside it so it could be
tested; Asher, editing it in 1860, called it an apologetical essay whose real
intention was to defend the mutineers. Everything below that is not from
Hudson's own journal is Pricket's word, and Pricket had reason to shape it: he
survived because the mutineers kept him alive, and every man he names as a
ringleader was dead before the ship reached England and could not answer him.

## The bay, and the winter

Hudson spent August, September and October 1610 working down the east side of
the bay and into the shallow bottom of it, looking for the way through — three
months, in Pricket's phrase, of a labyrinth without end. On 10 September there
was a shipboard trial: [[person:Robert Juet]], the mate, was disrated for words
tending to mutiny and [[person:Robert Bylot]] put in his place. That is
recorded not by Pricket but in a note found in the desk of Thomas Woodhouse, a
young student of mathematics who was later set adrift and so had no reason to
flatter anyone, and it independently confirms Pricket's picture of Juet.

The ship was hauled aground in the south-east corner of [[place:James Bay]] on
1 November — "and this was the first of November; by the tenth thereof we were
frozen in". The gunner John Williams died in mid-November; the quarrel over who
should have the dead man's coat — Hudson gave it to [[person:Henry Greene]] — is
one of the hinges Pricket hangs the mutiny on. Woodhouse recorded a decoction of
spruce buds drunk against the scurvy, which is the same remedy the Haudenosaunee
had given Cartier at Stadacona seventy-five years earlier. One Inuk came to the
ship once in the spring, traded skins, said he would come back, and did not.

## 21 and 22 June 1611

The ship got out of the harbour on 18 June and was caught in ice the same
night. Provisions were down to a fortnight's bread. Hudson shared out the last
of it, a pound a man, with a written bill of return, "and hee wept when hee
gave it unto them" ([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-039]]); he also had the
men's chests searched for hoarded bread, which is the grievance the rest was
built on.

On the night of Saturday 21 June, by Pricket's account, William Wilson and Henry
Greene came to him where he lay lame and told him they meant to turn the master
and the sick men into the shallop. Pricket says he argued. Greene's answer was
that [[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-040|of the two he would rather be hanged at
home then starved abroad]]. Juet joined, and an oath was sworn. At dawn:

> then came John Thomas and Bennet before him, while Wilson bound his armes
> behind him.

([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-036]]; the printed margin beside the line reads
"They bind the master".) Pricket says he then went on his knees and "besought
them, for the love of God, to remember themselves, and to doe as they would be
done unto" ([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-037]]). That sentence is Pricket
writing about Pricket, seven years before he stood trial for what happened next,
and it should be read as such.

Nine people went into the boat: Henry Hudson; his son John, a boy; Arnold
Lodlo; Sidrack Faner; Thomas Woodhouse; Adam Moore; Henry King; Michael Bute;
and the carpenter [[person:Philip Staffe]], who was not driven out and chose to
go. The shallop was towed clear of the ice astern, and then

> they cut her head fast from the sterne of our ship, then out with their
> top-sayles, and towards the east they stood in a cleere sea.

([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-038]].) No trace of any of them was ever found.
[[person:Thomas Button]] looked for them in 1612-13 and found nothing.

A note on the date: Asher's introduction and his synoptic table both say
21 June, and most books repeat it. Pricket's own text puts the oath on the night
of Saturday the 21st and the binding of Hudson at dawn, which is the 22nd. Both
dates are in the record here, as separate events, and the disagreement is
Asher's rather than the manuscript's.

## The Digges Islands, and home

Bylot navigated the ship back north-east across the bay. The survivors were
starving; they flayed the fowl they caught and fried the skins. At the
[[place:Digges Islands]] on 28 July they traded peacefully with Inuit; on the
29th, when the boat went in again, they were attacked. Greene, Wilson and John
Thomas were killed outright, Michael Perse was mortally wounded and died two
days later, and Pricket, wounded in the boat, killed the man who came at him.
Only the English side of this is recorded. What the people of the Digges Islands
understood themselves to be doing — whether this was an answer to something
done on the 28th, or to Hudson's men the year before — is not in any document.
See [[page:the-people-they-met]].

Juet died of hunger within sight of Ireland on about 3 September:
[[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-041|Then Robert Juet dyed for meere want, and all
our men were in despaire]]. A fishing bark out of Fowey found the Discovery on
6 September and brought her into Bere Haven with eight men alive.

## Nobody hanged

Every man Pricket names as a leader was dead before the ship reached England:
Greene, Wilson and Thomas at the Digges Islands, Perse of his wounds, Juet of
hunger. There was no prosecution for seven years. In 1618 Pricket, Edward
Wilson and two of their shipmates were put through a form of trial before the
Admiralty court, charged with murder rather than mutiny, and all four were
acquitted; the court's reasoning, as Neatby summarises it in
[[source:neatby1966hudson]], was that it was not murder to turn experienced
seamen adrift near a shore that was neither totally barren nor uninhabited. By
then Pricket had been back to the bay with Button and Bylot had twice commanded
the Discovery himself, and had sailed further north than any European had
([[page:baffins-farthest-north]]).

## What came of it

The papers the Discovery brought home were engraved in Amsterdam in 1612 as
Hessel Gerritsz's Tabula nautica ([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-012]]), the
first printed chart of the strait and the bay, and the reason the bay carries
Hudson's name and not his crew's. The Hakluyt Society's 1860 facsimile
([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-080]]) shows what it actually claims: the new
inland sea labelled "Mare Magnum de M. Hudson", Fretum Davis and Groenlandia
named, the royal arms, four compass roses and a fleet under sail. Asher printed
beside it a north polar chart from Purchas ([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-081]])
that shows how little of the top of the world anyone could yet draw. The
southern end of Hudson's bay is left open on the Tabula nautica, which is
exactly the shape that sent Button in 1612, [[person:Jens Munk]] in 1619
([[page:munk-at-the-churchill]]), and [[person:Luke Foxe]] and
[[person:Thomas James]] in 1631 after it.

Two and a half centuries later John Collier painted the shallop with Hudson
upright in the stern ([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-013]]); it is the image most
people carry, and it is not evidence. Collier had only Pricket to work from, and
Pricket was one of the men who put them there.
