On the morning of 9 August 1818 two Royal Navy ships, the *Isabella* and the
*Alexander*, lay in a bight of [[place:Qimusseriarsuaq|Melville Bay]] at, by
their own reckoning, 75° 55′ N and 65° 32′ W. Their orders were to find the
North-West Passage with all possible despatch. Eight sledges came out towards
them over the ice, halted a mile off, and the drivers climbed an iceberg to
look. The ships stayed where they were.

Neither people had known the other existed. The Inughuit had never seen a ship;
the Admiralty had no idea anyone lived north of Melville Bay. What followed is
the only well-documented first contact in the Arctic in which the two principals
were both Inuit.

## The man in the middle

[[person:John Sacheuse]] — Hans Zakaeus in his own language — was a Kalaallit
hunter from the Disko district of west Greenland who had reached Leith about
1816 as a stowaway on a whaler, had been taught in Edinburgh, and had volunteered
for the voyage. He was about twenty-one. On the morning of 10 August he asked
leave to go out alone, unarmed, with a white flag and some presents; a crack in
the floe too wide to step across separated the two parties, which is why
[[person:John Ross]] allowed it.

Sacheuse planted his flag, took off his hat, and beckoned. The Inughuit shouted;
he imitated the shout. They came nearer. He decided they were speaking something
close to a dialect he knew, drawled out to unusual length, and switched into it.

> **Sacheuse:** *Kahkeite* — "Come on!"
> **They:** *Naakrie, naakrieai-plaite* — "No, no — go away."

The boldest of them drew a knife from his boot and said, "Go away! I can kill
you." Sacheuse threw across a string of beads and a checked shirt, which they
looked at with alarm. Then he threw an English knife and said, "Take that." They
picked it up, shouted, and pulled their noses; Sacheuse pulled his nose and
called back, "Heigh, yaw!"

Then the questions began, and they are worth reading exactly as Ross set them
down. [[artifact:greenland-inughuit-010|The Inughuit asked first about the
ships]]: "What great creatures those were? Do they come from the sun or the moon?
Do they give us light by night or by day?" Sacheuse said he was a man, that he
had a father and mother like themselves, and that he came from a country far to
the south. They told him that could not be, because there was nothing but ice to
the south. He said the ships were houses made of wood. They said no, they are
alive, we have seen them move their wings.

Asked what they themselves were, they said they were men, that they lived to the
north, that there was much open water there, and that they had come here to fish
for sea unicorns — narwhals.

## Aboard

They would not cross to the ships until a plank was laid. When they finally came
aboard the *Isabella*, [[person:Ervick]] — about forty, the senior of the party
— came with his brother's sons [[person:Marshuick]] and
[[person:Otooniah (1818)]]. They spat out the salt meat they were offered. Ross,
trying to establish how many their nation numbered, pointed to the fragments of
ice alongside and asked whether there were as many people as that. They could
only count to ten, and answered: "Many more."

[[artifact:greenland-inughuit-019|Sacheuse learned afterwards]] what they had
actually been doing. They had sent their women and children into the mountains.
Their purpose in coming out was to ask the strangers to go away and not destroy
them. And they had waited on the iceberg for half an hour first, watching to see
whether the ships would fly off to the sun or the moon, from one of which they
had concluded the ships must have come. One man had been so frightened that he
ran into the hills and never came back.

Ross questioned Ervick about religion, decided that no word for a Supreme Being
could be got across, and wrote the Arctic Highlanders down as having no idea of
one. That conclusion says more about the interview than about the Inughuit,
whose cosmology [[person:Knud Rasmussen]] recorded at length eighty-five years
later.

He also asked Ervick and [[person:Meigack]] whether either would give up one of
his sons to be taken to England. Neither would.

## The iron

The most consequential thing said in those three days was said almost by
accident. The Inughuit knives had blades of iron, and iron does not occur in
useable native form anywhere in the Arctic.
[[artifact:greenland-inughuit-018|Meigack told Ross where it came from]]: a
mountain near the shore, at a place recorded as Sowallick, where several large
masses lay, one harder than the rest and part of the mountain itself. They cut
pieces off with a hard stone and beat them flat into flakes the size of a
sixpence. He said his family came each summer from Petowack to take seals and
narwhals and to get iron, and went home when the sun left them.

Sabine's party had landed on 9 August on what turned out to be a point almost
attached to the shore, with the iron mountain rising directly behind it, and had
not walked far enough. By the time Ross understood this the ships had gone past.

The specimens Ross took home were analysed and found to contain nickel. That
made the source meteoric — but nobody could find it. Finding "the Iron Mountain"
became a standing objective of Arctic expeditions for three-quarters of a
century: the *North Star* looked for it in 1849-50, the British expedition of
1875-76 looked for it (see [[topic:nares-greely]]), Nordenskiöld's ship went to
Cape York expressly for it in 1883 and could not get through the ice. In May 1894
an Inughuk hunter walked [[person:Robert Edwin Peary]] straight to it, and within
three years all three masses were in New York. That story is
[[page:peary-removals-and-minik|here]].

## Afterwards

Ross sailed on into Lancaster Sound, saw a range of mountains that was not there,
turned back, and spent the rest of his life defending the decision; see
[[topic:ross-parry]]. Sacheuse drew the meeting on the ice, and the drawing was
engraved for Ross's book — [[artifact:ross-parry-010|the earliest picture of the
Inughuit, made by an Inuk]]. He died at Edinburgh in 1819, aged about
twenty-two, before the next expedition sailed.

For the Inughuit, 1818 changed nothing immediately. No ship came back for
thirty-one years. But the district was on the charts now, and by the 1850s it
would be the staging ground for every American and British attempt on the pole.
