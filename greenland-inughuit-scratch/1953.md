On 25 May 1953 the people of [[place:Uummannaq (Thule)]], the settlement under
the flat-topped mountain on North Star Bay, were told they had to leave. Within
a few days — the sources disagree on exactly how few — twenty-six families,
116 people by the count that ended up in the court record, harnessed their dogs
and drove north over ice that would not carry a sledge much longer. They left
their houses, the hospital, the school, the radio station, the warehouses, the
church and the graveyard. The houses were later burned.

They stopped about 130 km north at a hunting camp called
[[place:Qaanaaq]] and built a town there.

This page tells that story in the same register as [[topic:relocations-qtc]]
tells the Canadian removals, because it is the same kind of event: a state
moving Inuit out of the way of a Cold War installation, without their consent,
and then arguing for fifty years about what it owed them.

## The base

Denmark and the United States signed an agreement on the defence of Greenland in
Copenhagen on **27 April 1951**. It entered into force on 8 June. A secret
technical appendix, which specified what would actually be built and where, was
never put before the Danish parliament; the Danish Supreme Court held in 2003
that this did not invalidate the agreement. No one asked the Inughuit anything.

On **9 July 1951** a convoy that had sailed from Norfolk a month earlier arrived
in North Star Bay with some 12,000 men and 300,000 tons of cargo. Operation Blue
Jay built most of an airfield in about sixty days, four kilometres from a
settlement of about a hundred and twenty hunters and their families. There had
been a precedent: a weather station put into the district in 1946, for which the
Hunters' Council received about 200 kroner.

In the spring of 1953 the Americans asked to add an anti-aircraft unit and to
extend the defence area over the whole Dundas peninsula. Denmark agreed. That is
the decision that produced 25 May.

## The move

What is firm: the date of notice (25 May 1953), the number of people (116), and
that they went by dog sledge while the sea ice held.

What is not firm, and should be flagged as such: the notice period. Kenn Harper
writes that they were told to leave immediately. The *Arctic Yearbook* says four
days. The Eastern High Court, in 1999, put it in the most careful available
form: **the tribe had had too little time to prepare their departure.** No source
consulted here gives the date they actually left.

Eleven days after the notice, on **5 June 1953**, the revised Danish constitution
came into force, making Greenlanders full Danish citizens. Scholarship on the
case notes the sequence and asks whether the timing was deliberate. That is a
question, not a finding.

At Qaanaaq the families lived in tents from late May until **September 1953**,
when twenty-seven wooden houses were finished, together with a school, a church,
a hospital, administration buildings, a power station and roads. It is often
written that they wintered in tents; on the court record they did not. What
survivors describe instead is housing built at speed and badly insulated. One
woman remembered looking back at her house and watching it flip over in the
wind.

The whole relocation cost about 8.65 million kroner, of which the United States
paid 700,000 dollars. Average family income in the district at the time was
estimated at 1,500 kroner a year.

## What was lost

Not just a village. The Uummannaq people had hunted the Dundas peninsula and the
waters off it; the defence area, whose exact size is disputed but was of the
order of 2,700 square kilometres, took that ground out of use. In the mid-1960s
a further settlement, [[place:Moriusaq]], was established between Qaanaaq and the
base to adapt to the changed hunting. The base area was halved by agreement in
1986.

Then there is Pituffik itself. On **21 January 1968** a B-52 carrying four
hydrogen bombs caught fire and came down on the sea ice of
[[place:North Star Bay]], about twelve kilometres west of the base. The
conventional explosives detonated and plutonium was scattered over the ice. The
cleanup, Project Crested Ice, ran to September and removed more than two
thousand cubic metres of contaminated ice and water. Whether the secondary of the
fourth weapon was ever recovered is genuinely disputed: a BBC investigation in
2008 said three were accounted for, a Danish study in 2009 concluded there was
no missing bomb. Danish workers on the cleanup were compensated in 1995.
[[person:Uusaqqak Qujaukitsoq]], who was twenty, watched the aircraft come down.

## The case

In 1996 the association **Hingitaq 53** — *the discarded*, the expelled of 1953 —
was founded, and on **20 December 1996** it sued the Prime Minister's Office. It
asked for four things: the right to live at Uummannaq, the right to move and hunt
in the whole Thule district, 25 million kroner to the tribe, and 250,000 kroner
to each person.

The **Eastern High Court**, on **20 August 1999**, in a judgment of 502 pages:

- held the base lawfully established under the 1951 agreement;
- held that the 1951 hunting restrictions and the 1953 eviction were
  interferences so serious that they amounted to **expropriations**;
- found that the tribe had had too little time to prepare their departure;
- held the claims not time-barred, and Denmark bound by Article 73 of the UN
  Charter;
- **refused** the right of return and the district-wide hunting right;
- awarded **500,000 kroner to the tribe**, **25,000 kroner to each person who was
  over eighteen in 1953**, **15,000 to each aged four to eighteen**, and nothing
  to those under four. No pecuniary damages: the substitute housing was held to
  be full compensation for the houses lost.

On **2 September 1999** the Danish Prime Minister, Poul Nyrup Rasmussen,
apologised — *utoqqatserpugut* — "to the Inughuit, the inhabitants of Thule, and
to the rest of Greenland, for the way in which the decision regarding the forced
movement was made and carried out in 1953." Read the sentence carefully: it
apologises for the *way* the decision was made and carried out, and it comes
attached to the observation that no one can be made responsible for actions
committed by past generations.

The **Supreme Court**, on **28 November 2003**, upheld the High Court
unanimously. The Prime Minister's Office had by then conceded that the relocation
"was decided and carried out in a way and under circumstances that constituted a
serious interference and unlawful conduct towards the population of Uummannaq."
The court nonetheless refused to increase the awards and refused the right of
return. And it held that the Thule tribe is **not** a distinct indigenous or
tribal people within the meaning of ILO Convention 169, because they now "in all
essential respects live under the same conditions as the rest of the Greenlandic
people." Denmark's ratification of the convention had declared that Greenland has
only one indigenous people, the Inuit.

That last holding is the live one. It is what the 2021 *Arctic Yearbook* paper
argues endangers Inughuit livelihood: a people whose distinctness is measured
against how much of it survived the removal.

The **European Court of Human Rights** declared the application inadmissible on
**12 January 2006**. The acts of 1951 and 1953 were instantaneous and had happened
before the Convention entered into force for Denmark on 3 September 1953, so were
outside its competence; on what remained, the Danish courts had not been
arbitrary and had struck a fair balance. Four hundred and twenty-eight Inughuit
were applicants.

## Now

At 1 January 2026 Statistics Greenland counted **586 people at Qaanaaq**, 40 at
[[place:Savissivik]], 32 at [[place:Siorapaluk]] and 25 at Qeqertat: **684 in the
district**, and falling. Moriusaq was abandoned in 2010. Fifty-five people are
registered as residents at Pituffik, which does not include the American and
contractor personnel on the base. In 2023 the base was renamed Pituffik Space
Base.

The right of return has never been granted. A memorandum of understanding in
February 2003 separated Dundas from the defence area, but the Supreme Court held
that the tribe could claim no privileged access even to that. Hingitaq 53 has
continued to press the case internationally, including in a joint submission to
the UN Universal Periodic Review in 2011 with the Inuit Circumpolar Council.

## A note on comparison

The Canadian High Arctic relocations of 1953-55 moved Inuit families from
Inukjuak and Mittimatalik to [[place:Ausuittuq]] and [[place:Qausuittuq]] for
reasons that included sovereignty, and produced a report by the Royal Commission
on Aboriginal Peoples in 1994 and an apology in 2010. See
[[topic:relocations-qtc]]. The Greenland case ran on a different legal track and
came out differently: an admission of unlawful conduct, a small award, an apology
for the manner of the decision, and no right to go home. The two are worth
reading together and are not the same story.
