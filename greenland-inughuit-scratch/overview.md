The Inughuit live on the north-west shoulder of Greenland, between the glacier
front of [[place:Qimusseriarsuaq|Melville Bay]] and the head of Smith Sound.
They call the district [[place:Avanersuaq]] — "the place farthest north" — and
themselves *Inughuit*, the great people. Roughly 800 of them live there now,
most at [[place:Qaanaaq]], the rest at [[place:Siorapaluk]],
[[place:Savissivik]] and one or two other settlements. Siorapaluk is the
northernmost place in the world where people live year round by choice.

This page is the frame for six others. What follows is the shape of the story.

## A population alone

For most of the last thousand years the Inughuit were the most isolated human
community on earth. Melville Bay, a hundred and fifty miles of calving glacier
with no coast to travel, cut them off from the Kalaallit of the Danish colonies
to the south. Smith Sound and the ice of Nares Strait cut them off — except when
they did not — from the Canadian side.

They were not, however, without history. Their ancestors came across from Arctic
Canada as part of the Thule expansion of about the thirteenth century, and on
[[place:Ruin Island]] and the Ellesmere shore the same houses hold Norse iron,
Norse chain mail and chips of Cape York meteorite iron: see
[[topic:norse-helluland]]. They worked meteoric iron into knife and harpoon
edges for centuries before any European saw the metal.

Isolation had a cost. Some time before 1820 an epidemic killed the older
generation of the district, and with them went the knowledge of how to build a
kayak, how to make a bow, and how to spear salmon in a river. The kayaks of the
dead had been buried with their owners. When the survivors' grandchildren
described this to Knud Rasmussen a lifetime later, they said flatly that their
forefathers had known these things and that they themselves had had to be taught
them again. [[page:qitdlarssuaq-migration|Who taught them, and how, is the best
documented tribal migration in Inuit history.]]

## 1818: the ships

On 10 August 1818 two Royal Navy ships lay beset in a bight of Melville Bay that
John Ross named Prince Regent's Bay. Eight sledges came out over the ice. The
man who walked out alone and unarmed to meet them was
[[person:John Sacheuse]], a Greenlandic Inuk from the Disko district who had
reached Scotland as a stowaway; the man who answered him was
[[person:Ervick]]. The conversation that followed — "Do they come from the sun
or the moon?" — is the first recorded exchange between the Inughuit and
Europeans, and it happened between two Inuit.
[[page:the-1818-meeting|The meeting is told in full here.]]

## The workforce

From 1853 the Smith Sound shore became the base camp of American and British
attempts on the North Pole, and every one of them ran on Inughuit labour, dogs,
clothing and meat. [[person:Elisha Kent Kane]] survived his second winter
because [[person:Metek]] and [[person:Kalutunah]] fed his people;
[[person:Hans Hendrik]], a Kalaallit hunter from south Greenland, served four
expeditions over twenty-three years and wrote his own book about it;
[[person:Merkut]], an Inughuit woman of Etah, built snow houses through the
six-month drift of the Polaris floe party. See [[topic:kane-hall-polaris]] and
[[topic:nares-greely]].

[[person:Robert Edwin Peary]] went further than any of them. Over eight voyages
between 1886 and 1909 he made the whole district into a labour and supply
system: he hired the men by name, shipped whole families north on the
*Roosevelt*, dressed his expeditions in clothing Inughuit women sewed, and drove
to his farthest north in 1909 with [[person:Matthew Henson]] and four Inughuit —
[[person:Ootah]], Egingwah, Seegloo and Ooqueah. See
[[topic:sverdrup-bernier-peary]] for the sovereignty and Pole-claim side of
this. [[page:inughuit-and-the-explorers|The labour side is here.]]

## What Peary took

Peary also removed things. In May 1894 an Inughuk hunter guided him to the
*saviksue*, the three great irons of Cape York, from which his people had cut
their knife metal for centuries. Peary shipped two of them out in 1895 and the
largest, [[artifact:sverdrup-bernier-peary-031|Ahnighito]], in 1897, and sold
all three to the American Museum of Natural History for forty thousand dollars.
On the same 1897 voyage he brought six Inughuit to New York at the request of
the anthropologist [[person:Franz Boas]]. Four were dead of tuberculosis within
eight months, and the museum kept their bones. One of the survivors was a boy,
[[person:Minik Wallace]], who grew up in New York and learned that the funeral
he had attended for his father [[person:Qisuk]] had been staged over a log.
[[page:peary-removals-and-minik|That story is told in full here.]]

## Thule

In 1910 [[person:Knud Rasmussen]] and [[person:Peter Freuchen]] built a trading
station at [[place:Uummannaq (Thule)]] on North Star Bay, to give the Inughuit a
market for fox skins that was not a passing ship. The station named itself
Thule, and the district took the name. Its profits paid for seven Thule
expeditions. The Fifth, from 1921 to 1924, put two Inughuit —
[[person:Arnarulunnguaq]] and [[person:Qaavigarsuaq]] — across Arctic North
America from Hudson Bay to the Pacific, the longest dog-sledge journey on
record. [[page:thule-station-and-expeditions|The station and the expeditions.]]

## 1953

In 1951 the United States began building an airbase at
[[place:Pituffik]], on the hunting ground of the Uummannaq people, under a
defence agreement signed with Denmark and never put to the Inughuit. In May 1953
the twenty-six families of Uummannaq were given a few days' notice and moved
about 130 km north. They built [[place:Qaanaaq]] themselves, in tents, through
the following winter. The Danish Supreme Court found in 2003 that the removal
had been unlawful; it did not grant the community the right of return. The story
belongs beside the Canadian removals in [[topic:relocations-qtc]], and is told
in the same terms. [[page:the-1953-move-to-qaanaaq|Here.]]

## Why it matters on this ship

Three reasons a scientist working the eastern Arctic should know this history.

First, the ice. The Inughuit crossed Smith Sound on foot behind
[[person:Qitdlarssuaq]] in the 1860s and did it routinely afterwards; the North
Water polynya at the head of Baffin Bay, which the *Amundsen* works, is the
reason there was game to cross to. The polynya is the oldest and most productive
of its kind in the Arctic, and the settlement pattern of both shores is drawn
around it.

Second, the record. Rasmussen's Thule reports and *The People of the Polar
North* are the fullest ethnographic record of any Inuit population, and they are
largely dictated: Merqusaq's account of the migration is his own words, taken
down by a man who spoke his language.

Third, the continuity. The families moved out of Uummannaq in 1953 are the
families at Qaanaaq now, and the Hingitaq 53 association still asks for the right
to return. The bones of the four who died in New York in 1897 and 1898 were
returned to Qaanaaq for burial in 1993. These are not closed files.
