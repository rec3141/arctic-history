Some time in the middle of the nineteenth century a shaman of the Tununirmiut
of north Baffin Island told his village that his soul had flown over the sea and
found a country where other Inuit lived. He proposed to go there. Nine sledges
joined him at once.

    "Do you know the desire for new countries? Do you know the desire
    to see new people?"

That is [[artifact:greenland-inughuit-011|what he said]], as one of the
children who went with him repeated it to [[person:Knud Rasmussen]] fifty years
later. His name was [[person:Qitdlarssuaq]]. The journey he led is the only
tribal migration of Inuit anywhere that we have from the people who made it.

## The record

Almost everything narrative we know comes from one man.
[[person:Merqusaq]] was born on the sea ice on a winter journey somewhere on the
Baffin side, was a half-grown boy when the party reached Greenland, and was an
old man bent with rheumatism when Rasmussen wintered in
[[place:Avanersuaq]] in 1903-04. He travelled up to Saunders Island that year
because he had heard Rasmussen wanted to talk to him.
[[artifact:greenland-inughuit-002|His account]] fills a chapter of *The People
of the Polar North*: the names of all thirty-eight who set out, the dimensions
of the sledges (twenty feet long, four wide, to carry the kayaks), why you do
not drive more than twelve dogs on traces of equal length, and what happened in
the houses when the food ran out.

Merqusaq gave no years and few place names. The calendar comes from a second
source: Father Guy Mary-Rousselière spent decades matching the oral traditions
of both ends of the migration against explorers' journals, Dundee whaling logs
and newspapers, and published the result as
[[source:maryrousseliere1991qitdlarssuaq|*Qitdlarssuaq: The Story of a Polar
Migration*]]. In 1972 he took elders from Mittimatalik, Ikpiarjuk and Iglulik to
Qaanaaq so that the two traditions could be compared in person. The dates below
are his, and they are reconstructions, not records. Where they and Merqusaq
disagree, both are given.

## The route

The party left the Tununirmiut country about **1852**, thirty-eight people in
ten sledges — men, women and children, with kayaks lashed on top. They crossed
Lancaster Sound and spent about five years on Devon Island. Two of the Franklin
search expeditions ran into them there: **Inglefield in 1853**, who had a West
Greenlandic interpreter aboard and who, Mary-Rousselière argues, was probably
where Qitdlarssuaq first learned where the far-northern people actually lived;
and **McClintock at [[place:Dundas Harbour]] in 1858**.

The travelling pattern was seasonal. They started each year in winter, after the
light came, and camped when the ice broke; they hunted from kayaks through the
open season, built stone houses roofed with turf for the dark, and moved on when
the light returned. There was no shortage of food for the first two years —
seal, white whale, walrus, bear, auk cliffs and eider islands.

Then, on the Ellesmere coast about **1862**, the party broke apart.
[[person:Oqe]], one of the oldest men, grew silent, and then began talking about
whale meat. He was homesick. He said Qitdlarssuaq had invented the whole thing
to make himself a leader; Qitdlarssuaq said Oqe was envious.
**Five sledges and twenty-four people turned back with Oqe; five sledges and
fourteen went on** — including, Merqusaq notes drily, Oqe's own son. Mary-
Rousselière puts the split at [[place:Talbot Inlet]].

Late in the following spring they came to a place where the sea narrowed.
Qitdlarssuaq made camp, held an incantation, and announced that this was where
they were to cross. They crossed the frozen [[place:Smith Sound]], found empty
houses on the far shore, and went on to [[place:Etah]], where they killed
caribou inland. Then someone shouted "Sledges!" and two men drove up:
[[person:Arrutsak]], who ran on a wooden leg his mother had made him after he
fell from a bird cliff, and [[person:Agina]], from [[place:Pitoravik]]. The year
was **1862 or 1863**.

## What each people taught the other

The Inughuit had been alone for a very long time, and an epidemic some
generations earlier had killed the old people who knew how things were made. The
kayaks had been buried with their owners.

Merqusaq's list of what the newcomers taught is
[[artifact:greenland-inughuit-012|the most consequential passage in the whole
account]]:

- the snow house built in a spiral with a tunnel entrance from below, so no
  draught reaches the sitting room;
- the bow and arrow — before this, he says, the Inughuit did not hunt the
  caribou in their own country, and if one was killed by accident they fed it to
  the dogs rather than risk eating it;
- the leister, for spearing salmon and char in the rivers;
- the kayak, and hunting from it.

Before the kayak came back the Inughuit could hunt only from the ice, and had to
take enough seal, walrus and narwhal in spring to last the whole open-water
summer. Mary-Rousselière, comparing the head counts Kane made in 1855 and Hayes
in 1867, argues that the arrival of the Baffin Islanders may have saved the
population from dying out altogether. That is a strong claim and it rests on two
rough censuses, but no one disputes the direction of it.

The traffic went both ways. "We adopted their type of sledge," Merqusaq said,
"for it was better than ours, and had uprights on it."

## The return

After about seven years the old man wanted to see his own country before he
died, and almost everyone who had followed him north made ready to follow him
back. Only his son [[person:Itsukusuk]] stayed, because a child was ill. A local
man named Ere came along to see the country they had come from.

Qitdlarssuaq died in the first winter of the journey, somewhere between
[[place:Cape Sabine]] and [[place:Cape Herschel]] on the Ellesmere coast, about
**1870**. After that everything went wrong. The party wintered at
[[place:Makinson Inlet]], failed to find game in the spring, moved inland in the
autumn to a fish lake, and starved through the dark.

    "Qitdlarssuaq's wife, Agpaq, and my father and mother and Ere's, died of
    hunger. And those who were left, and who refused the salmon, began to eat
    the dead bodies. Minik and Mataq were the worst. I saw them eat my father
    and my mother. I was too young and could not stop them. Then one day Minik
    flung himself upon me from behind, to kill me and eat me."

Merqusaq lost an eye in that struggle and his brother saved his life. Of the
twenty-one who had started back, five reached [[place:Avanersuaq]] again, about
**1873** — hauling their own sledges, having eaten the dogs. In the same year
Emil Bessels of the *Polaris* met Baffin Islanders near Etah who told him some
of their party had gone home years before; see [[topic:kane-hall-polaris]].

Oqe's twenty-four, who had turned back in 1862, fared no better. Mary-
Rousselière pieces their end together from Baffin Island stories and the log of
the Dundee whaler *Ravenscraig*: famine, killing and cannibalism again, with two
survivors, Qimmingajak and his wife Angiliq.

## What it left

The genetic and cultural trace stayed. When Rasmussen arrived in 1903 he found
that the immigrants had merged completely into the Cape York people by marriage
and wife-exchange within fifty years, though they were still recognisably
taller. Their descendants are the families of [[place:Qaanaaq]] today. The
technologies came to stay: every kayak and every leister in Avanersuaq in the
twentieth century descends from that crossing.

Mary-Rousselière also found names in north Baffin oral tradition of people said
to have gone to Greenland who cannot be fitted into Qitdlarssuaq's party at all,
and concluded that there was **a second, almost undocumented attempt in the
1890s**, possibly by the band of Tununirusirmiut who disappeared from Admiralty
Inlet. Nobody knows what became of them.
