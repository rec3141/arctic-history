On 19 August 1576 five of the Gabriel's crew rowed an Inuk pilot ashore in
Frobisher Bay, went on beyond the point against orders, and vanished.
[[person:Martin Frobisher]] waited a day and a night, fired a gun over the
tents, searched three days for hostages, came back and found the camp gone. In
1577 the English found English clothing in Inuit tents in
[[place:York Sound]] — a canvas doublet, a shirt, a girdle, three odd shoes —
left a letter in case any of the men were alive, and then killed five or six
Inuit at [[artifact:frobisher-010|Bloody Point]] and took a woman and her baby.
[[person:Kalicho]] told them the men were alive and that a letter should be
sent. It went on 7 August 1577. No answer ever came, and no Englishman ever
found out what happened.

## What Inuit knew

In the winter of 1860-61, at [[place:Field Bay]],
[[person:Charles Francis Hall]] — who had gone north to look for survivors of
[[topic:franklin-1845|the Franklin expedition]] and never got anywhere near
King William Island — was told by an old woman, [[person:Ookijoxy Ninoo]], the
grandmother of [[person:Ebierbing]] and mother of the hunter
[[person:Ugarng]], that a long time ago white men had come in ships to that
bay; that they had built a ship on an island; that they had sailed in it, been
stopped by ice and frozen to death.

On 11 May 1861 he went to her tupic on Cooper's Island, with
[[person:Tookoolito]] interpreting from the entrance, and had the whole account
again. The ships had come three years running: "first two, then two or three,
then many — very many vessels." White men had landed at Niountelik, near
Oopungnewing. Five Inuit had been killed by them, and on the Kingaite side of
the bay two Inuit women had been taken away and never came back. And five white
men had been taken by Inuit at the time the ships came; they wintered ashore,
she could not say how many winters; they lived among the Inuit; they built an
oomien — a large boat — put a mast in her and had sails; early in the season,
before much water appeared, they tried to leave, and some froze their hands in
the attempt; and finally they got into open water and away they went, "which
was the last seen or heard of them."

That ending is not the ending Hall had been given before, or was given
afterwards, and he printed both without smoothing them together. The version he
had heard "in previous conversations with her", and again from his guide
[[person:Koojesse]], and which he found "well known, as a traditionary fact",
was that the kodlunas "had built a ship there; had launched it, and started
away for their homes; but that, before they got out of the bay, hands and feet
were frozen, and finally the whole of them perished of cold." Koojesse put it
shortest: [[artifact:frobisher-025|"The men who built the ship, and started
with it, all died — died with the cold."]] Asked how he knew, he said all the
old Innuits said so.

[[person:Ebierbing]] had it from his father, who had it from those before him,
and gave Hall
[[artifact:frobisher-026|the clearest statement anyone has written down of how
the record is kept]]: "When our baby boy," said he, "gets old enough, we tell
him all about you . . . and so all Innuits will know what we now know."

[[person:Blind George]], blind by then, mapped
[[place:Countess of Warwick's Sound]] on a cabin table from memory, setting
Oopungnewing on his own body, Niountelik on the corner of a sea-chest, then
Twerpukjua, Kodlunarn and Tikkoon on the table, going back over the
arrangement until it satisfied him. He identified a lump of Frobisher's iron by
weight, by feeling its indentations and roughness, and by touching his lips to
it, and described the trenches without being asked a leading question.
[[person:Tweroong]] confirmed it and added that the old Innuits said the ship
was built from wood the white men had left on the island for an igloo — the
word used for their own snow houses and for the dwellings of others alike.

[[person:Petato]], questioned near Jones's Cape in December 1861, gave the
fullest version: [[artifact:frobisher-027|"A long, long, long, long time ago, a
great many ships came here. A northerly gale prevailed, and broke — or smashed
— some of the ships."]] White men landed, put things on the island in
quantities, made a house of stones with something white between the courses.
Inuit all around were kind to them and brought them seals and caribou. They
wintered once. When the ice broke up they went away in the ship. After a while
they came back — ice brought them back, they could not get out, it was very
cold, there was a great storm. Inuit built them igloos on Kodlunarn. And they
all died. Asked how many there were, she held up one hand, thought, held up the
other, and then said she could not remember whether it was few or many. Asked
who told her, she said: "My mother's grandmother's grandfather knew a good deal
about it."

[[person:Karping]], asked what sort of place the ship was built in,
[[artifact:frobisher-031|carved it in a block of snow]] and said its floor was
rotten wood — which is what Hall had already dug out of it, and what the
Frobisher papers in London say was buried there. Only three men built the ship,
he said; the others stood about "all same as captains"; the Inuit did not help
build her but did help get her down into the water.

In June 1862, near Farrington's Cape, Ookijoxy Ninoo gave Hall something no
European record contains: two personal names carried down from the 1570s.
[[person:Eloudjuarng]] was a pimmain, a great man — "All same as king", as
Tookoolito rendered it — who was particularly kind to the white men, and who,
when they were about to set out for home, had a song made wishing them a quick
passage and much joy and set his people, then very numerous, to sing it.
[[person:Mannu]] was one of those who saw the kodlunas. Hall cited the two
names as evidence of how exactly the traditions were handed down.

## How much of it is right

The account is not a transcript of 1576-78 and does not pretend to be. It is a
compressed and rearranged version, as any long oral account is: it folds three
voyages into "a great many ships", it attaches the wintering and the
ship-building to the same group of men, and it disagrees with itself about the
ending. In Ookijoxy Ninoo's telling of 11 May 1861 the five got clear into open
water and were never heard of again; in her earlier telling, in Koojesse's, and
in Petato's, they died of cold. Hall printed both, and both are still on the
record. The number five, which is the number that matters for the missing
Englishmen, comes from Ookijoxy Ninoo and from the shape of Petato's
hesitation, not from any consistent count.

But the load-bearing parts of it are demonstrably right, and were not
recoverable in 1861 from any other source. That white men came to that bay:
right. That they came three years running, first two ships, then a few, then
many: right, and it is the shape of Frobisher's three voyages exactly — Hall
checked it against Barrow's history on the spot. That they built and worked on
that particular island out of three hundred islands: right. That there is a
trench there of a shape used for building on the stocks, with rotten timber at
the bottom: right, and confirmed afterwards from manuscripts in the British
Museum that Hall had not seen. That five Inuit were killed and people carried
off: right. That some Englishmen were left behind: consistent with everything
the English recorded, and the only account of it that exists.

Hall himself did not deal with the source fairly at first — his own first
instinct, when Koojesse told him white men had built a ship on that island, was
to disbelieve it because there is no wood in the country. Two winters later he
was writing that Inuit oral history is a reliable record and telling the Royal
Geographical Society so. He reckoned nine generations had passed since
Frobisher, and wrote of the fate of the five men, "shrouded in mystery for 285
years", as all but determined by inquiry among Inuit. When his paper was read
at the RGS on 13 April 1863,
[[person:John Rae]] — who had himself been called a liar for reporting Inuit
testimony about Franklin — stood up and said
[[artifact:frobisher-028|that he had never in his life found an Esquimaux tell
a falsehood unless it was to gain some particular object]]. The president
reminded the meeting that the Inuit had told Hall the strait was a bay before
he went to look.

## What became of them

Nobody knows. The likeliest reading of the Inuit account is that the five were
taken in and lived among Inuit for a time — the clothing in the tents in 1577
is consistent with that, and so is Kalicho's insistence that they were alive —
and then tried to get away in something they had built. Whether they froze in
the attempt or got clear and were lost at sea, the tradition itself does not
settle. Whether the ship of the tradition is a boat the five knocked together,
or a memory of the pinnaces and the shipwrights of 1578, or both run together,
cannot be settled from what survives either.

What can be said is that Inuit remembered them, in detail, for two hundred and
eighty-five years, and that the English who left them there gave up looking
after three days.
