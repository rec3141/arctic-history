Four Inuit were taken to England by force between 1576 and 1578. None of them
lived more than a few weeks after landing. Three of them were drawn from life,
and the drawings survive; two of them are in a Bristol parish register; one is
in a set of company accounts. That is the whole record of four people, and it
exists because a joint-stock company went bankrupt and its papers were kept for
the lawsuits.

## 1576

[[person:The Inuk taken in 1576]] was seized in Frobisher Bay in late August,
after five Englishmen had disappeared and Frobisher wanted a hostage to trade
for them. He was lured alongside the Gabriel with a hand-bell, and
[[artifact:frobisher-021|lifted bodily out of the water, kayak and all]]. Best
writes that when he found himself in captivity, "for very choller and disdain,
he bit his tong in twayne within his mouth: notwithstanding, he died not
therof". He reached
London alive on 9 October and was, in [[person:Michael Lok]]'s words, "such a
wonder onto the whole city and to the rest of the realm . . . as seemed never
to have happened the like great matter to any man's knowledge."

He died within weeks, of a cold taken at sea. He was painted, and likenesses
were taken in wax and clay; none survive, though a drawing by the Dutch artist
Lucas de Heere does. Master William Crow, a warden of the Company of Barber
Surgeons, was paid five pounds to embalm him so that the body could be sent
back to his own country. Then the company thought better of it and he was
buried in the churchyard of St Olave's, Hart Street, in London. Nobody wrote
down his name.

## 1577

[[person:Kalicho]] was taken at Mount Warwick, after Frobisher
and his master tried to grab two men in the middle of a trade, slipped on the
snow, and were driven back to their boats with an arrow in Frobisher's buttock.
A servant of the Earl of Warwick's — Best names him Nicholas Conyer, a good
footman and, being a Cornishman, a good wrestler; Cheshire and his co-authors
spell him [[person:Nicholas Conger]] — ran one of the men down and showed him
"such a Cornishe tricke, that he made his sides ake against the grounde for a
moneth after". That throw very probably killed him, four months later, in
Bristol.

**The date is disputed.** Best, who was on the voyage, is exact: the landing
party went ashore "on Friday the ninteenth of July in the morning early", and
the seizure followed within the hour. Cheshire and his co-authors, working
backwards from the injuries in [[person:Edward Dodding]]'s post-mortem, place
the capture "at the very end of July or beginning of August". Nothing in the
record reconciles the two, and the difference matters, because it is the
interval between the throw and the death that carries the medical argument.
This page follows Best for the day and notes the disagreement wherever the date
is used.

[[person:Arnaq]] and her son [[person:Nutaaq]] were taken on 1 August at the
place the English named Bloody Point in [[place:York Sound]], after a party of
thirty or forty pursued an Inuit camp of sixteen or eighteen people and killed
five or six of them. Some of the wounded threw themselves off the rocks into
the sea rather than be taken, because they believed the English ate people.
Arnaq was hiding behind rocks with the baby on her back when
[[artifact:frobisher-022|an English arrow went through her hair and through the
child's arm]]. The surgeon put salves on it;
[[artifact:frobisher-023|she pulled them off and healed the arm herself]]. An
older woman taken with her was let go, because the English "thought she had bin
a divell or some witch".

Kalicho was made to interpret in the negotiations that followed. When his own
people came to treat for Arnaq's return he "fell so out into teares, that he
could not speake a worde in a greate space". Frobisher demanded his five
Englishmen first, and sent [[artifact:frobisher-035|a letter]] out to them on
7 August. No answer ever came.

## Bristol

The ships put in at Milford Haven on 23 September 1577 and the three Inuit came
on to Bristol. They were a sensation. The chronicler William Adams, who saw
them, thought it strange that their clothes had "no linen or woolle at all";
Bristol people were intrigued by the way Arnaq carried Nutaaq on the back of
her shoulder and "gave suck by casting her breastes over her shoulder", exactly
as [[artifact:frobisher-009|John White drew her]]. Early in October the mayor,
Thomas Colston, had all three to his house, and on 9 October — perhaps the same
day — [[artifact:frobisher-035|Kalicho was launched in his kayak on the Avon]]
"at the back of Bristol" at full tide before the mayor and a crowd. He paddled
up and down, killed a couple of ducks with his bird-dart — Adams: "he would hit
a ducke at a good distance of (sic) and not misse" — and afterwards landed at
the Marsh and "carried his bote through the Cittie uppon his backe".

He was dead within a month. Dr Edward Dodding, who attended him
throughout, wrote a long post-mortem report in Latin dated at Bristol on
8 November. It records broken ribs "badly broken, in sustaining a fall of some
force and impact", still gaping apart without having knit; a chest full of
fluid; and a lung that "in the end stuck to the ribs more firmly than anyone
would think". It also records that Dodding had
wanted to bleed him at the onset and had been overruled; that the man had
concealed a dread of England behind a cheerful face; and that at the end,
called an hour before he died, Dodding found him with his speech nearly gone
and no pulse, and then saw him rally, recognise the people around him, answer
questions relevantly, and sing the tune with which his companions had marked
his departure from the shore when he was taken.
His last words, given in English, were "God be with you."

[[artifact:frobisher-024|The parish register of St Stephen's]] reads, under the
heading "Burials in Anno 1577":
"Collichang a heathen man buried the 8th of November. Egnock a heathen woman
buried the 13th of November." Dodding had the burial done fast and without
ceremony and made Arnaq attend and stay until the body was completely covered
with earth, showing her human bones dug out of the churchyard, so that she
would understand the English did not eat their dead. Cheshire and his
co-authors think this may be the earliest entry in an English parish register
recording the burial of two non-Christians.

Arnaq was dead within the week. Dodding noted that she was troubled with boils
on the day Kalicho died and that they broke out densely on her skin the next,
but did not treat them as a fatal illness and never wrote down what killed her.
What he did write down, at length, was that she showed no distress at Kalicho's
death — from which he concluded that she had held him in contempt, though the
two were strangers who had been put in the same bed by their captors.

Nutaaq, still at the breast, was taken to London with a nurse to be shown to
the Queen, and lodged eight days at the Three Swans at the company's expense.
He fell ill. A surgeon, "Iohn gymblet surgione", was paid five shillings. The
boy died before he could be presented at court. Lok's accounts say he was
buried in the parish of St Olave's, Hart Street, beside the man of the year
before. The register there does not mention either of them. His age is usually
given as fourteen or fifteen months, but that figure — and the spelling
"Nutioc" — comes from the 1578 Geneva edition of Settle, a source that also
states, falsely, that Kalicho was presented to the Queen.

## Their names

Only one of the three names is a name. Kalicho — Calichough, Collichang,
Callicho, depending on who was writing — is a personal name. "Egnock" and
"Nutioc" are not: they are the Baffin Inuktitut words for a woman and a child,
which is what the English got when they pointed and asked. We record them here
as [[person:Arnaq]] and [[person:Nutaaq]] because those are the words that were
written down, but the entry is a mother and her son whose names nobody in
England thought to ask for.

The drawings [[person:John White]] made are the earliest European images of
Inuit from life and among the earliest of any Indigenous people of the
Americas. They are also, unavoidably, pictures of captives. Whether White
sailed on the 1577 voyage or drew them in Bristol is still argued;
[[artifact:frobisher-010|his picture of the fight at Bloody Point]] looks like
an eyewitness's, but it may copy one.

## Counted from the other end

Inuit remembered the taking. In May 1861, at Cooper's Island,
[[person:Ookijoxy Ninoo]] told [[person:Charles Francis Hall]], through
[[person:Tookoolito]], that when the kodlunas' ships came five Inuit were
killed by them, and that on the Kingaite side of the bay the white men took
away two Inuit women who never came back. No European source says two women
were taken — Best says the older one was let go — but the killing and the
carrying-off had been held in memory for two hundred and eighty-five years, and
the names the English never asked for were not what Inuit remembered either.
See [[page:frobisher-the-five-men]] and [[topic:inuit-oral-history]].
