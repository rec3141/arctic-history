Frobisher Bay is about 230 kilometres long, twenty to sixty wide, and closed at
its head, where Iqaluit now stands. For nearly three centuries European maps
showed it as an open strait, and for much of that time they showed it in the
wrong place — on the wrong side of the Atlantic, cutting through southern
Greenland. The error is a good case study in how a map can be wrong for a long
time, and in what it takes to correct one.

## Step one: the strait

[[person:Martin Frobisher]] sailed some sixty leagues into the inlet in August
1576 without reaching its head, and named it for himself following Magellan's
precedent. He took the land to the north for Asia and the land to the south for
America. Nothing he saw contradicted that: the tides run hard, the water stays
open ahead, and there are islands enough to hide a shoreline. The mistake was
reasonable. What made it durable was that the second and third voyages were
sent to dig ore, not to survey, and neither of them ever went to the head of
the bay.

[[artifact:frobisher-005|The world map in Best's True Discourse]], printed in
1578, shows the result: "Frobusshers Straightes" running clean through the top
of North America to a "Cathaia in asia" placed about where Alaska is, with the
"Straight of Anian" at the far end. It is the first printed map to carry
Frobisher's strait by name, and it is a sales document.
[[artifact:frobisher-011|Michael Lok's chart of 1582]] does much the same, with
"Meta Incognita" as an island and the strait open beside it.

## Step two: Frisland

The second error came from the first voyage's landfall.
Frobisher sighted the east coast of Greenland on 1 July 1576
and took it for Frisland — an island that appears on almost every
sixteenth-century North Atlantic chart, complete with harbours and place names,
and that does not exist. It comes from the Zeno map, published in Venice in
1558 and purporting to record a fourteenth-century voyage. Frobisher's charts
therefore placed Greenland and "Frisland" as separate lands, and fixed his own
new coasts by reference to a phantom.

Once cartographers had a strait defined against a non-existent island,
the strait had nowhere firm to sit. On
[[artifact:frobisher-012|Mercator's polar map of 1595]] the Frobisher material
is folded into a north polar geography of four islands round a magnetic rock,
with Frisland given an inset of its own. Later mapmakers, working from
Mercator, slid "Frobisher's Strait" progressively eastward until it was drawn
cutting into the coast of Greenland, where it stayed on published charts for
generations. As [[source:neatby1983frobisher|L. H. Neatby]] puts it, for a long
time it was supposed that Frobisher Bay was on the east shore of Greenland.
Ships looked for it there. It was not there. John Davis visited the real bay
without recognising what it was; see [[topic:davis-baffin-hudson]].

## Step three: doubts

Whalers working the Baffin coast knew perfectly well that these openings were
bays. At the Royal Geographical Society in 1863, Captain Sherard Osborn said he
had been told so a decade earlier by the whaling master William Penny, who had
fished to the head of most of them, and who explained why he had not published
it: "My knowledge is money." Sir George Back recalled at the same meeting that
"the celebrated geographer Dalrymple endeavoured to prove that it could not be
a strait, but a bay, though probably from a feeling of respect towards the
gallant Frobisher, his name remained affixed to it as a strait."

## Step four: asking the people who lived there

[[person:Charles Francis Hall]], wintering in [[place:Field Bay]] in 1860-62,
was told by the Inuit he lived among that the water was a bay. They also gave
him the Inuktitut name for it, which he wrote as Tin-nu-jok-ping-oo-se-ong. In
April and May 1862 he went to see, most of the fifty-one days by sledge and the
rest by boat, and found the head closed. The chart he published from that work
is [[artifact:frobisher-013|A Chart of Frobisher Bay and part of the West Coast
of Davis Strait]]: the bay drawn shut, the Countess of Warwick's Sound and
Kodlunarn in insets, and the islands of the sound carrying the names Inuit
gave him.

[[artifact:frobisher-017|His paper was read at the RGS on 13 April 1863]] with
the Kodlunarn relics on the table, under a title that says it plainly:
"Frobisher Strait proved to be a Bay". The discussion that followed is worth
reading for what it shows about whose evidence counted. Sherard Osborn would
allow Hall the credit, as a geographer, "of having made the fact known with
respect to the so-called Frobisher Strait, provided he produced latitudes and
longitudes". Sir George Back, who had been off Resolution Island with Franklin
in 1819, mostly told the meeting about the rock that lodged in his ship's
bottom there. [[person:John Rae]] spoke to
the reliability of Inuit testimony, having been vilified for relying on it
himself. And the president closed the point by observing that Hall had been
told the water was a bay by Inuit before he ever went to look, so "it was quite
clear, therefore, that the natives were not bad geographers, and were acquainted
with the coast-line of their country."

## A footnote: the strait that was real

There is one more twist. In July 1578 the fleet was driven south and west into
a wide opening and carried some sixty leagues into it, with land always to
starboard and open sea ahead. [[person:Christopher Hall]] said it was not
Frobisher's strait. Frobisher
[[artifact:frobisher-029|maintained that it was]], and admitted later that he
had done so because he hoped to be allowed to follow it through to the South
Sea. Best called it the mistaken straits. It was Hudson Strait, and it does
lead somewhere — into Hudson Bay, which [[person:Henry Hudson]] reached in 1610
and died in the year after. Frobisher spent three summers looking for a
passage, mistook a bay for one, and then sailed into a real one and was talked
out of it. See [[topic:davis-baffin-hudson]].
