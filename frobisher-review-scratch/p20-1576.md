[[person:Martin Frobisher]] had been trying to raise money for a north-west
passage voyage for fifteen years. He had been to Guinea twice before he was
twenty-one, had been held hostage for some months by an African chief, and had
been taken into custody three times on charges of piracy without once coming to
trial. What he could not do was promise investors "sure, certayne, and present
gaynes". In December 1574 the Privy Council leaned on the Muscovy Company,
which held the licence for north-west exploration and had no intention of using
it, to either go or let someone else go. [[person:Michael Lok]], a director of
the company, took up Frobisher's plan, raised £875 from eighteen of the many
people he asked, and undertook the remaining cost himself, more than £700.

They built one ship of about 20 tons, the Gabriel, bought another of 25, the
Michael, and a pinnace of 10. Dr John Dee spent the spring of 1576 aboard
teaching the men the new mathematical navigation. On 7 June the three vessels
left Ratcliffe on the Thames with thirty-five men in all, and the Queen waved
to them from a window as they passed Greenwich.

## Across

They were at Shetland on 26 June and off the east coast of Greenland by 1 July,
which Frobisher took for Frisland, an island that appears on almost every
sixteenth-century North Atlantic chart and does not exist. Ice and fog kept
them off. Then, on 13 July, a storm laid the Gabriel flat on her side and
filled her through the waist. Frobisher went out along the chain wales of the
capsized ship and got hold of the weather leech of the foresail; the foreyard
broke, the mizzen was cut away, and she came up. The pinnace went down with its
four men. The Michael's captain, badly frightened by the ice, parted company
and sailed home to report the expedition lost.

The Gabriel went on. Best writes that Frobisher held westward
[[artifact:frobisher-039|"knowing that the Sea at length must needes have an
endyng, and that some lande shoulde have a beginning that way"]].
On 29 July Frobisher sighted a high coast and named its
headland Queen Elizabeth's Foreland — probably Resolution Island. North of it
lay what Best called a "greate gutte, bay or passage", and on 11 August
Christopher Hall landed on an island at its mouth, which was named Hall's
Island after him. Frobisher sailed some sixty leagues in. The land to the north
he took for Asia and the land to the south for America, and the water between
them he named, after Magellan's example, [[place:Frobisher Bay|Frobisher's
Strait]].

## The five men

On 19 August, deep in the bay near what is now
[[place:Countess of Warwick's Sound]], seven Inuit boats came out to the ship.
Hostages were exchanged. Frobisher's master was taken ashore and shown inside
the tents; a man came aboard and was given trifles of haberdashery. Then, by
signs, a bargain was struck: one Inuk would pilot the ship through to the West
Sea, paddling ahead of her, and said it would take two days' rowing.

Five of the crew were sent to put him ashore at a rock in the captain's sight,
and were told to come straight back. Instead
[[artifact:frobisher-020|they rowed on beyond the point and out of view]].
Three landed. Two stayed in the boat, were called and signalled to from the
ship, and rowed in to their fellows. That was the last anyone aboard saw of
them. Frobisher lay at anchor all that day and night, sailed past the tents
next morning sounding a trumpet and firing a piece over the houses, searched
for three days for other people to take as hostages, and came back to find the
camp gone.

He then lured a kayaker alongside with hand-bells, dropped one into the water
to bring him closer, and
[[artifact:frobisher-021|lifted the man and his kayak bodily out of the sea]].
He made signs that if the five were brought back the man could go free; the
man would not appear to understand. On 25 August, with thirteen men and boys
still fit, Frobisher turned for England. He reached Harwich on 2 October and
London on 9 October, and was received with amazement by a city that had given
him up.

## What he brought back

Two things. [[person:The Inuk taken in 1576]], who with his kayak was, in Lok's
words, "such a wonder onto the whole city and to the rest of the realm . . . as
seemed never to have happened the like great matter to any man's knowledge",
and who was dead of a cold within weeks; and
[[artifact:frobisher-033|a piece of black stone]] that Frobisher gave Lok
because he had promised him the first thing he found in the new land.

That stone turned the enterprise inside out. The passage was set aside; the
next two voyages were sent for ore. Frobisher's commission for 1577 directed
him, Best writes, "only for the searching of the gold ore, and to deferre the
further discouverie of the passage untill another tyme". Neither the
strait nor the five men were ever properly looked for again.
