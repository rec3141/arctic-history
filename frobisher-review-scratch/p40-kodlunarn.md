[[place:Kodlunarn Island]] is about two hectares of low rock in
[[place:Countess of Warwick's Sound]], near the head of Frobisher Bay, 190
kilometres by water from Iqaluit. Frobisher's ships came into the sound on
29 July 1577, and he named it for Anne Dudley, Countess of Warwick, wife of his
chief patron. The Inuit named the island for what happened on it: Kodlunarn,
the qallunaat's place — the white men's island. That name has outlasted the
English one by four centuries, and Parks Canada carries Qallunaan, White Man's
Island, among the site's official names.

## What was done there

In 1577 the ore was got out in a little over three weeks: about two hundred
tons, cut by five miners with everyone else, gentlemen included, taking a turn
— in Best's words, "for example sake". In 1578 the whole fleet came back and
spent August on it. Men and stores were landed on 1 August; the pits and
trenches were deepened; assay furnaces were run; the damaged ships were mended;
and something like thirteen hundred tons of rock went into the holds: the
auditors later counted 1,296.

The hundred-person colony that was supposed to winter there never happened,
because the bark Dennis had gone down in the ice with part of the framed timber
house aboard. Instead Frobisher had
[[artifact:frobisher-034|a small house of lime and stone]] built near the mines
and left it standing deliberately, as an experiment: to see what an Arctic
winter would do to English masonry, against the day they came back. Bread,
peas and trinkets were left inside it to draw the Inuit into trade. Nobody came
back.

## What was still there in 1861

[[person:Charles Francis Hall]] landed on 11 August 1861, having been told the
story by [[person:Ookijoxy Ninoo]] three months before and having had the
island pointed out to him earlier by his guide [[person:Koojesse]], whose
statement that white men built a ship there Hall had dismissed because there is
no wood in the country. He listed what he saw:

> Coal; flint-stone; fragments of tile, glass, and pottery; an excavation which
> I have called an abandoned mine; a trench made by the shore on an inclined
> plane, such as is used in building a ship on the stocks; the ruins of three
> stone houses, one of which was twelve feet in diameter, with palpable
> evidence of its having been erected on a foundation of stone cemented
> together with lime and sand; and some chips of wood which I found on digging
> at the base of the ship's trench.

He mapped the island and keyed the features by letter. In
[[artifact:frobisher-037|his descriptive catalogue of the relics]] every
specimen is placed against that key: a a the ship's trench, b the ruins of the
stone house, c the reservoir, d what he marked, with his own question mark, as
the ruins of a blacksmith's shop, e "Best's Bulwark", g g the ship's
embankments, i the exact spot where the glass lay. The Kodlunarn inset on
[[artifact:frobisher-013|his published chart]] carries the same features under
capitals, A to I, with its own reference list: the ship's trench or mine, the
ruins of the house, the reservoir, the ruins of the shop, Best's Bulwark, where
iron ballast was found, the ship's banks, and where iron and glass were picked
up. Nearby, at Ekkeluzhun, a bay on the east side of the sound, he found a
deposit of English sea-coal with wood chips and a small piece of cord,
apparently of hair, embedded deeply in it; on Niountelik more coal; and on
Oopungnewing and at Tikkoon lumps of
[[artifact:frobisher-032|worked iron the Inuit called heavy stone]], carried
from island to island over the centuries and used to test people's strength by
lifting.

## The trench, and a detail nobody could have guessed

[[artifact:frobisher-031|The ship's trench]] is the feature the Inuit account
and the English archive meet over. Inuit called it the place where the kodlunas
built their ship. Hall dug rotten timber out of the bottom of it. In December
1861, near Jones's Cape, he asked the elder [[person:Karping]] — through
[[person:Tookoolito]], with instructions not to prompt him — what sort of place
it was. Karping took a snow-knife to a block of snow in the igloo and cut a
trench: wide and deep at the seaward edge, shallow and narrow at its
termination, then swept the knife round the block to show where on the island
it lay. Hall then put his finger at the bottom of the model and asked what the
ground was like there, expecting to be told stone, because he knew the floor of
the excavation was stone. Karping said soft. Pressed, he said: "Soft — very
soft — same as wood all falling in pieces; the tarrio — sea — came up into the
trench where the wood was." Asked more, he added that only three men built the
ship and the others stood about "all same as captains", and that Inuit did not
help build her but did help get her down into the water.

Hall wrote afterwards that manuscript records of Frobisher's expedition in the
British Museum, which he saw only after returning to England, show that
quantities of timber carried out to build a fort were buried at the bottom of
one of the mines. An elder who had never been to the island described the state
of the bottom of a trench, correctly, from a story handed down since the
1570s.

## Where the relics went

Hall carried away everything he could and divided it between the Smithsonian
Institution and the Royal Geographical Society.
[[artifact:frobisher-036|Both collections have since been lost]]. What survives
is [[artifact:frobisher-037|Hall's own descriptive catalogue]], printed as an
appendix to [[source:collinson1867three]], which records every specimen and
where on the island it came from: glazed and unglazed tile, one piece of it
carrying a human leg and foot in relief; sea-coal and coke and charcoal;
limestone; glass from a jar or bottle; oxide of iron; lime cement out of the
house foundation; wood chips from the base of the trench. Hall's phrase for the
patina on all of it is "the moss of ages".

Archaeologists have worked the island over more carefully since — the
Smithsonian's Meta Incognita work on the Frobisher sites ran through the 1980s
and 1990s — and the island has been
a National Historic Site of Canada since 27 October 1964. The designation
statement is
worth reading: it names Inuit oral tradition as part of the heritage value of
the place, alongside the archaeological evidence — the island is recognised
partly because Inuit "have preserved an account of this first European attempt
to exploit the natural resources of the Arctic". See
[[source:parkscanada1964kodlunarn]]. But the first survey of it was made by an
American who could not have found it without Inuit direction, working from a
story that had been kept without writing for nearly three hundred years. See
[[page:frobisher-the-five-men]], and [[topic:inuit-oral-history]].
