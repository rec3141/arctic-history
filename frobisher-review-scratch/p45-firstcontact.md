The Frobisher voyages produced the first sustained European contact with Inuit
in what is now Canada, and the first written descriptions and pictures of them.
The people the English met around the mouth and middle of Frobisher Bay were
Nugumiut, whose descendants live in Iqaluit and Kimmirut today. Almost
everything below comes from the English side: [[person:George Best]],
[[person:Dionyse Settle]] and [[person:Michael Lok]], writing to be read at
home. It should be read as such. The Inuit side of it survives too, but it was
not written down until 1861, and it is in
[[page:frobisher-the-five-men|what Inuit told Charles Francis Hall]].

## The first meetings

On 19 August 1576 seven boats came out to the Gabriel deep in the bay. Hostages
were exchanged; Frobisher's master was led by the hand into the tents and shown
how the people lived; a man came aboard, was given trifles, and refused the
ship's meat and drink and wine without any sign of liking. A bargain was struck
by signs for him to pilot the ship through to the West Sea. That transaction
ended with [[page:frobisher-1576-first-voyage|five Englishmen gone]] and, days
later, [[person:The Inuk taken in 1576|an Inuk hauled out of the water by
force]]. Lok, writing the voyage up in London, concluded from that one day that
these were people "of countenance and conversation proceding of a nature geven
to fyersnes and rapyne", which is the earliest recorded English judgement of
Inuit and rests on an encounter the English themselves broke.

On 19 July 1577, near [[place:Mount Warwick]], the English raised a column or
cross of stones on a hill and sounded a trumpet. Inuit appeared on the summit
with a flag, waving them back and calling out; Frobisher, who knew the call from
the year before, answered in kind, and Best writes that they "seemed greatly to
rejoyce, skipping, laughing, and dancing for joy". Two men from each side went
forward unarmed and traded pins and points for bow cases. The English recorded
the protocol carefully, because it was not theirs:

> they doe use to laye down of their marchandise uppon the ground, so much as
> they meane to parte withall, and so looking that the other partie, with
> whome they make trade, shoulde doe the like, they themselves doe departe, and
> then, if they doe like of their marte, they come againe, and take in exchange
> the others marchandise, otherwise, if they like not, they take their owne and
> departe.

Within the hour Frobisher and his master tried to seize two of the same men, in
order to keep one as an interpreter. That is the pattern of the whole
encounter: an exchange conducted on Inuit terms, broken by an English attempt
at abduction.

## What the English recorded

They noted that the sledges were pulled by dogs, and only learned how because
[[person:Kalicho]], already a prisoner, picked up one of the harnesses they had
found cached under stones in Bear Sound, caught one of the ship's dogs,
harnessed it, took a whip and drove it — a demonstration Best describes with
something close to admiration. They recorded kettles made of fish skins, knives
of bone, tents of sealskin, caches of fish under stones, a bird-dart thrown
with a hand-board, the kayak, and the amauti. In 1576 Lok's party found the
walls of twelve old houses at one place and thought them workshops rather than
dwellings, from the whale and seal bone lying about. In 1577 they opened a tomb
on an island in Bear Sound with a dead man's bones in it, and asked the captive
by signs whether his countrymen had killed the man and eaten the flesh from
them; he made signs to the contrary, and that the man had been killed by wolves
and wild beasts.

They also recorded, repeatedly, their conviction that Inuit ate human flesh,
and acted on it: at [[artifact:frobisher-010|Bloody Point]] on 1 August 1577
some of the wounded threw themselves off the rocks into the sea rather than be
taken, "least perhaps their enemies shoulde receive glory or praye of their
dead carcasses; for they supposed us be like to be canibales". Best, for his
part, concluded from an English doublet in an Inuit tent that "it is to be
thoughte that they had slaine and devoured oure men". Each side believed the
other ate people, and each side had just watched the other kill.
When [[person:Arnaq]] was made to watch Kalicho's burial in Bristol
that November, it was to disprove the same charge.

Best's ethnography in the third book of his True Discourse is worth reading for
what it says about the writer as much as the subject. In one paragraph the
people are "desperate in their fight, sullen of nature, and ravenous in their
manner of feedinge"; in the next they are "exceedingly friendly and kinde
harted, one to the other", they mourn their dead with "a mournefull song, and
Dirges", they are "verye chaste in [the] maner of their living", they delight in
music "above measure" and will keep time to any tune. Much of the detail is
not about Meta Incognita at all but about Kalicho and Arnaq in England: that
they "wondred muche at all our things, and were afraide of our horses, and
other beastes, out of measure", and that they "beganne to grow more civill,
familiar, pleasant, and docible amongst us in a verye shorte time". Best also
records that a man who had lost his liberty, meeting his own people again,
"fell so out into teares, that he could not speake a worde in a greate space".

## The violence, plainly

Over three summers the English attempted at least four abductions and carried
out one attack. Four Inuit were taken to England and all four died there. At
York Sound on 1 August 1577 an English party of thirty or forty pursued a camp
of sixteen or eighteen people, drove them onto a point, and killed five or six
of them; one Englishman was badly wounded in the belly with an arrow. The dead
are not named in any English source, because nobody asked.
The English word for the place, Bloody Point, is the only
memorial they got.

Inuit kept their own count. In May 1861 [[person:Ookijoxy Ninoo]] told
[[person:Charles Francis Hall]], through [[person:Tookoolito]], that when the
kodlunas' ships came, five Inuit were killed by them, and that on the Kingaite
side the white men took away two Inuit women who never came back. That is the
1577 fight and the taking of Arnaq, counted from the other end, and kept for
two hundred and eighty-five years.

## And after

The English left in 1578 and did not come back. What they left behind entered
Inuit life as material: coal that was still being picked up and burned in 1861,
iron blooms carried from island to island and used to test strength, tile and
brick and glass. It also entered Inuit memory, in the account of
[[page:frobisher-the-five-men|the kodlunas who built a ship]],
which was still accurate enough in 1861 to lead
Hall to a site no European had seen for 283 years.

Regular European presence in these waters resumed only with the whalers of the
nineteenth century; for that, see [[topic:whaling]] and
[[topic:hudson-strait-south-baffin]]. For the Nugumiut and their neighbours in
the twentieth century, see [[topic:relocations-qtc]].
