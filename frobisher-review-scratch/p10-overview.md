Between 1576 and 1578 England sent three fleets to the same corner of southeast
Qikiqtaaluk (Baffin Island). The first was a passage voyage of three small
vessels and thirty-five men. The third was fifteen ships, a hundred intended
colonists and a prefabricated house — the largest force England had yet sent
anywhere in the New World. The second and third fleets saw nothing the first
had not already seen, and the whole enterprise ended in bankruptcy, because the
ore they crossed the Atlantic to dig was worthless rock.

What the voyages did leave behind was a set of firsts, most of them unhappy:
the first sustained European contact with Inuit in what is now Canada, the
first European mine and building site in the Arctic, the first European
pictures of Arctic people and of an Arctic event, five Englishmen who
disappeared into Frobisher Bay in 1576, and four Inuit who were carried to
England and died there within weeks. And they left a question that stayed open
for 285 years, until Inuit answered it.

## The three voyages

[[artifact:frobisher-001|The Gabriel's voyage of 1576]] left the Thames on
7 June with two small ships and a pinnace. The pinnace foundered off Greenland
with four men; the Michael turned back and reported the rest lost. The Gabriel
alone reached a wide inlet in Baffin Island on 11 August, and [[person:Martin
Frobisher]] — following Magellan's precedent of naming a strait for its finder
— named it for himself, with Asia supposed to lie on the north side and America
on the south. On 19 August five of his men rowed out of sight and were never
seen again; he seized an Inuk kayaker to hold against them, failed to get them
back, and sailed home with the man and a lump of black stone.

The man died in London within weeks. The stone was assayed four times. Three
assayers said marcasite. The fourth, an Italian named Giovanni Battista
Agnello, produced a few grains of gold, and when [[person:Michael Lok]] said he
marvelled at it, since three others had found no such thing in the rock,
Agnello answered him — Lok wrote the words down in Italian — *Bisogna sapere
adulare la natura*: one must know how to flatter nature. On that answer the
Cathay Company was chartered, the Queen put in a thousand pounds and lent a
ship, and [[artifact:frobisher-002|the second voyage of 1577]] and
[[artifact:frobisher-003|the third of 1578]] became mining expeditions.
[[page:frobisher-the-gold-that-was-not]] follows that money.

## The people taken

Four Inuit were carried to England: an unnamed man in 1576, and in 1577
[[person:Kalicho]], [[person:Arnaq]] and her infant son [[person:Nutaaq]].
All four were taken by force, two of them out of a fight in which five or six
Inuit were killed, and all four were dead within weeks of arriving. Only
Kalicho's is a personal name: "Egnock" and "Nutioc", what the English wrote
down for the other two, are the Inuktitut words for a woman and a child.
The watercolours [[person:John White]] made of them —
[[artifact:frobisher-008|Kalicho with his bow]] and
[[artifact:frobisher-009|Arnaq with Nutaaq in her hood]] — are the earliest
European portraits of Inuit drawn from life. What happened to them is set out
in [[page:frobisher-the-captives]].

## Kodlunarn

The mining was done in a sound near the head of the bay that Frobisher named
for the Countess of Warwick, on an island the Inuit came to call
[[place:Kodlunarn Island]] — the white men's island. Between 1577 and 1578 the
English cut a great trench and several pits into it, ran assay furnaces, built
at least three stone structures, and shipped away something over fifteen hundred
tons of rock. [[page:frobisher-kodlunarn]] describes the site.

## What Inuit remembered

In 1861 and 1862 [[person:Charles Francis Hall]], living in
[[place:Field Bay]] with [[person:Tookoolito]] and [[person:Ebierbing]], was
told by [[person:Ookijoxy Ninoo]] that white men had once come to that bay in
ships, three years running; that they had built a ship on an island and sailed
away in it; that five Inuit had been killed by them and two Inuit women carried
off. He was told substantially the same thing, separately, by
[[person:Petato]], [[person:Karping]], [[person:Blind George]],
[[person:Tweroong]] and [[person:Koojesse]]. Petato traced her knowledge to her
mother's grandmother's grandfather. Ookijoxy Ninoo gave Hall two personal names
carried down from the 1570s — [[person:Eloudjuarng]], who was kind to the white
men and had a song made to send them home, and [[person:Mannu]], who saw them —
that appear in no European document. Karping, who had never seen the trench on
Kodlunarn, carved its exact shape in a block of snow and said its floor was
rotten wood, which is what Hall had already dug out of it and what the
Frobisher manuscripts in the British Museum describe.
[[page:frobisher-the-five-men]] takes that story seriously, and
[[topic:inuit-oral-history]] and [[topic:kane-hall-polaris]] tell the Hall side
of it at length.

## The strait that was a bay

Frobisher's "strait" is a bay about 230 kilometres long, closed at
its head. It was drawn on European maps as an open passage, and then — through
a chain of copying errors beginning with the Zeno map's phantom island of
Frisland — slid across the Atlantic onto the south coast of Greenland, where it
stayed for the best part of two centuries. Hall settled it in 1862 by going and
looking, having been told by Inuit before he set out that it was a bay.
[[page:frobisher-strait-on-the-map]] follows the error.

## Why it matters here

The ship works in waters these voyages named. Iqaluit stands at the head of the
bay Frobisher took for a passage to China; Kodlunarn lies about 190
kilometres down the bay from it and has been a National Historic Site since
1964. For Inuit in
Qikiqtaaluk the Frobisher story is not a curiosity from a European archive: it
is a family account that was kept accurately, without writing, for two hundred
and eighty-five years, and that an American in 1861 was able to check against the ground
and against documents in London. It is one of the strongest demonstrations
anywhere of what oral history can carry. It is also a plain account of what
first contact looked like from the receiving end: an armed party, a fight, and
people taken away.
