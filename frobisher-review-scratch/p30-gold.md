The passage voyage lasted one season. What followed was a mining boom, and it
ran on a single assay that was probably a fraud.

## Flattering nature

[[person:Martin Frobisher]] had promised [[person:Michael Lok]] the first thing
he found in the new land, and handed over
[[artifact:frobisher-033|a piece of black stone]], heavy, and about the colour
of sea coal. Lok took it to three assayers in London. All three said marcasite:
iron pyrites, worthless. He took it to a fourth, an Italian named Giovanni
Battista Agnello, who returned three tiny showings of gold from three pieces of
the same rock. Lok said he marvelled much at his doings, since he had given
pieces to three others who could find no such thing in it. Agnello's answer,
which Lok wrote down in the Italian in which it was given, was
*Bisogna sapere adulare la natura* — one must know how to flatter nature.

Whether Agnello salted the crucible deliberately or used a lead flux that
carried gold of its own, nobody now can say. The effect was immediate. In March
1577 the backers of the first voyage were incorporated by royal charter as the
Cathay Company, with Lok as governor and Frobisher styled high admiral.
Queen Elizabeth subscribed £1,000 and lent the Ayde, a ship of 200 tons. She
also gave the new land its name: Meta Incognita, the unknown limit.

## 1577: two hundred tons

[[artifact:frobisher-002|The second voyage]] left Harwich on 31 May 1577 with
three ships and about 120 men, including five miners and a refiner. The ore
body on Hall's Island, where the black stone had come from, turned out to be a
few hundredweight. Frobisher crossed to the north land, found what looked like
a very rich mine and got nearly twenty tons out before ice drove into the
anchorage and forced him to leave it on the beach — hence Bear Sound. On 29
July he reached [[place:Countess of Warwick's Sound]], and everything after that
was digging. About two hundred tons were loaded in a little over three weeks;
the gentlemen volunteers worked with picks alongside the miners, in Best's
words, "for example sake". The ships sailed on 23 August.

The ore was locked in Bristol castle under four separate locks, one key each to
the mayor, to Sir Richard Barkley, to Frobisher and to Lok — a precaution that
tells you what everyone believed it was worth. Through the winter of 1577-78
the refiners quarrelled and the investors quarrelled with them. Dr Burcot
produced figures; Jonas Schutz wrote about the furnaces at Dartford in November
1577; the results kept not arriving. Rather than stop, the company sent a
bigger fleet.

## 1578: fifteen ships

[[artifact:frobisher-003|The third voyage]] was on another scale entirely:
fifteen ships out of Harwich on 31 May 1578, a hundred people intended to
winter, and a framed timber house — a "strong forte or house of timber,
artificially framed, and cunningly devised by a notable learned man here at
home". The orders were to bring back two thousand tons.

It went wrong at once. The ice at the mouth of the bay was impassable; the bark
Dennis, a hundred tons, was holed and sank with part of the house aboard; and
the fleet was driven south and west into the water Best calls
[[artifact:frobisher-029|the mistaken straits]] and carried about sixty leagues
into it, with open sea ahead. [[person:Christopher Hall]] said flatly that it
was not Frobisher's strait. Frobisher insisted for a time that it was, and
admitted afterwards that he had said so hoping to be allowed to follow it
through to the South Sea. It was Hudson Strait, entered thirty-two years before
[[person:Henry Hudson]] sailed into it and thirty-three before he died in it;
see [[topic:davis-baffin-hudson]].

The fleet worked back to [[place:Kodlunarn Island]] and spent August mining.
Without the timber there could be no colony, so
[[artifact:frobisher-034|a small house of lime and stone]] was built near the
mines instead and left standing on purpose, with bread and peas inside it, to
see what a winter would do to it and to draw the Inuit into trade. Thirteen
vessels sailed at the end of August with close on thirteen hundred tons of rock and got
home about 1 October.

## Nothing

Smelting went on at Dartford until at least 1583, and then both refiners and
investors admitted failure. No gold was ever produced, because there was none:
the rock is amphibolite, a hornblende rock, and the gold Agnello found was not
in it. When auditors closed
[[artifact:frobisher-038|the company's books]] in 1580-81 the arithmetic was
plain. The three voyages had raised £20,345, £4,000 of it the Queen's.
The third voyage alone had landed 1,296 tons of ore at Dartford. Everything
ever refined out of the whole enterprise came to two ingots of gold
weighing nine pennyweights and eight grains, two ingots of silver weighing
seven ounces eighteen pennyweights, and 210 ounces of silver mixed with gold
out of sixteen tons worked at Dartford. Thirteen hundred tons still lay at
Dartford, carried in the books at £13 6s 8d a ton; the ore had cost the company
£16 a ton, and the like of it could afterwards be had for £6.
[[source:collinson1867three|Collinson]], printing the audit in 1867, could
find no record of what finally became of it.

The Cathay Company collapsed. Lok, who had put in
his own money and then borrowed more, was ruined, and spent years afterwards in
accounts and memorials against Frobisher — the state papers of the company
include one headed "the abuses of Captain Furbisher against the Companye".
Frobisher went back
to sea, sailed with Drake to the West Indies, commanded against the Armada, was
knighted, and in 1594 was shot in the side storming a Spanish-held fort at
Crozon in Brittany; he died of the wound at Plymouth on 22 November.

One accidental good came of the failure. Because the company ended in
litigation, its papers were kept — Lok's accounts down to the five shillings
paid to a surgeon for a dying Inuit child, the assay correspondence, the
victualling lists. Had the venture paid, most of that would have been thrown
away, and we would know far less than we do. [[source:collinson1867three]]
prints the bulk of it.
