Everything on this ship's map that has a European name got it in the last four
hundred years. The human history of the same coasts is about eleven times
longer, and almost all of it was lived by people who left no writing. Two
bodies of evidence tell it. One is archaeology: houses, hearths, middens,
harpoon heads, radiocarbon dates and, since about 2010, ancient DNA. The other
is Inuit oral tradition, which has its own account of who was here before, and
which was written down — by [[person:Franz Boas]] in Cumberland Sound in the
1880s and by [[person:Knud Rasmussen]] at [[place:Iglulik]] in the 1920s —
long before the archaeologists had a chronology to check it against.

The two accounts are not the same kind of thing and this topic does not try to
merge them. It sets them beside each other.

## The archaeological sequence, in five lines

- **circa 2500 BC.** People carrying the Arctic Small Tool tradition cross east
  out of Alaska into a landscape no human being had ever entered. In the High
  Arctic and northeast Greenland they are called Independence I; in Foxe Basin
  and along Hudson Strait, Pre-Dorset. Archaeologists now call all of them
  Paleo-Inuit. See [[page:the-first-arrivals]].
- **circa 800 BC to AD 1300.** Out of the Pre-Dorset, in place and without any
  new arrival from outside, grows the **Dorset** culture: soapstone lamps and
  pots, ground slate, harpoon heads with no drilled line hole, extraordinary
  carving — and no bow, no rotary drill, no dog team, no large skin boat. See
  [[page:dorset-life]] and [[page:the-dorset-carvings]].
- **circa AD 1200–1300.** The **Thule**, whale hunters out of north Alaska,
  move east across the top of the continent with the umiaq, the dog sledge, the
  sinew-backed bow and the float harpoon, and reach Greenland within a century
  or so. They are the ancestors of today's Inuit. See
  [[page:the-thule-migration]].
- **circa AD 1300.** The Dorset disappear. Genome-wide data published in 2014
  found no sign that they were absorbed: [[artifact:thule-dorset-001|the
  incoming population]] and the resident one barely mixed at all.
- **circa AD 1400–1850.** Heavier sea ice closes the leads the bowhead needs.
  The big whaling villages empty, the winter house gives way to the snow house
  and the sealing camp, and the pattern Europeans meet from the sixteenth
  century on takes shape. See [[page:after-the-whales]].

Every date above is coarse on purpose. Radiocarbon on Arctic sea-mammal bone
and ivory carries a marine reservoir offset that is hard to correct, and much
of the older dating was run on walrus tusk. Where this topic gives a century,
the source gave a century.

## The Tuniit

Inuit tradition, independently of all this, holds that the country was
occupied before by a people called the **Tuniit** (Tunit; Tornit in Boas's
Cumberland Sound spelling). They were taller and far stronger than Inuit. They
lived in stone houses. They hunted walrus and whale from the shore. They were
shy, easily offended, and when trouble came they left rather than fight.

[[artifact:thule-dorset-002|Boas's Cumberland Sound informants]] told him the
Tornit made "neither kayaks nor bows", and that a sealing Tuniq carried
[[artifact:thule-dorset-003|a small lamp under his coat]] to melt snow while he
waited at the breathing hole. [[person:Ivaluardjuk]] told Rasmussen that the
Tunit had many villages at [[place:Uglit]] near Iglulik, built of stone and
whale bone, and that when they were finally driven out
[[artifact:thule-dorset-009|one man harpooned the rocks]] in grief and made the
stones fly about like bits of ice.

Most archaeologists take the Tuniit of tradition to be the Dorset. The fit is
close in several particulars that no one had any reason to invent — the stone
houses, the sea-mammal hunting, the missing bow — and it is not close in others.
The tradition is discussed on its own terms in [[page:the-tuniit]]. Related
material sits in [[topic:inuit-oral-history]].

## Why it matters on this ship

The archipelago the Amundsen works in has been continuously inhabited for four
and a half thousand years, and the settlements are still there to be seen:
[[place:Qaummaarviit]] twelve kilometres west of [[place:Iqaluit]],
[[place:Brooman Point]] on Bathurst Island, [[place:Nunguvik]] on Navy Board
Inlet, the beaches at [[place:Alarnerk]] near Iglulik. Sites the ship can
anchor near are collected in [[page:winter-houses-you-can-anchor-near]].

Two of them matter for the science as well as the history. The raised beaches
at Alarnerk are a record of isostatic rebound that happens also to be a
chronological ladder; the houses on the 22 m terrace are older than the houses
on the 8 m terrace because the land rose between them. And the Thule expansion
and the Little Ice Age contraction are both, in part, sea-ice stories: a
whaling economy that worked when the leads opened and stopped working when they
did not. There are four and a half thousand years of Arctic climate response
in this record, told through where people could live.

The Norse arrive on this coast from the other side, out of Greenland, during
the Late Dorset and early Thule centuries; that meeting is written up in
[[topic:norse-helluland]] and is not retold here.
