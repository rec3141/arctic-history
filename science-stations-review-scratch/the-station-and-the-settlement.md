There are two settlements at Resolute Bay and there always have been. One is the
airfield and the station on the flat ground by the strip; the other is
[[place:Qausuittuq]], the Inuit hamlet. In 1953 they were
[[artifact:science-stations-028|eight kilometres apart]], and the distance was
policy.

This page is about the relation between them. The relocation itself — why Ottawa
did it, what was promised, what it cost — belongs to
[[topic:relocations-qtc]] and to
[[page:relocations-the-1953-and-1955-high-arctic-relocations]], which set it out
in the Qikiqtani Truth Commission's own terms and with the relocatees' own words.
What follows is the part that is inseparable from the science station, told from
the Commission's community history of Resolute Bay
([[source:qtc2013resolutebay]]) and the Royal Commission's 1994 report
([[source:rcap1994high]]).

## The station came first, and by accident

Nobody lived on [[place:Cornwallis Island]] when
[[artifact:science-stations-011|the weather station]] was put ashore on 30 August
1947. The station was not even meant to be there: the main Joint Arctic Weather
Station was intended for Winter Harbour on [[place:Melville Island]], and heavy
ice turned the ships back four times before Charles Hubbard settled for Resolute
Bay, whose harbour was usually clear enough in summer to unload over the beach
(see [[page:the-five-stations]]). An RCAF base followed in 1949, three kilometres
from the airstrip. Within a few years Resolute was the hinge of the entire High
Arctic: everything that reached [[place:CFS Alert]], [[place:Eureka]],
[[place:Isachsen]] and [[place:Mould Bay]] went through it, and so did every
scientist.

## The eight kilometres

The families came north on the *C. D. Howe*, were transferred at sea to the
*d'Iberville*, and were put ashore at Resolute Bay on 7 September 1953: three
families from [[place:Inukjuak]] in Nunavik and one from
[[place:Mittimatalik]] (Pond Inlet), with their qimmiit and their belongings.
Constable Ross Gibson came with them to open an RCMP detachment and a store. No
site had been surveyed for the people. The Commission records that the only
stipulation was that they could not establish themselves too close to the RCAF
property, and that the detachment, the store and the settlement therefore went up
eight kilometres from the base and weather station.

What that meant in practice, in the first winter, was this. The families arrived
in September, when the walrus had already gone; there were restrictions on
hunting the few caribou; the fish in the local lakes and streams carried
parasites and were inedible. They had no boat but two one-man kayaks, no storage,
no store yet, and tents in poor condition; Gibson reported more than a thousand
dollars' worth of missing goods and had to ask the RCAF for accommodation, storage,
transport and medical supplies. [[person:John Amagoalik]] put the first problem
plainly to the Commission in 2008: the men had to learn a new country from
nothing — where to hunt, whether there were fish, whether there were caribou.

Their first building, that October, was made of lumber taken from the weather
station dump. It dried pelts, and served as workshop, church and classroom. By
the spring of 1954 people were building houses out of scrap and surplus wood
gathered at the RCAF base and the dump; by 1958 the RCMP reported everyone living
in well-built scrap-lumber houses, with more than a hundred qimmiit, four motor
boats and a few canoes. Fourteen houses stood by 1960, eighteen by 1964. The
richest country in the Arctic for building material was a garbage tip, and it was
eight kilometres away.

## A closed base

The base was not a neighbour; it was a jurisdiction. In the Commission's words,
[[artifact:science-stations-026|the RCAF base and the weather station were out of
bounds to Inuit unless they worked there]] — and it adds that the traffic was
barred in both directions, since Qallunaat were not allowed to visit the
settlement and Qausuitturmiut women and children were effectively prohibited from
leaving it. Inuit were warned they would have to demonstrate good working
behaviour before they could be hired.

Work, when it came, came through the base. Men were taken on each spring to load
and unload the airlift. By 1960 four held permanent jobs — one teacher and three
at the RCAF Survival School — picked, the Commission records, because they were
"the top hunters in Resolute" and were declared "deserving [of] employment and
the benefits that accompany wage employment". By 1963 there
were nine permanent full-time positions: two janitors, three assistant mechanics,
three at the Survival School, and a caretaker for the government buildings. Wages
brought a steady income and access to the base canteen; as early as 1961 the RCAF
had restricted alcohol sales there. In April 1964
the base and airstrip passed from the RCAF to the Department of Transport, and
the number of permanent jobs fell to five. In 1966 there was one full-time hunter
left in the community, though the wage earners still hunted.

The base also carried the diseases. In 1957 the *C. D. Howe*, working the Eastern
Arctic Patrol with measles aboard, was forced to stop at Resolute; infected
passengers were put ashore in spite of efforts to keep the two populations apart,
and the measles went straight through the Qausuitturmiut. Regular medical
attention arrived only in 1961, as a Saturday-morning round of the Inuit houses
by an RCMP constable and an RCAF medic. A nursing station came in 1968, a Hudson's
Bay Company store not until December 1971, an Anglican church in 1965. The
qimmiit that had come north on the ship fell from more than a hundred in 1958 to
eighty in 1964, seven of them to rabies and the rest, the Commission says, to the
enforcement of the ordinance that required dogs to be tied at all times — the
pattern set out in
[[page:relocations-the-killing-of-the-qimmiit]].

## Moved again

In 1974 the settlement held about two hundred people in thirty-two households.
Its water came from tapping icebergs, gathering snow, or hauling from Char Lake
in summer. Drifting snow, the need to get the houses out from under the airport
approach line, and a wish to bring all the residential areas together led to a
new townsite; the architect Ralph Erskine — English, working from Sweden — was
hired to design it, and envisaged a community of seven hundred, Inuit and
Qallunaat together, ringed by a wall of three-storey apartment blocks. In the end
one of his buildings was put up. In the summer of 1975 the community was moved
about three
kilometres, from the western shore of the bay to the eastern. Television arrived
the same year. [[person:Saroomie Manik]] told the Commission in 2008 what the
move had cost:
[[artifact:science-stations-027|"We were close to the shore and we are too far
from the shore right now."]] Resolute Bay became a hamlet in 1987 and part of
Nunavut in 1999.

## Why this belongs in a page about science

Because the science and the settlement are the same arrival. The airstrip that
lets a geologist reach Ellef Ringnes, the runway lights that bring in a
[[artifact:science-stations-014|Polar Continental Shelf Project]] Twin Otter, the
hangar the ship's helicopter refuels at — these exist because a weather station
was put where the ice happened to allow it in 1947, and the community exists
because that station was there and Ottawa wanted people on the ground near it.
Every field party that stages through Resolute is using an infrastructure whose
first local employer would not let the neighbours through the gate. The
Commission's word for what is owed is *saimaqatigiingniq*, and its findings are in
[[page:relocations-saimaqatigiingniq]].
