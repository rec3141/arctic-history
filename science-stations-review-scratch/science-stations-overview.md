If you are reading this on the ship, you are the current chapter of it.

The Arctic was a place people passed through for three hundred years. Ships came
in July and left in September, or were caught and spent a winter they had not
chosen. What they brought back was a narrative and a chart. Then, in the space
of seventy years, the Arctic became a place where science *lives*: where the same
instrument reads the same air at the same hour, every day, for decades, and the
value of the reading is in the fact that nobody stopped. This topic is about how
that happened, and it ends with the vessel under your feet.

## Weyprecht's idea

The turn begins with an Austrian naval officer who had been to the pack and
concluded that expeditions were the wrong instrument. Karl Weyprecht argued that
a dash for a latitude teaches nothing, and that what the Arctic needs is a ring
of fixed stations, agreed in advance, all reading the same instruments to the
same schedule for a full year. The International Polar Conferences of Hamburg,
Berne and St. Petersburg turned the argument into a plan, and in 1882-83 the
first International Polar Year put fourteen stations round the pole.

Two of them were in North America. The German station was at
[[place:Kingua Fiord]] at the head of Cumberland Sound. The northernmost of all
was [[place:Fort Conger]] on Ellesmere Island, held by Adolphus Greely's party
for the United States — a station that is famous for what happened afterwards
rather than for what it did. What it did was
[[artifact:science-stations-018|five hundred and twenty-six observations a day]],
for two years. Greely's own account puts the science first:
[[artifact:science-stations-017|"one of the circumpolar international stations"]].
The retreat, the starvation at Cape Sabine and the nineteen dead belong to
[[topic:nares-greely]], which tells that story properly. What belongs here is
that the observations came home, and were used.

The [[artifact:science-stations-019|second International Polar Year of 1932-33]]
was larger and much less remembered: forty nations, and in Canada a chain of
stations from Cape Hope's Advance on Hudson Strait through
[[place:Igluligaarjuk]] (Chesterfield Inlet) to [[place:Kugluktuk]] (Coppermine)
on Coronation Gulf. It was planned for Canada by a Meteorological Service man
named [[person:Andrew Thomson]]. Remember the name: fifteen years later he was
running the weather service when the permanent stations went in.

## Five stations, 1947 to 1950

The Second World War left behind long-range aircraft, prefabricated huts,
icebreakers and a reason. In 1946 the Americans proposed a chain of weather
stations across the Canadian Arctic; Ottawa took eight months to think about it
and approved a Joint Arctic Programme on 28 January 1947. Five stations went in
over four years, Canadian and American staff in equal numbers, everything flown
from the American base at [[place:Pituffik]] (Thule) in Greenland.

- [[artifact:science-stations-004|Eureka]], 7 April 1947, on Slidre Fiord.
- [[artifact:science-stations-011|Resolute]], 30 August 1947 — chosen only
  because [[artifact:science-stations-001|four attempts to reach Winter Harbour]]
  failed.
- [[artifact:science-stations-005|Isachsen]], 2 April 1948, on Ellef Ringnes
  Island.
- [[artifact:science-stations-006|Mould Bay]], 11 April 1948, on Prince Patrick
  Island.
- [[artifact:science-stations-007|Alert]], 1950, at the top of Ellesmere, built
  from [[artifact:science-stations-002|a cache landed in 1948]] and paid for with
  [[artifact:science-stations-008|nine lives]].

[[page:the-five-stations]] tells how each of them was put in.
[[page:building-alert]] tells the Alert story on its own, because it is the one
with the deaths in it.

The stations were built for weather, and weather is what they gave: balloons
twice a day, surface observations, and after a while ozone, seismographs, tide
gauges, background carbon dioxide. They were also, unmistakably, a way of
occupying ground. A post office opened at Eureka in August 1947 with a Canadian
postmaster sworn in by a Mountie
([[artifact:science-stations-010|the northernmost post office in Canada]]),
because a postmark is an argument about sovereignty that costs almost nothing.
When [[artifact:science-stations-016|the American flag came down at Resolute in
1972]] and the stations became Canadian, the historians who went through the
files found that on the ground it changed nearly nothing.

## What it cost, and who paid it

The station at Resolute is why there is a community at
[[place:Qausuittuq]] (Resolute Bay). In September 1953 three families from
[[place:Inukjuak]] and one from [[place:Mittimatalik]] (Pond Inlet) were landed
on the south shore of [[place:Cornwallis Island]] with their qimmiit and their
belongings. No site had been chosen for them. The only stipulation was that they
were not to settle too close to RCAF property, so the RCMP detachment, the store
and the settlement went up eight kilometres from the base, and the first
community building was made of lumber taken from the weather station dump. The
families had been told they could go home after a year or two if they did not
like it. They were not taken home. That is not a footnote to the science; it is
part of the same arrival, and [[topic:relocations-qtc]] and
[[page:the-station-and-the-settlement]] set it out in the Qikiqtani Truth
Commission's own terms.

## The apparatus grows

- [[artifact:science-stations-014|The Polar Continental Shelf Project]], 1958:
  created to survey the continental shelf and hold jurisdiction over it, and now
  the outfit that flies almost every scientific field party in the High Arctic.
- [[artifact:science-stations-012|The drifting ice islands]]: slabs of the
  [[place:Ward Hunt Ice Shelf]] found on radar from 1946 and lived on from 1952,
  drifting a mile a day round the Beaufort Gyre. See [[page:the-ice-islands]].
- [[artifact:science-stations-013|PEARL]], the laboratory on the ridge above
  Eureka, which exists because Eureka does. See [[page:the-eureka-record]].
- CFS Alert, a signals station from 1 September 1958 and the northernmost
  permanently inhabited place on Earth, with the longest clean-air record in the
  Canadian Arctic running beside the antennas.

## The line to this ship

Ninety per cent of what the fifth International Polar Year of 2007-08 measured in
the Canadian Arctic was measured from ships and camps that this chain of stations
made possible. By then Canada had a research icebreaker again. In 2001 there was
no Coast Guard ship available for Arctic science at all; fifty-four researchers
from twelve universities, led by [[person:Louis Fortier]], put a proposal to the
Canada Foundation for Innovation, and the laid-up icebreaker *Sir John Franklin*
was rebuilt at [[place:Les Mechins]] and recommissioned on 26 August 2003 as the
**CCGS Amundsen**. She has been north nearly every year since, working for
ArcticNet, and she has run up more than 2,200 days of research at sea.
[[page:from-the-stations-to-the-ship]] follows that line.

So: Weyprecht's fixed station, Greely's 526 observations a day, the balloon that
went up at Eureka this morning for the twenty-nine-thousandth time, and the
instrument you are watching on this ship. It is one argument, continued. The
Arctic is not a place you visit and describe; it is a place you stay in and
measure. The scientists reading this tab are the current chapter of that, and
the record you are adding to began at Fort Conger in 1882.
