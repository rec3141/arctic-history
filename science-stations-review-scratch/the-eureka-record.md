At 11:02 on Easter Sunday, 7 April 1947, a C-47 from
[[place:Pituffik]] (Thule) landed on the sea ice of Slidre Fiord. Four men got
out. [[person:Justin Courtney]], the Canadian officer-in-charge, tasted the ice
before he let anyone unload — fresh ice is multi-year ice, and multi-year ice will
carry a loaded aircraft. They looked at the ground, built a snow cairn, and were
back aboard in forty minutes ([[artifact:science-stations-004|Eureka]]).

That is the whole of the founding. The interesting part is what has happened
since, which is nothing: [[place:Eureka]] has been occupied and observed every
single day for seventy-nine years. There is no other record like it in the
Canadian High Arctic, and its value lies entirely in the fact that nobody stopped.

## What is in the record

The digital archive Environment and Climate Change Canada holds for Eureka begins
on 1 May 1947 and has not had a gap worth the name since; 28,986 daily rows under
the original station number alone ([[artifact:science-stations-035|the record
itself]]). Upper-air soundings began on 18 September 1947 and have gone up twice a
day ever since. The station also carries, or has carried, ozone, solar radiation,
seismic and tide observations.

The place it describes:

- **Mean annual temperature −18.8 °C** (1981–2010 normals). January averages
  −36.5 °C; July averages +6.1 °C.
- **79 mm of precipitation a year.** Eureka is a polar desert, and this is the
  driest long climate record in Canada. Sixty centimetres of snowfall a year is
  most of it.
- **Highest temperature ever measured: 21.4 °C, on 27 June 2020.** It beat
  20.9 °C, set on 14 July 2009. Note the month: the new record is a June record.
- **Lowest: −55.3 °C, reached twice** — on 15 February 1979 and again on
  18 February 1987.

Annual means worked out from the daily archive itself — sixty-six complete years
between 1948 and 2015 — put the coldest decade of the record in the 1970s
(−20.5 °C) and the warmest in the 2000s (−18.0 °C). The warmest single year is
2010 at −15.9 °C; the coldest is 1972 at −21.8 °C, nearly six degrees apart. Those
decadal figures come from one station's raw daily means, with no correction for
instrument or site changes, and the station identifier changed in 2016, so treat
them as an indication rather than a trend estimate;
[[artifact:science-stations-035|the record itself]] carries the figures and how
they were reached.

## The people who kept it

For twenty-seven years the crews were entirely male: eight or so people, half
Canadian and half American until 1972, wintering because the money was good and
there was nothing to spend it on. In 1974 the Atmospheric Environment Service
posted two meteorological technicians, [[person:Heather Blain]] and
[[person:Cheryl Leyten]], to Eureka —
[[artifact:science-stations-015|the first women at any High Arctic weather
station]]. Blain's memory of the practical difficulty is that there was no women's
washroom; the officer-in-charge gave up his own. By the 1980s women worked at most
of the stations.

Eureka also got [[artifact:science-stations-010|Canada's northernmost post
office]] on 10 August 1947, an RCMP constable swearing Courtney in as postmaster
while the *Edisto* lay offshore. The postmark was an argument about sovereignty
that cost almost nothing — the same argument the station itself was making, and
one reason it has never been allowed to close.

## What grew on top of it

[[artifact:science-stations-036|Look at the station from the airstrip]] and almost
everything you see is life support: fuel, kitchen, power, beds, a runway. That is
the asset. A continuously staffed, continuously supplied site at 80°N is
enormously expensive to create and nearly free to add to, which is why the
instruments have accumulated.

Six hundred metres above the station, on a ridge, Environment Canada built the
Arctic Stratospheric Ozone Observatory in the early 1990s to watch the ozone layer
over the pole. Budget cuts mothballed it in 2001. In 2005 a university consortium,
CANDAC, reopened it as [[artifact:science-stations-013|PEARL]], the Polar
Environment Atmospheric Research Laboratory, which measures ozone, aerosols, water
vapour and greenhouse gases straight through the polar night — the one thing
satellites cannot do well, because there is no sunlight to work with.
[[place:PEARL]] exists because Eureka exists. Its runway, its fuel and its cooks
are the weather station's, and have been since 1947.

The same logic runs at the other end of the archipelago:
[[place:CFS Alert]] keeps the longest background greenhouse-gas record in the
Canadian Arctic on a ridge behind a signals station that was built for reasons
having nothing to do with carbon dioxide ([[page:building-alert]]).

## Why this matters to a ship

Two things.

First, a ship gives you a section — a line of casts and profiles through the water
on the days you were there. Eureka gives you a column of air measured the same way
at the same hour for seventy-nine years. Neither is a substitute for the other, and
almost every question worth asking in this region needs both: the ship's
measurement is only interpretable against a baseline somebody else kept. When the
Amundsen works in Eureka Sound or Nansen Sound, PEARL and the station are
upstairs, sounding the same air.

Second, the record is a warning about endings. Isachsen closed in 1978 and Mould
Bay in 1997 ([[page:the-five-stations]]), and the argument in both cases was that
one High Arctic station costs what twenty southern ones cost. What was lost was
not a building. PEARL nearly went the same way in 2001, and its funding has been
precarious more than once since. The unglamorous fact underneath this whole topic
is that the value of a long record is superlinear in its length and the cost is
linear, and that budget cycles do not know this.
