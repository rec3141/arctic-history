Alert sits at 82°30'N on the northeast shoulder of Ellesmere Island, about 800 km
from the North Pole and closer to Moscow than to Ottawa. It is the northernmost
permanently inhabited place on Earth. It was put there by twelve men in one
summer, and the record of how is unusually good, because one of them —
[[person:J. Peter Johnson, Jr.]], the airstrip mechanic — became a geographer and
wrote it up forty years later for the journal *Arctic*
([[source:johnson1990establishment]]).

## A cache on the beach, 1948

The fifth Joint Arctic Weather Station was planned before it had a name or a
site. In the summer of 1948 the icebreakers USS *Edisto* and USCGC *Eastwind*
worked north from [[place:Pituffik]] (Thule) to look for one:
[[artifact:science-stations-002|30 July to 5 August 1948]]. The *Edisto* lost a
propeller blade in loose pack on the way and anchored in
[[place:Dumbbell Bay]], where Charles Hubbard and W.I. Griffith of the Canadian
Meteorological Division walked the ground and chose the site. Their reasons were
practical: a ship could reach it when the ice allowed; two lakes inland would
give fresh water and, if the bay ice proved too rough, a winter airstrip; part of
Cape Belknap was flat enough for a gravel strip; and the United States Range lay
far enough off not to spoil the weather observations.

The unloading went badly. A wind shift threatened to pin the icebreakers against
the shore and they had to withdraw to Robeson Channel, leaving seventeen men —
Hubbard among them — on the beach for thirty-six hours until a helicopter could
lift them off. The rest of the cache went ashore from the *Eastwind* on 5 and 6
August: a T-9 bulldozer, an engine heater, twelve drums of fuel, cargo sleds and
a Jamesway hut. Then the ships left and the beach was empty for twenty months.

The station still had no name. Its crates were stencilled "Cape Columbia", a cape
almost 100 km to the west. On 17 November 1948 F.W. Reichelderfer of the US
Weather Bureau wrote to [[person:Andrew Thomson]] in Ottawa proposing two: *Alert*,
after the ship [[person:George Strong Nares]] brought to this coast in
1875-76, and *Belknap*, after her naturalist-surgeon. The Board of Geographical
Names chose **Alert** on 6 January 1949. The spring 1949 airlift was then
cancelled for want of cargo aircraft, and the whole thing slipped a year.

## Easter Sunday, 1950

On 9 April 1950 — Easter Sunday, as at [[place:Eureka]] three years before — a
USAF ski-wheel C-47 put an advance party down on the ice of Dumbbell Bay at
−46 °C. Snow was cleared for a runway; 1000 m of it were ready by 14 April, and
on 18 April the first C-54 landed, under Capt. M. Buls. From then until 2 May the
aircraft came almost continuously: 308 tons delivered, sleep and food snatched
between arrivals, cargo sleds cut free of the tractors on the hill so they would
not run forward into the tracks.

Twelve men stayed: J. Leo Lafranchise (officer-in-charge, Canadian), C.J. Clifton
(executive officer, American), and ten more — radio, rawinsonde, cook, carpenter,
mechanics, airstrip. Equal numbers of each nationality was the rule of the whole
programme. Small things marked the calendar. A gull came out of the blowing snow
on 18 April; a snowy owl the next day; the first wolf on 1 May, spotted by an
aircrew running their pre-flight checks at the end of the runway. And on 30 April
[[artifact:science-stations-025|the first mail left Alert]], hand-cancelled in a
Jamesway hut by Lafranchise, who had been sworn in at Resolute as postmaster of
the world's most northerly post office.

## What was already there

The crew were not the first on that coast, and they kept running into the
evidence. On the evening the airlift ended, six of them drove a weasel to
[[place:Cape Sheridan]] and climbed to the cairns on the headland; at about
midnight they pulled an old powdered-egg tin from behind a rock and found
[[artifact:science-stations-023|a message Peary had left in 1907]]. Below it, the
cross on Ross Marvin's memorial cairn had fallen, and they stood it up again.

On 24 June, walking east along the sea ice, Clifton and Johnson found a boulder
near Ravine Bay with [[artifact:science-stations-024|HMS ALERT 1876]] chiselled
into it — an inscription in no expedition record and in nothing written since.
Markham's sledge party had camped near that spot in September and October 1875 - see [[page:nares-1875-the-alert-at-floeberg-beach]].
More cairns turned up on Mount Pullen and above the Dumbbell Lakes, origins
unknown. Johnson's judgement on the boulder is the honest one: in its long
unobserved anonymity it serves as a fitting marker for what those men did.

## The crash

Work stalled through July on broken machinery — a cracked block in the T-9, a
scraper whose hydraulics, towing gear and tyres failed in turn, and finally a
spark plug snapped off with no spares within 2,000 km. Spare parts were found at
Thule and loaded aboard an RCAF Lancaster there for ice reconnaissance. Shortly
after noon on 31 July 1950 the Lancaster came in over the station to drop them;
the parachute fouled the elevator and the aircraft dived into the ground about
450 m west of the camp. There were no survivors.
[[artifact:science-stations-008|Nine died]], among them Hubbard, who had chosen
the site, and the Canadian ice observer Dr D.W. Kirk. The crash and the 1991
Boxtop Hercules crash are told together in
[[page:alerts-two-air-disasters]]; they are not retold here.

What followed belongs to this page. The station kept its three-a-day weather
observations without a break. The spark plugs were recovered from the charred
parachute container and put into the tractor. An RCAF Canso sent to investigate
on 4 August was beached, wrecked its take-off on the 7th, and had to be repaired
by the *Eastwind*'s shops. Hubbard's family asked that he be buried where he had
died; five of the crew dug the grave near the north end of the airstrip, looking
out over Cape Belknap and the Arctic Ocean. When it became clear the Canadian
bodies could not be flown out in time, the ground beside it was opened and a
[[artifact:science-stations-009|joint military funeral]] was held late on 7 August
1950 in the world's northernmost cemetery.

## Finished

The *Eastwind* landed a second bulldozer, a big cable scraper and a towed grader,
and the work went quickly after that: 900 m of gravel runway on 19 August, 1350 m
serviceable on 24 August — hard, dry and usable year-round, which meant Alert
could never again be isolated. The first aircraft landed on it on 8 September.
Rawinsonde ascents began on 11 September 1950 and the station was operational.
On 18 October the first replacements flew in and five of the twelve went home.

Alert's later life was not meteorological. An RCAF communications team arrived in
1956; [[place:CFS Alert]] was established as a signals station on 1 September 1958
and the population of specialists passed two hundred. But the weather record never
stopped, and neither did what grew out of it: the greenhouse-gas and clean-air
observatory on the ridge behind the station now holds the longest background
atmospheric record in the Canadian Arctic. Johnson's own last word is that little
of the place's original character survives — "the graves and airstrip are,
however, a reminder of its origins."
