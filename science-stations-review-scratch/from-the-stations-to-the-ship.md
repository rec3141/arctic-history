This page is the join between the rest of this topic and the deck you are standing
on. The claim is simple and, I think, defensible: almost nothing this ship does in
the Queen Elizabeth Islands would be possible if five weather stations had not gone
in between 1947 and 1950, and the reasons are logistical before they are
intellectual.

## The stations built the infrastructure

[[artifact:science-stations-011|Resolute]] was the accident that mattered. Meant
for Winter Harbour, it ended up on [[place:Cornwallis Island]] because the ice said
so, and became the main station, then an RCAF base in 1949, then the airfield
through which [[place:CFS Alert]], [[place:Eureka]], [[place:Isachsen]] and
[[place:Mould Bay]] were supplied. Runway, fuel farm, hangar, weather forecast,
beds. Every one of those is a fixed cost that nobody would pay for a single field
season, and all of them were paid for by 1950 for other reasons entirely.
[[place:Qausuittuq]], the community beside it, is part of the same arrival, and
what that cost is set out in [[page:the-station-and-the-settlement]].

## PCSP: the stations become a service

In 1958 Ottawa created the
[[artifact:science-stations-014|Polar Continental Shelf Project]] with
[[person:Fred Roots]] as its first coordinator. Its mandate was double and openly
so: survey the Arctic continental shelf, and make sure Canada had jurisdiction over
what was on it. Canada's resource rights had just been extended to the shelf; the
weather stations and then the DEW Line had shown that the Arctic could be worked
year-round; the Project turned that into a standing logistics service.

The pressures were specific and all from 1957-58: Sputnik, an American request for
Canadian Arctic gravity data for its space programme, the *Nautilus* crossing under
the pole, and the UN Law of the Sea conference giving nations the resources of
their continental shelves to a depth of 200 m. Canada was claiming a shelf about
which, in NRCan's own words, it knew virtually nothing.

The first field party went out on 9 March 1959 — to the weather station at
Isachsen, because that is where the runway and the fuel were.
[[artifact:science-stations-037|What they did there]] is a good picture of the
whole transition: twenty-two men from March to October, a ski Beaver and two
Otters making over 400 landings on unprepared ice and ground, tellurometer
baselines run out over the Arctic Ocean, Decca positioning stations, hydrographic
soundings, tide records under ice, eighty gravity readings on land and sea ice,
sea-floor cores off Cape Isachsen, and a glaciology programme on the Meighen Island
ice cap. A first base at Resolute followed in 1960, a western base at Mould Bay in
1964 (moved to Tuktoyaktuk in 1968), and in 1986 the Project gave up doing its own
science altogether and became purely a logistics agency. That is what it still is:
PCSP flies, feeds, fuels and communicates for field parties across the High Arctic
out of Resolute. If you have ever been put on an island by Twin Otter north of 74°,
it was probably PCSP that put you there.

The founding date is a small unresolved disagreement. PCSP's own chronology says
**26 May 1958**, and that is what this topic records; the date usually quoted
elsewhere is a cabinet directive of 5 April 1958, which comes from a 1981 paper by
[[person:George Hobson]] in the *Journal of Canadian Petroleum Technology* that
this project has not been able to obtain — 403 direct, 404 through the library
proxy, 403 at OnePetro, and no University of Manitoba holding.

## The ice islands were the first drifting laboratories

Before there were research icebreakers there were
[[page:the-ice-islands|ice islands]]. T-3, a slab of the Ellesmere shelf, carried
huts, a power plant and a wheeled-aircraft runway round the Beaufort Gyre from 1952
and was worked on and off until 1978, and PCSP ran its own,
[[artifact:science-stations-038|Hobson's Choice]], from 1984 until it broke up in
1993. It was the same idea as a ship — put the laboratory on the ice and let the
ocean move it — with one crucial difference: you could not steer, and you could not
leave.

## The ship

By 2001 Canada had no vessel available for Arctic science at all. Fifty-four
researchers from twelve universities, led by [[person:Louis Fortier]] (1953–2020),
put the case for one; in March 2001 the Canada Foundation for Innovation agreed to
fund it; and at Les Méchins on the St Lawrence the decommissioned hull of the CCGS
*Sir John Franklin* was rebuilt into a research icebreaker. She was recommissioned
on [[artifact:science-stations-020|26 August 2003]] as the **CCGS Amundsen**, and
sailed that autumn on a year-long inaugural expedition with
[[person:Martin Fortier]] as chief scientist — the Canadian Arctic Shelf Exchange
Study. [[source:brunette2021ccgs]] is the short institutional account.

ArcticNet, formed at the end of 2003, has been her main user ever since. The
Amundsen has since accumulated more than 2,200 research days at sea and about
250,000 nautical miles — eleven and a half times round the Earth — carrying 116
teams in 20 major programmes from 26 Canadian universities and 98 international
organisations. She has also carried the Nunavik Inuit Health Survey (2004, 2007–08,
2017), an oil-and-gas partnership in the Beaufort that ended in 2015, and the
Schools on Board programme. She went through a major life-extension refit from May
2020 to July 2022.

## The Circumpolar Flaw Lead, and the fourth Polar Year

The clearest demonstration that the ship had inherited the stations' logic came in
2007–08. For [[artifact:science-stations-021|the fourth International Polar Year]]
the Amundsen did not visit the Beaufort Sea; she overwintered in it, held in the
flaw lead from the autumn of 2007 to the summer of 2008, measuring the same system
continuously through the dark. That is Weyprecht's argument of 1875 — a fixed
station reading the same instruments to the same schedule for a full year — carried
out from a hull instead of a hut. Ninety per cent of what the Polar Year measured in
the Canadian Arctic went through camps, stations and ships that this chain made
possible.

## What the two halves give each other

A ship and a station are complementary instruments and neither is sufficient.

- The ship gives **space**: a section through the water column along a track,
  sampling that cannot be done from land at all — CTD casts, cores, moorings,
  net tows, an ROV, a helicopter that reaches an ice camp.
- The station gives **time**: [[artifact:science-stations-035|seventy-nine years]]
  of the same column of air over [[place:Eureka]], the longest background
  greenhouse-gas record in the Canadian Arctic behind
  [[place:CFS Alert]], a tide gauge that has not moved.

A cast in Eureka Sound this week means one thing on its own and something much
larger read against what [[place:PEARL]] has been recording overhead since 2005 and
the weather station under it since 1947. When the
[[artifact:science-stations-034|Ayles Ice Shelf broke up in 2005]] the sequence was
exactly this: a Canadian Ice Service analyst saw it on RADARSAT, researchers pinned
it to the hour from satellite and seismic records, and it was first put to a
scientific audience at the ArcticNet Annual Science Meeting — the ship's own
network — in December 2006.

## The line, in one sentence

Weyprecht argued in the 1870s that expeditions teach less than fixed stations;
Greely's men at [[place:Fort Conger]] took
[[artifact:science-stations-018|526 observations a day]] for two years to prove it;
five stations went into these islands in 1947–50 for weather and for sovereignty;
PCSP turned them into a service; the ice islands and then a rebuilt icebreaker put
the station on something that moves; and the instrument you are reading right now
is the current entry in that record. The [[topic:science-stations|whole topic]]
is about people who did not stop measuring, and the only interesting question it
asks of anyone aboard is whether the thing you are recording today will still be
comparable in seventy-nine years.
