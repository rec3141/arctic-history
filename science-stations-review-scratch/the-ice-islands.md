On 14 August 1946 a USAF photo-reconnaissance crew out of Ladd Field, flying the
routine weather track north from Alaska, put a radar return on the screen that
should not have been there: a mass of floating ice more than 200 square miles in
area, far thicker than the pack around it, less than 300 miles north of Point
Barrow. They called it Target X, then **T-1**, and classified it secret. Four more
years passed before anyone said so in public.

Everything below comes from one paper,
[[artifact:science-stations-033|*Arctic Ice Islands*]] (1952), which is four
papers under one cover: [[person:Lawrence S. Koenig]] of the USAF on how the
three were found, [[person:Keith R. Greenaway]] of the RCAF on the photographs,
[[person:Moira Dunbar]] of the Defence Research Board on the ones nobody had
noticed, and [[person:Geoffrey Hattersley-Smith]] on where they came from
([[source:koenig1952arctic]]). All its measurements are in nautical miles, and so
are the ones here.

## Three islands in the ocean

T-1 measured about 15 by 18 miles, shaped, Koenig thought, like an arrowhead or a
chicken's heart; nobody landed on it, and much of what is known of it is
[[artifact:science-stations-043|a radar sweep]] and
[[artifact:science-stations-046|a run past it at 250 feet]]. It was followed for
thirty-eight months and then lost for twenty-two. Between
14 August 1946 and 6 October 1949 it travelled about 1,400 miles — an average of
1.2 miles a day — clockwise with the Beaufort Gyre and then east across the top
of the ocean:
[[artifact:science-stations-029|the drift of T-1, 1946-1952]].

Encouraged, the 58th Strategic Reconnaissance Squadron began searching on radar
along the Ptarmigan track in late May 1950. On 21 July they found
**T-2** at 86°40'N, 167°00'E — 300 square miles, 17 by 18 miles, visible against
the pack from 75 miles away by its colour alone
([[artifact:science-stations-030|its drift]];
[[artifact:science-stations-045|the photograph taken the next day, from 45 miles]]).
Another American aircraft had in fact photographed T-2 on 19 July and not reported
it. Eight days after the sighting that counted, on 29 July,
**T-3** came up on radar at about 75°24'N, 173°00'W: kidney-shaped, only 4.5 by
9 miles, but with the ridges and troughs more strongly marked than on either of
the others ([[artifact:science-stations-003|its drift, 1947-1952]];
[[artifact:science-stations-047|T-3 under snow from 250 feet in May 1951]]). The
paper prints [[artifact:science-stations-044|all three drifts on one polar chart]],
plotted at three-monthly intervals.

The dating of T-3 then went backwards. Greenaway remembered photographing a floe
with an odd rippled surface on a joint USAF/RCAF flight on 27 April 1947, thirty
miles north of Isachsen Peninsula on [[place:Ellef Ringnes Island]] — three days
after the same crew had photographed identical ice fast to the north coast of
Ellesmere. At the time they wrote it off as a broken piece of land-fast ice.
Compared with the T-photographs in 1951 it was unmistakably T-3, and a USAF
survey frame of 9 July 1948 off [[place:Prince Patrick Island]] was T-3 as well.
Three years were added to the island's known life at a stroke.

These are drifts, not tracks. The published chart smooths the real path because
celestial fixes are often impossible at those latitudes in daylight or twilight,
and between some fixes there are years of nothing. The waypoints in the three
track artifacts say which positions are printed in the paper and which are
inferred; treat the lines between them as sketches.

## Where they come from

Hattersley-Smith's argument, in Part IV, is the one that has held. The islands are
too big, too thick and too strong to be sea ice, and too regular to be glacier
calvings; they are slabs of shelf ice, and the shelf is the fringe along the north
coast of Ellesmere, continuous between Cape Columbia and Yelverton Bay
([[artifact:science-stations-042|the shelf photographed whole on 16 July 1950]]).
The paper makes the case with two photographs side by side:
[[artifact:science-stations-048|an ice island lying off Cape Columbia in July
1947]], and
[[artifact:science-stations-049|the shelf off Cape Nares in June 1950]] with a
jagged bay in its edge into which two sides of that island fit like a jigsaw
piece. The gap had since filled with smooth fast ice.

It was proved from the air on two flights. On the night of 19 March 1951 a B-29
flew Ptarmigan Special Roger to Ward Hunt Island and brought back photographs of
ridged ice, exactly patterned like T-3, filling the fiords and running out from
the coast. On 1 August 1951 they flew it again in summer light, when the melt made
the shelf unmistakable: it stood out five to ten miles from shore, and at 73°W
there was a stretch at least fifty miles long with
[[place:Ward Hunt Ice Shelf|Ward Hunt Island]] in the middle. On the approach they
found T-1, missing for twenty-two months, fifty miles north of the island, and
examined it from 250 feet; a few hours later they picked up T-2 at 87°07'N,
25°00'W. One flight refound both lost islands and settled where they were born.

The older record backs it, and it is Dunbar in Part III who reads it. Pelham
Aldrich, sledging west from the *Alert* for [[topic:nares-greely|Nares]] in
1875-76, described what he called "ice waves" or "ice rollers" along that coast,
low ridges 30 to 40 feet high; Peary, on the same coast in 1906, wrote of "the
long, prairie-like swells of its surface". Set beside the 1950 photographs, both
descriptions of Disraeli Bay match so exactly — Aldrich's rollers running
southeast to northwest, Peary's ridges especially marked off the western end of
Ward Hunt Island — that Dunbar concluded that ice had very likely not moved in
the seventy-six years between. Yelverton Bay was the opposite case: both men give
the impression it was full of rollers, and by 1950 there was
[[artifact:science-stations-054|almost no shelf ice left in it]], so the shelf in
Peary's time reached further west than it does now. Going through the older
narratives for earlier sightings, Dunbar found the likeliest descriptions in three
areas: the Arctic Ocean north of Axel Heiberg (Cook), the Beaufort Sea
(Storkerson, and Takpuk's island), and from Robeson Channel to Smith Sound
(Greely) — and noted that in that last area, where the most candidates turn up in
the books, the photographs showed no ice islands at all. Her explanation is that
in the middle of the nineteenth century the shelf may have extended further east
as well, and that source is now more or less exhausted. She is careful about the
old accounts:
[[artifact:science-stations-053|worn old floes in Smith Sound]] "might easily give
rise to descriptions by ground travellers that sound like ice islands", and the
flat-topped bergs that
[[artifact:science-stations-052|the glaciers of Dobbin Bay]] still produce could
account for others.

## The twenty-eight nobody had noticed

The part of this paper most useful to a ship in the Queen Elizabeth Islands is
Dunbar's. Working through RCAF trimetrogon air photographs of the Archipelago, she
identified [[artifact:science-stations-031|twenty-eight ice islands]] in the
channels themselves, from a quarter of a mile to seven or eight miles across, plus
a mass of fragments, and
[[artifact:science-stations-050|put them all on one map]]: three in the Arctic
Ocean off Alert Point, three in the Nansen Sound–[[place:Eureka Sound]] reach,
five in Peary Channel, one in the Prince Gustaf Adolf Sea, three in Maclean
Strait, one in Byam Martin Channel, four in Hecla and
Griper Bay, one in Peel Sound off Bellot Strait, three in James Ross Strait, and
four in [[place:Erebus Bay]] on the west coast of King William Island — the
farthest south any has been found, and
[[artifact:science-stations-051|photographed there in August 1949]] with its
slivers around it. One in Maclean Strait moved sixty miles south
between August 1950 and July 1951 with a fragment still stuck to its southeast
corner, the whole pack having moved as one piece. No ice island was found in the
channels east of Ellesmere, and none in M'Clure Strait, though there the air
coverage was poor.

They are not a curiosity. Ice islands drift with the current, not the wind, so
they part company with the pack around them and often have open water on one or
more sides when there is none anywhere else nearby — a useful thing to know from a
bridge. And they get in the way. In late August 1950 the USCGC *Eastwind*, going up
Eureka Sound to resupply [[place:Eureka]], could not force a passage through
[[artifact:science-stations-032|an ice island lying off Stor Island]]. Eric Fry of
the Canadian Topographical Survey photographed it over the rail: until men landed
on T-3 those were the only photographs of an ice island ever taken from the
surface, and what surprised everyone was how flat it looked from sea level when
from the air it was among the roughest.

## Living on one

On 19 March 1952, when T-3 was two degrees from the North Pole,
[[person:Joseph O. Fletcher]] landed on it — the first men ever to stand on an ice
island. Preliminary reports of that first summer's seismic soundings gave an
average ice thickness of 200 feet, a maximum of about 250, and a maximum height
above sea level of 40 feet; the rolls stood about 10 feet above the troughs and
lay 300 to 1,000 yards apart, and in places the "coast" fell away in cliffs up to
30 feet high, flanked by rafted pack ice standing on edge.
T-3 became Fletcher's Ice Island, a drifting station with huts, a power plant and a
runway for wheeled aircraft, worked on and off until 1978. It is the reason the
ice islands are remembered at all, and the paper barely covers it: Koenig and his
co-authors were writing while the party was still out there, quoting preliminary
reports and promising a proper account later.

Canada eventually did the same thing. In 1984 the
[[artifact:science-stations-014|Polar Continental Shelf Project]] put seven wooden
buildings and a runway on a slab of the [[place:Ward Hunt Ice Shelf]] about 8 by
3 km, of freshwater ice some 3,000 years old, and called it
[[artifact:science-stations-038|Hobson's Choice]] after
[[person:George Hobson]], the Project's director. The runway melted out every
summer and was rebuilt every spring. Seismic work was run from it in 1985 to read
the structure of the continental shelf — which was the Project's founding purpose.
In 1993 the island drifted south into the Archipelago, broke up, and the station
was decommissioned.

## Why it still matters here

The shelf Hattersley-Smith described is mostly gone. On 13 August 2005, in less
than an hour between 1730 and 1830 UTC, almost the whole
[[place:Ayles Ice Shelf]] — about 87 km² — broke away and became
[[artifact:science-stations-034|an ice island of 66 km²]], 15 km by 5 km and 30 to
40 m thick, of ice thought to be as much as 4,500 years old. It was the largest
break-up in the Canadian Arctic in thirty years, and it happened after the
landfast fringe that normally presses on that coast even in summer was replaced by
an open lead. Laurie Weir of the Canadian Ice Service saw it in routine RADARSAT
monitoring; Luke Copland and Derek Mueller worked the satellite and seismic records
back to the hour; and the first scientific audience to hear it was the ArcticNet
Annual Science Meeting in December 2006 — this ship's own network
([[page:from-the-stations-to-the-ship]]).

Between 1946 and 2005 the question changed. In 1952 the puzzle was where these
things came from. Now the source is understood and the interesting number is the
rate. Anything the ship sees drifting in these channels with a rolled surface and
open water along its flank is a piece of the same shelf, a few thousand years old,
on its way out.
