In the last week of August 1850, eight vessels lay together in the water between [[place:Beechey Island]] and [[place:Cape Spencer]] — a place they named [[place:Union Bay]], "to perpetuate the day on which so many of the ships met." It was the largest gathering of ships that had ever been in the Canadian Arctic, and it happened because within four days they had all found the same thing: the first and, as it turned out, almost the only physical trace of the [[topic:franklin-1845|Franklin expedition]].

## Who was there

- **[[person:Horatio Thomas Austin|Austin's]] naval squadron**: HMS Resolute (Austin), HMS Assistance ([[person:Erasmus Ommanney]]) and the screw steam tenders Pioneer ([[person:Sherard Osborn]]) and Intrepid ([[person:Bertie Cator]]) — four ships, 180 men, and among the lieutenants [[person:Francis Leopold McClintock]]. [[artifact:franklin-search-020|Their track]] ran through forty days beset in [[place:Melville Bay]].
- **[[person:William Penny|Penny's]] two Aberdeen brigs** Lady Franklin and Sophia, under Penny and [[person:Alexander Stewart]], with the surgeon [[person:Peter Cormack Sutherland]], the naturalist [[person:Robert Anstruther Goodsir]] — whose brother Harry was assistant surgeon of the Erebus — and the Greenland interpreter [[person:Carl Petersen]]. Penny was a whaling master, not a naval officer, and he took his own route through the ice and beat the Navy to Lancaster Sound.
- **[[person:Edwin Jesse De Haven|De Haven's]] two American brigs** Advance and Rescue, paid for by [[person:Henry Grinnell]], with the young surgeon [[person:Elisha Kent Kane]] aboard (see [[topic:kane-hall-polaris]]).
- **[[person:John Ross|Sir John Ross]]**, aged seventy-three, in the schooner Felix with the interpreter [[person:Adam Beck]]; and [[person:Jane Franklin|Lady Franklin's]] own ketch Prince Albert under [[person:Charles Codrington Forsyth]] with [[person:William Parker Snow]] (see [[page:prince-albert-and-bellot]]).

## Four days in August

**23 August.** Ommanney, with the officers of Assistance and Intrepid, landed at [[place:Cape Riley]] and found the marks of a tent-place, rag, rope, broken bottles and a long-handled dredging instrument. A cairn was visible on the height of Beechey Island; the Intrepid's boat went to it. [[artifact:franklin-search-031|They pulled it down, turned every stone and dug under it, and found nothing]]. Ommanney left his own notice in a cairn at the foot of Cape Riley — [[artifact:franklin-search-030|the only contemporary document of the discovery]]. Sutherland's boat party copied a fuller text of it than Snow did, including the news that a depot had been left at Whaler Point, [[place:Port Leopold]], and that Ommanney intended to go on to Cape Hotham and [[place:Cape Walker]].

**25 August.** The Advance touched at Cape Riley to read the notice and left her own; Snow landed the same day from the Prince Albert and copied both. An American boat brought the news across to Penny's brigs, and Penny went aboard the Assistance himself that afternoon to hear it from Ommanney.

**26 August.** Penny went ashore with Stewart, Goodsir and Petersen and worked north from Cape Spencer. About six miles up the coast they found a stone-walled encampment twelve feet across with a paved floor and a fireplace: soup canisters (one marked "Mr. M'Donald"), oak staves, a cask end scratched "mixed pickles", leaves of a manuscript book, and part of a newspaper dated September 1844. Petersen judged the hut about four years old.

**27 August.** The brigs made fast in Union Bay at eight in the morning, alongside the Felix and the Americans, and Penny at once sent a party across to Beechey Island — [[artifact:franklin-search-061|all his officers except the chief mates, under Stewart's orders]]. It is worth being exact, because the credit is usually given wrongly: Ommanney found the first traces at Cape Riley on the 23rd, and **Stewart's party found the winter camp and the graves on the 27th**, with Penny coming ashore afterwards when word was sent to him, bringing Sir John Ross, De Haven and the rest of the commanders with him.

What they found on the southern slope was [[artifact:franklin-search-039|a cairn built up in layers of preserved-meat tins packed with gravel]] — [[artifact:franklin-search-062|six or seven hundred tins counted, and every one of them opened]]. Along the northern shore: the embankment of a storehouse, a carpenter's shop, an armourer's heap of cinders and iron scraps, washing-tubs made from the ends of salt-meat casks, written and printed paper dated 1844 and 1845 — and [[artifact:franklin-search-033|three graves]]. John Torrington of the Terror, dead 1 January 1846, aged 20. John Hartnell of the Erebus, dead 4 January 1846, aged 25. William Braine, Royal Marines, of the Erebus, dead 3 April 1846, aged 32.

**27–29 August.** Stewart and Sutherland carried the news east in a boat and put it aboard the Resolute off Gascoyne Inlet on the evening of the 27th; the Pioneer ran aground rounding Beechey Island the same night. Osborn got ashore on the 29th and walked the camp: [[artifact:franklin-search-038|a garden]] with its border of transplanted moss and poppies, already dug up by searchers looking for a buried record; a shooting gallery with a "Soup and Bouilli" tin shot through as a target; and [[artifact:franklin-search-037|a pair of Cashmere gloves laid out on a stone to dry, a pebble on each palm]], where somebody had left them in 1846.

## What was not found

No record. No document of any kind. [[artifact:franklin-search-032|The discovery settled that Franklin had reached Lancaster Sound]] — until that week nobody could be sure the ships had not foundered in Baffin Bay — but it said nothing about where they had gone next. [[artifact:franklin-search-034|"The silent cliff bore no mark."]]

Why Franklin left no message at a camp he occupied for ten months has never been explained. It was standard practice, the cairn was there, and it was empty. The consequence was immediate and expensive: [[artifact:franklin-search-036|with no record, the search had to divide three ways]] — south-west by Cape Walker, north-west by [[place:Wellington Channel]], west by [[place:Melville Island]] — and it spent four more years going down all three. Franklin had taken a fourth route, south down [[place:Peel Sound]], which the Admiralty thought too shallow and too choked to matter.

From the top of the Beechey cairn Osborn could see Leopold Island, where [[person:James Clark Ross]] had wintered two years earlier without crossing to look: [[artifact:franklin-search-035|"Would to God that, in 1848, Sir James Ross had known that within forty miles of him Franklin had wintered."]]

What happened next — the two winter camps, the 105-man sledge campaign of the following spring, and the quarrel that ended the season — is on its own page: [[page:the-winter-of-1850-51]].
