Eight vessels found Franklin's first winter camp in August 1850 (see [[page:beechey-island-1850]]) and none of them got out of the archipelago that season. Seven of them wintered within twenty miles of each other on the [[place:Cornwallis Island]] shore; the other two drifted for nine months in the pack. What they did with the following spring is the largest sledging operation ever mounted in the Arctic, and the reason a great deal of this archipelago is on the chart at all.

## The winter

Nobody got out. Austin's four ships froze in on the fixed floe between [[place:Griffith Island]] and Cape Martyr at the end of September; Penny's two brigs and Ross's Felix anchored in [[place:Assistance Bay]] on the [[place:Cornwallis Island]] shore about 12 September; the two American brigs were caught in the pack and drifted with it for nine months, back down Wellington Channel and out into Baffin Bay.

The two winter camps came through very differently. Penny's brigs had no deaths and, he told the Admiralty, "there has not been one single case of sickness in either the 'Lady Franklin' or 'Sophia.'" [[artifact:franklin-search-066|A theatre and a school opened on 4 November]]; the government had put about eighty volumes aboard the Sophia and more than twice that number aboard the Lady Franklin, and the two crews exchanged them as they were read; men hooked fish through holes cut in the ice of the lake ashore; and the crews of both vessels were paraded for two hours a day on the Lady Franklin's deck and marched round to fife and drum, which [[person:Peter Cormack Sutherland|Sutherland]] thought was what kept them well. The Felix, alongside them, had eight cases of scurvy by the end of November — a straightforward verdict on her provisions — and Penny kept her supplied with preserved carrots, potatoes and porter all winter. [[artifact:franklin-search-076|The four naval ships housed over on the floe]] is the only contemporary picture of that winter here.

## The sledge campaign of spring 1851

The naval squadron spent the winter preparing what is still the largest man-hauling operation ever mounted in the Arctic. Depots went out in October 1850. On the evening of **15 April 1851** the sledges marched away from the north-west bluff of Griffith Island and were lost to one another in snow and mist within minutes. The two accounts count them differently, and both are printed: [[person:Sherard Osborn|Osborn]] writes that "fifteen sledges, manned by one hundred and five men and officers, were equipped for the search, leaving on board the four vessels of the squadron, seventy-five souls"; Austin's own official narrative in the [[source:greatbritain1852arctic|blue book]] says that on the morning of the 12th "the whole of the sledges, fourteen in number, (manned by 104 officers and men, and provisioned some for forty and other for forty-two days, with an average dragging weight of 205 lbs. per man,) were conducted under the command of Captain Ommanney to an advanced position on the ice off the north-west end of Griffith Island", a gale on the 14th holding them there until the evening of the 15th. Penny's own six sledges and forty-one men started two days later, on the 17th.

- [[artifact:franklin-search-021|McClintock took the sledge Perseverance to Melville Island]]: eight hundred miles in eighty days, out to Parry's [[place:Winter Harbour]] and Cape Dundas, back with part of Parry's cart wheel and the news that the island was full of musk-oxen.
- [[artifact:franklin-search-022|Ommanney took seven sledges to Cape Walker]] and west along [[place:Prince of Wales Island]]. On 14 May he and Osborn saw from a table-land "a wide break in the continuation of the land… a channel full twenty-five miles wide" — [[place:McClintock Channel]]. Franklin's ships were beset in ice that had come down it, two hundred miles south.
- Aldrich went sixty-two days up the west coast of [[place:Bathurst Island]] to 76° 15′ N; M'Dougall's sledge proved Bathurst and Cornwallis one land; Browne went south down Peel Sound from Cape Walker — the right direction, and not far enough.
- [[artifact:franklin-search-067|Penny went up Wellington Channel by dog sledge]] with Petersen and one seaman, and on 16 May found [[artifact:franklin-search-064|a strait and open water]] at Point Surprise, in [[place:Queens Channel]], a month before Barrow Strait broke up. He came back, mounted a whale boat on a sledge, and in June and July got it into that water and pushed to Cape Sir John Franklin beyond 77° N.

The cost, on the naval side: eighteen frostbite cases, several needing amputation of parts of the feet, sixteen men and one officer snow-blind in one tent on 1 May, and a single death. Osborn's book calls the man John Malcolm, a seaman; Sutherland calls him George Malcolm, captain of the hold. [[artifact:franklin-search-095|Austin's own official narrative in the 1852 blue book settles it]]: "George S. Malcolm, captain of the hold of the Resolute, a native of Dundee, whose death was attributed to exhaustion and frostbite brought on whilst labouring as captain of the sledge Excellent, (virtually, it may be said,) died at his post." He was buried on the north-east shore of [[place:Griffith Island]]. Osborn had the forename and the rating wrong.

## The quarrel, and what came of it

On **11 August 1851** Austin's squadron steamed into Assistance Bay and the two commanders consulted. Penny brought his open water and a piece of English elm picked up on the beach beyond Cape Becher; Austin was unmoved — [[artifact:franklin-search-065|he said that in Penny's place he would conclude at once that the search need not go north-west of Beechey Island]]. Penny had no steam power and no stores for another winter, and both would have had to come from Austin. He wrote Austin a note saying he had done all that could be done within Wellington Channel, and asking, "What more can be done?" The squadrons sailed for home the next day.

## What the inquiry actually found

The Admiralty appointed an [[source:greatbritain1852arctic|Arctic Committee]], which took evidence from both sides in the spring of 1852. It did not, as is often said, split the difference. On the central question — did Penny ask Austin for the steamer on 11 August 1851? — it decided against Penny: "the evidence brought before us has not, in our opinion, corroborated the statement, that he expressed to Captain Austin at the time any such sentiments," and it accounted for his change of story by his own words in evidence, that on reaching England "he found everybody disappointed."

The reason was on paper, in Penny's own hand. [[artifact:franklin-search-096|His letter to Austin of 4 August 1851]] described the open water above Wellington Strait as "dangerous even for a boat, much more to a ship, unless clear of ice, which from its present appearance would not be so that season," and told Austin he was determined to go home at once. The Committee thought Austin "could only put one construction" on that. It also noted that although Penny afterwards drew a distinction between Wellington Strait and the channels beyond, which he had by then named Queen Victoria's Channel, "no such distinction appears in the track chart delivered by him to Captain Austin at the time."

It did not let Austin off entirely. [[artifact:franklin-search-097|It found that "some desultory conversation... certainly appears to have taken place"]] on 11 August, and that this was exactly why Austin had pressed Penny so hard for a written opinion — "very prudently and properly, in our opinion." And it thought "considerable benefit might have arisen" if one of the two expeditions had hung on near the entrance a fortnight longer to see what the ice did, and that Penny could have done it without risking a winter.

[[artifact:franklin-search-098|Its verdict on both men]] turns on the emptiness of Beechey Island: since no record of Franklin's intended route had been found "after the most diligent and repeated search, (thus leaving this question a mere matter of conjecture,) both Captain Austin and Mr. Penny were fully justified... in not risking their detention in the ice during another winter."

Neither man commanded in the Arctic again. But the theory that Franklin had gone north up Wellington Channel survived the inquiry — which is why [[person:Edward Belcher|Belcher]] was sent up that channel with two ships the next season. See [[page:belchers-five-ships]] and [[page:belchers-eastern-division-1853]].

## On the chart today

A ship in Tallurutiup Imanga now passes all of this inside a day's steaming. Beechey Island is a National Historic Site; the three headboards on the beach are replicas, the originals being in the Canadian Museum of History, and there is a fourth grave beside them — Thomas Morgan, a seaman late of the Investigator, buried on 24 May 1854. Northumberland House, the depot the North Star built in 1852–54, stands in ruins on the shore. Qausuittuq (Resolute), fifty miles west, carries the name of Austin's flagship, and Assistance Bay, Griffith Island, Cape Riley and Union Bay are all names given in that one crowded season.
