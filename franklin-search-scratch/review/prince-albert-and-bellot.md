Of the fifty-odd expeditions that went looking for Franklin, the two that got closest to the right ground were both sent by his widow in the same small ship, and both were paid for by public subscription and by [[person:Jane Franklin|Lady Franklin]] selling out everything she could legally touch.

The Prince Albert was a ninety-ton ketch built for the Azores fruit trade, bought from Messrs White of Cowes and sent to Aberdeen to be doubled with American elm, sheathed with iron at the bows and re-rigged. She made two voyages, in 1850 and in 1851-52. The second one found [[place:Bellot Strait]].

## 1850: Forsyth, Snow, and the news brought home

[[artifact:franklin-search-025|The first voyage]] sailed from Aberdeen on 5 June 1850 under Commander [[person:Charles Codrington Forsyth]], with [[person:William Parker Snow]] aboard as Lady Franklin's man. Her orders were the best orders any ship got that year: search the west coast of [[place:Prince Regent Inlet]] to the bottom of the Gulf of Boothia, then the west side of Boothia into James Ross Strait and down to Simpson Strait. That is within a hundred miles of where the [[topic:franklin-1845|Erebus and Terror]] actually lay.

She got as far as Fury Beach on 23 August 1850 and was stopped by hummocky ice. Snow begged for a boat to land; he was refused, and at four that afternoon the crew were told the ship was going home. Forsyth had one season, a crew of eighteen and no ice master, and the decision was defensible; Snow never forgave it, and said so in print.

What the voyage did do was carry the news. On her way out she called at [[place:Cape Riley]] on 25 August, where Snow copied [[artifact:franklin-search-030|Ommanney's notice of the 23rd]] and De Haven's of that morning. The Prince Albert was back at Aberdeen on 1 October 1850, a year before the naval squadrons, and so it was Lady Franklin's little private ketch that told England the first traces of her husband had been found. She had also called at [[place:Port Leopold]] on 21 August, where Snow found [[person:James Clark Ross|James Clark Ross's]] house and depot standing and nothing in the cylinders on the flagstaff — "they had not been there."

## 1851-52: a Company man and a French officer

For the second voyage Lady Franklin chose [[person:William Kennedy]], a Hudson's Bay Company trader born at Cumberland House to a Cree mother and an Orkneyman father, who had never commanded a ship and who ran the expedition on Company principles: skins instead of naval slops, pemmican, snow houses instead of tents, dogs — and not a drop of wine, beer or spirits on board. [[artifact:franklin-search-051|Bellot thought the last of these was why the crew stayed healthy and good-tempered]].

His second in command was [[person:Joseph Rene Bellot]], a twenty-five-year-old sub-lieutenant of the French Imperial Navy, an orphan educated by public subscription in Rochefort, who had volunteered and whose government had lent him. He sailed with the French flag at the fore. Between them they made the most effective use of a small ship of anyone in the search.

[[artifact:franklin-search-026|The ship]] left Aberdeen on 22 May 1851, forced 120 miles of pack ice on a southern route no naval captain would have tried, and reached Barrow Strait in September to find it barred from side to side. On 10 September, with Kennedy and four men ashore at Whaler Point, the ship was blown away; the captain was separated from his vessel for six weeks, and it was Bellot who found him, dragging the jolly boat overland from Batty Bay at the third attempt and reaching Whaler Point on 17 October. The Prince Albert then lay in [[place:Batty Bay]] for 330 days.

## The sledge journey, and the strait

[[artifact:franklin-search-027|The journey]] began on 25 February 1852 and ended on 30 May: ninety-seven days, about 1,100 miles, six men and five dogs, sleeping every night in snow houses. [[artifact:franklin-search-052|Bellot described the method as simply adopting "the manner of life and the customs of the Esquimaux and the Indians."]] Nothing in the naval sledge campaigns of 1851 came close to it for distance covered per man.

On 5 April they camped at [[place:Brentford Bay]] and saw a column of vapour rising from open water to the west — in April, in a country where everything should be frozen. On the 6th two pairs went to look. On the **7th of April 1852** the six of them travelled the northern channel from end to end, about twenty miles with its windings, and from a hill at the west end saw a broad channel running away north and south. [[artifact:franklin-search-050|Kennedy named it Bellot Strait]].

It matters for two reasons. It proves North Somerset an island; and its southern shore, [[place:Murchison Promontory]], is the northernmost point of the continent of North America, so the strait is the true line between the mainland and the archipelago. Kennedy grasped neither. He named the south shore "Levesque Island", took it for an island, crossed the ice of [[place:Peel Sound]] believing he had crossed a channel, walked over land he called Prince of Wales Land, and went home convinced that North Somerset and Prince of Wales Land were joined and that Franklin therefore could not have gone south of [[place:Cape Walker]]. The one useful negative he could report was [[artifact:franklin-search-056|"no record or trace"]].

He had, in fact, walked across the mouth of the channel down which the ships had sailed. The party had severe scurvy by the time they turned for home, and they reached Whaler Point on 15 May living on lime juice and cranberries.

The ship cut out of Batty Bay on 6 August 1852 and reached [[place:Beechey Island]] on the 19th, where Kennedy's plan to stay a third year collapsed — [[person:Edward Belcher|Belcher's]] squadron had the ground already (see [[page:belchers-five-ships]]). He left his stores, his dogs and Prince Albert's barrel-organ with the North Star and sailed for home on the 24th, reaching Aberdeen on 7 October 1852.

## Bellot's second season, and his death

Refused an expedition of his own by the French Ministry of Marine, Bellot asked leave in March 1853 to go north again in [[person:Edward Augustus Inglefield|Inglefield's]] steamer Phoenix, and was aboard at Woolwich on 10 May. The Phoenix's business was to get Admiralty despatches to Belcher, somewhere up [[place:Wellington Channel]].

Inglefield being away looking for Captain [[person:William John Samuel Pullen|Pullen]], Bellot would not wait. He set out from [[place:Beechey Island]] on 12 August 1853 with the quarter-master of the North Star and three seamen, a sledge and an india-rubber canoe, up the eastern shore of Wellington Channel. On the night of the 14th, three miles off shore in a violent south-easterly gale, two of the party got ashore and rigged a pass-rope; then the floe began to drive, Bellot shouted to let it go, and the ice carried him and the seamen William Johnson and David Hook out into the channel. [[artifact:franklin-search-054|Madden watched them from a hill for six hours]].

Four days later, at about a quarter past eight on the morning of **18 August 1853**, Bellot lashed up his books and stepped out of their shelter to see how the ice was driving. [[artifact:franklin-search-053|He was gone in four minutes]]; his stick was found across a crack five fathoms wide. Johnson and Hook came ashore alive. He was twenty-seven, and he is the only man in the whole thirty years of searching who was killed by an accident rather than by disease.

A head-board went up on Beechey Island; on the morning of 27 August 1854, hours before the squadron left the Arctic for the last time, the French officer [[person:Emile de Bray]] set on it [[artifact:franklin-search-045|the marble tablet sent out from England]], and three volleys were fired. An obelisk to him stands outside the Royal Naval Hospital at Greenwich, paid for by subscription in a country not his own.

Six years later [[person:Francis Leopold McClintock]] wintered the Fox at the eastern end of Bellot Strait, and it was from there that the sledges went out that found the record at Victory Point. See [[page:closing-the-record-trail]].

## A note on two dates

The sources disagree about when the Phoenix reached Beechey Island in 1853, and therefore about the timing of Bellot's last journey. M'Dougall gives 14 August for her arrival, which cannot be reconciled with Bellot setting out on the 12th as the Memoirs have it. The date of Bellot's death, 18 August 1853, is fixed by three sworn depositions and is not in doubt.
