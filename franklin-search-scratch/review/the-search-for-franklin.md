Between 1848 and 1880 something like fifty expeditions went looking for Sir John Franklin's two ships. They came from the Royal Navy, the United States Navy, the Hudson's Bay Company, the Aberdeen whaling fleet, the New York newspapers, and from the private purse of one woman in London. They sailed into every entrance of the Canadian Arctic Archipelago and walked across most of its islands. In the course of doing so they lost more ships than Franklin lost, produced the first chart of the archipelago, and found — eventually, and mostly by asking Inuit — almost the whole of what we know about how 129 men died.

This page is about the search as a thing in itself. What happened to the [[topic:franklin-1845|Franklin expedition]] — the voyage, [[person:John Rae|Rae's]] report of 1854, [[source:mcclintock1859voyage|McClintock's]] voyage in the Fox, the Victory Point record, and the Inuit testimony about the last months — is told there. Here the subject is the thirty years of looking.

## Why it went on so long

Franklin sailed in May 1845 with provisions for three years. He was not expected back before 1847 and nobody was surprised by silence until the autumn of that year. When the Admiralty finally moved, in the spring of 1848, it had no information at all: no message, no wreckage, no rumour. Its officers did not know whether the ships were north-west of Melville Island, south in the mainland channels, or already out into the Pacific. [[artifact:franklin-search-101|The map at the front of McClintock's book]] shows what was known of the region when Franklin sailed, and it is mostly blank where the answer lay.

So the first search went out in three directions at once — [[person:James Clark Ross]] east through [[place:Lancaster Sound]], [[person:John Richardson|Richardson]] and Rae overland down the Mackenzie, and [[person:Thomas Edward Laws Moore|Moore's]] Plover west through Bering Strait. None of the three came within four hundred miles of where the ships actually lay.

That set the pattern. Each season's failure was read not as evidence that the men were dead but as evidence that the wrong place had been looked at, which justified another season. The Admiralty's public reward of £20,000 — mentioned by [[person:Sherard Osborn|Osborn]] and by every narrative of the period, though the wording of the proclamation itself is not among the papers used here — drew in whalers and private ventures. And [[person:Jane Franklin|Lady Franklin]] would not let it stop.

## The shape of it

The search falls into five parts.

**1848–49, the three prongs.** All three fail. Ross winters at [[place:Port Leopold]]; his sledges, and a young lieutenant named [[person:Francis Leopold McClintock|McClintock]], learn how to man-haul. See [[page:the-search-begins-1848]].

**1850–51, the year of the fleets.** Thirteen or fourteen vessels converge on Lancaster Sound in one summer: [[person:Horatio Thomas Austin|Austin's]] four naval ships, [[person:William Penny|Penny's]] two whaling brigs, [[person:Edwin Jesse De Haven|De Haven's]] two American brigs, old [[person:John Ross|Sir John Ross]] at seventy-three in a schooner, and Lady Franklin's little Prince Albert. On 23 August 1850 [[person:Erasmus Ommanney]] found the first traces at [[place:Cape Riley]]; four days later a party from Penny's brigs, under [[person:Alexander Stewart]], walked up on the winter camp and the three graves on [[place:Beechey Island]]. There was no written record of where the ships had gone next, and there still is none. See [[page:beechey-island-1850]] for the four days, and [[page:the-winter-of-1850-51]] for the winter that followed and the 105-man sledge campaign of the next spring. [[artifact:franklin-search-076|Austin's four ships housed over for that winter]] is the one contemporary picture of the wintering the search has here.

**1851–54, the private ships and the great naval failure.** [[person:William Kennedy|Kennedy]] and [[person:Joseph Rene Bellot|Bellot]] sledge round Somerset Island for Lady Franklin and reach [[place:Bellot Strait]]; Bellot drowns two years later carrying dispatches. [[person:Edward Belcher|Belcher]] takes five ships north and leaves four of them in the ice in 1854 — the single worst decision of the search, and the end of the Navy's part in it. See [[page:prince-albert-and-bellot]] and [[page:belchers-five-ships]].

**1854–59, the record trail opens.** Rae, surveying [[place:Boothia Peninsula]] for the Hudson's Bay Company and not looking for Franklin at all, meets a man at Pelly Bay ([[place:Kugaaruk]]) in April 1854 who tells him about forty white men who starved near a large river. The Admiralty stops. Lady Franklin does not: she buys the steam yacht Fox and sends McClintock, and in 1859 [[person:William Hobson]] finds the only written record the expedition ever left.

**1860–80, the long tail.** [[person:Charles Francis Hall]] spends the best part of two decades among Inuit in Frobisher Bay and at Naujaat (Repulse Bay) collecting testimony (see [[topic:kane-hall-polaris]]), and [[person:Frederick Schwatka]] closes the file in 1878–80 with the longest sledge journey then on record, looking for papers that no longer existed. See [[page:closing-the-record-trail]].

## What it cost

The often-repeated claim that more men were lost looking for Franklin than were lost with him is not true. Franklin lost 129 men and two ships. The search lost far fewer men, and it is worth being exact about which deaths are actually documented in the sources used here rather than repeating a round number. Austin's squadron of 180 men lost exactly one in two years — [[artifact:franklin-search-095|George S. Malcolm, captain of the hold of the Resolute, a native of Dundee]], who died of exhaustion and frostbite in the sledge campaign of May 1851. Bellot was drowned in Wellington Channel in August 1853. Three of the Investigator's people died in [[place:Mercy Bay]] in 1853 and a fourth, Thomas Morgan, died aboard the North Star and was buried beside Franklin's three men at Beechey Island on 24 May 1854 (see [[topic:mcclure-investigator]]). [[person:Elisha Kent Kane|Kane's]] second Grinnell expedition lost men in 1854–55 as well (see [[topic:kane-hall-polaris]]). A full count across all fifty expeditions has not been made from the muster rolls, and the round figures in general circulation are guesses.

Two of the search's own winter camps show how much depended on provisions rather than on luck. Penny's two brigs came through 1850–51 in Assistance Bay with no deaths and, on his own report to the Admiralty, "not one single case of sickness in either the 'Lady Franklin' or 'Sophia'"; Sir John Ross's Felix, moored alongside them, had eight cases of scurvy by the end of November 1850, and Penny kept her supplied with preserved carrots, potatoes and porter all winter.

Ships are a different matter. HMS Investigator was left in [[place:Mercy Bay]] in 1853 (see [[topic:mcclure-investigator]]); Resolute, Intrepid, Assistance and Pioneer were left in the ice by Belcher's order in 1854; the transport Breadalbane was crushed by ice and sank off Beechey Island in fifteen minutes on 21 August 1853. Six ships lost looking, against Franklin's two — and all six were lost within about two hundred miles of Beechey Island.

## What it found

Not Franklin, for eleven years, and never the ships: HMS Erebus and HMS Terror were located by Parks Canada and Inuit knowledge in 2014 and 2016, in water that Inuit had described to Hall in 1869 and to Schwatka's people in 1879.

What the search did produce was the map. Nobody set out to survey the Canadian Arctic Archipelago; it was surveyed as a by-product of looking under every stone in it. [[artifact:franklin-search-077|Arrowsmith's chart of 1860]] is the summary sheet, and [[artifact:franklin-search-115|the folding map at the end of McClintock's narrative]] shows the archipelago as it stood when the search ended. [[person:George Frederick Mecham|Mecham]] and McClintock walked the coasts of [[place:Prince Patrick Island]] and Eglinton Island; Belcher's parties laid down Grinnell Peninsula and the channels north of Devon Island; Ommanney and Osborn charted [[place:Prince of Wales Island]]; Rae completed the last unmapped link of the continental coast and showed King William Land to be an island; Kennedy and Bellot fixed the northernmost point of mainland North America at [[place:Murchison Promontory]]. The Dictionary of Canadian Biography's judgement is that the loss of Franklin's expedition did more for the exploration of the Canadian Arctic than its success would have done, and that the credit for that belongs to Lady Franklin ([[source:cooke1972griffin]]). See [[page:the-mapping-of-the-archipelago]].

## Why it still matters here

Every ship working the eastern Canadian Arctic today, this one included, navigates on a coastline whose outline was first drawn by men dragging sledges in the 1850s looking for other men. The place names on the chart around us — Assistance Bay, Griffith Island, Resolute, Bellot Strait, Cape Riley — were given during the search, not by anyone who had been there before.

The search also created the first sustained encounter between Qallunaat and several Inuit communities: the Netsilingmiut of [[place:Boothia Peninsula]], the Ukjulingmiut of the Adelaide Peninsula, the Iglulingmiut at [[place:Naujaat]]. Almost everything the searchers eventually learned, they learned by asking Inuit and being told the truth — and by ignoring the answer for years because it was not the answer they wanted. That is the part of this history that the communities of Uqsuqtuuq (Gjoa Haven), Kugaaruk and Taloyoak have been correcting ever since. See [[topic:inuit-oral-history]].
