Franklin sailed in May 1845 with provisions for three years. Nobody expected him back before the autumn of 1847, and nobody was seriously alarmed until the winter that followed. When the Admiralty finally moved, in the spring of 1848, it had nothing at all to go on: no message, no wreckage, no rumour. Its officers did not know whether the ships were north-west of [[place:Melville Island]], south among the mainland channels, or already through into the Pacific. [[artifact:franklin-search-101|The sketch map of the Arctic as it was known when Franklin sailed]] shows the problem better than any description: the northern channels are guesswork and the west coast of King William Land is not drawn at all.

So the first search went out in three directions at once, and every one of them was aimed at a place where the ships were not.

## The overland prong: Richardson and Rae

[[person:John Richardson]] was sixty, senior physician at the naval hospital at Haslar, and the last man alive who had been on both of Franklin's overland expeditions of the 1820s. He volunteered to take the land route, and in November 1847 he asked the Admiralty to give him [[person:John Rae]] — a Hudson's Bay Company chief trader, "a skilful hunter… an accurate observer with the sextant" — as his second.

The object was exact: "to trace the coast between the Mackenzie and Coppermine Rivers, and the shores of Victoria and Wollaston Lands lying opposite to Cape Krusenstern." Four boats went out a year ahead under John Bell, with twenty seamen and sappers chosen as much for being joiners and blacksmiths as for being sailors, so that they could build their own winter quarters. [[artifact:franklin-search-068|Richardson and Rae went after them]] by mail steamer from Liverpool on 25 March 1848 and caught the boats in July.

They were into the [[place:Mackenzie River|Mackenzie]] estuary by 3 August, where two hundred Inuvialuit came off in kayaks and umiaks within the hour. Working east past Point Atkinson, Cape Bathurst and Cape Parry and laying depots as their instructions required, they asked at every camp — through their interpreter [[person:Albert|Albert One-eye]], an Inuk from the East Main — and got the same answer everywhere: [[artifact:franklin-search-070|no ships, no white men, ever, on this coast]].

The ice beat them. By late August the boats were being dragged over hummocks and portaged round [[place:Cape Krusenstern]], and on 1 September, eight miles short of Cape Kendall, Richardson gave them up: [[artifact:franklin-search-071|"the sole hinderance to my crossing Dolphin and Union Straits was the impracticable condition of the close-packed drift-ice."]] They walked thirteen days to [[place:Fort Confidence]] on [[place:Great Bear Lake]], sixty to seventy pounds a man, [[artifact:franklin-search-072|one night on bare rock in fog with no fuel]], and arrived on 15 September — [[artifact:franklin-search-074|forty-two souls to feed, eighteen of them sent away two days later so the rest could eat]].

Richardson went home in 1849, reaching Liverpool on 6 November after nineteen months. Rae stayed, tried the crossing again that summer, and got the same answer from the people of Wollaston Land: neither ships nor white men. The second attempt cost Albert One-eye his life — he was drowned at the Bloody Fall on the Coppermine, and Rae's despatch from Fort Confidence records the loss without giving the day. Richardson thought the two negatives had done something — "the knowledge of these facts had an influence with the Admiralty in concentrating the future search in the vicinity of Melville Island" — and he was right. It was the wrong conclusion drawn correctly from good evidence, honestly gathered by asking. Rae's later journeys added more new coast per mile walked than anyone else's in the search (see [[page:the-mapping-of-the-archipelago]]), and it was Rae who finally got the answer in 1854 ([[page:closing-the-record-trail]]).

## The eastern prong: James Clark Ross at Port Leopold

[[person:James Clark Ross]] — the man who had reached the North Magnetic Pole in 1831 and had just come back from four years in the Antarctic — took HMS Enterprise and HMS Investigator into Lancaster Sound in the summer of 1848 with orders to work west and then south-west after Franklin. Two of his sledge parties were meant to come down to the continental coast and meet Richardson's boats at Cape Bathurst and Cape Krusenstern; Richardson noted drily that those two parties "would pass over that space in which most probably the ships have become involved."

The ice stopped Ross at once. He wintered at [[place:Port Leopold]] on the north-east corner of Somerset Island, and did three things there that outlasted him:

- He built a house at [[place:Whaler Point]] and left a steam launch, a depot of provisions and a flagstaff with message cylinders. Every expedition that came into [[place:Prince Regent Inlet]] for eleven years afterwards called there — [[person:William Parker Snow|Snow]] in 1850, [[person:William Kennedy|Kennedy]] and [[person:Joseph Rene Bellot|Bellot]] in 1851-52, [[person:Francis Leopold McClintock|McClintock]] in 1858 — and Kennedy's stranded party lived on that depot for six weeks.
- He trapped Arctic foxes, riveted engraved copper collars to them saying where the ships and depots were, and released them, in the hope that Franklin's men might shoot one. None was ever recovered.
- He sent out sledge parties in the spring of 1849, and in one of them a young lieutenant named Francis Leopold McClintock learned the trade. Ross and McClintock went about five hundred miles in forty days along the north and west coasts of Somerset Island — the longest naval sledge journey to that date, and the direct ancestor of [[page:the-winter-of-1850-51|the 1851 campaign]] and of the Fox.

Ross found nothing. His ships were beset in August 1849 and carried helplessly out into Baffin Bay by the pack before they could be freed, and he came home in November having, in the Navy's judgement, wasted a season.

The bitterest fact about 1848 only became clear two years later. Beechey Island is about forty miles from Port Leopold across [[place:Barrow Strait]]. Ross never crossed to look. [[artifact:franklin-search-035|Osborn, standing on the Beechey cairn in 1850 with Leopold Island in plain sight, wrote what everybody was thinking.]]

## The western prong: Bering Strait

The third prong assumed Franklin might have got through and be coming out the other side. HMS Plover, under [[person:Thomas Edward Laws Moore]], and HMS Herald, under [[person:Henry Kellett]], were stationed at [[place:Bering Strait]] from 1848 to meet him.

They stayed a very long time. The Plover was in the western Arctic from 1848 until 1854 — depot ship, mail drop and floating base for a series of other expeditions, wintering on the Chukotka coast, at [[place:Point Barrow]] and in [[place:Kotzebue Sound]]. Kellett sighted Herald Island on 17 August 1849, and in 1852 went to the other end of the search as captain of HMS Resolute (see [[page:belchers-five-ships]]). The station's one substantial piece of new survey was [[person:William John Samuel Pullen|Pullen's]] boat voyage of July 1849, east from Wainwright Inlet to the Mackenzie and up to [[place:Fort Simpson]] — over a thousand miles in four boats, joining the two ends of the search on the map. From the same station, in January 1850, the Enterprise and Investigator went in again under Collinson and McClure: [[topic:mcclure-investigator]].

## Why none of it worked

None of the three prongs came within four hundred miles of where the Erebus and Terror lay, off the north-west coast of [[place:King William Island]]. Three reasons explain the eleven years that followed.

**The Admiralty was searching for a ship, not a wreck.** The plan assumed a vessel beset somewhere and a crew able to walk to a depot — hence the caches at Point Separation, Cape Bathurst, Cape Parry and Fort Good Hope, and hence the foxes with collars.

**[[place:Peel Sound]] was not on anyone's list.** Franklin's orders named [[place:Cape Walker]] and, failing that, [[place:Wellington Channel]]. He had gone south down Peel Sound, which the Admiralty regarded as too shallow and too choked with ice to be a route. It was the one direction the first search did not test.

**The right question was asked in the wrong place.** Richardson and Rae questioned Inuvialuit along six hundred miles of coast and were told the truth. So were Rae's informants at Pelly Bay in 1854 — but Pelly Bay is eight hundred miles east of Dolphin and Union Strait, in country the first search had no reason to visit.

What 1848 did produce was the machinery for everything after it: the sledge system, the depots, the reward, and a public appetite that turned into thirteen ships in Lancaster Sound in one summer two years later.
