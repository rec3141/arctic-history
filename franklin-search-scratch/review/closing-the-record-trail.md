By the spring of 1854 the Admiralty had had enough. On 31 March the officers and crews of the Erebus and Terror were struck off the Navy's books as dead; in April the order went out to withdraw everything from the Arctic (see [[page:belchers-five-ships]]). Six years, some forty expeditions and four abandoned ships had produced one winter camp on [[place:Beechey Island]] and not a line of writing.

The answer came four months later, from a man who was not looking.

## Rae at Pelly Bay, April 1854

[[person:John Rae]] was surveying the west coast of [[place:Boothia Peninsula]] for the Hudson's Bay Company — closing the last gap in the continental coastline, which was his own work and had nothing to do with Franklin. In April 1854, on the ice near [[place:Kugaaruk|Pelly Bay]], he met Inuit hunters, and from one of them — [[person:In-nook-poo-zhee-jook]] — he learned that a party of white men had starved to death some distance to the west, beyond a large river full of falls and rapids.

Over that spring and summer, at Pelly Bay and then at [[place:Repulse Bay|Naujaat (Repulse Bay)]], he took down the rest and bought the relics: silver forks and spoons with the crests and initials of Franklin's officers, a small silver plate engraved *Sir John Franklin, K.C.B.*, watches, telescopes, part of an order of merit. His report to the Admiralty of 29 July 1854 laid it out plainly — about forty men seen dragging a boat south over the ice off [[place:King William Island|King William's Land]] in the spring of 1850, and later the bodies of some thirty on the mainland and five on an island near the mouth of the Back River. And then the sentence that ruined him:

> "From the mutilated state of many of the corpses and the contents of the kettles, it is evident that our wretched countrymen had been driven to the last resource — cannibalism — as a means of prolonging existence."

The report reached London on 23 October 1854 and was printed at once. What followed is told in [[topic:inuit-oral-history]] and [[topic:franklin-1845]]: Lady Franklin refused to accept it, Charles Dickens wrote two essays in *Household Words* attacking the testimony of "covetous, treacherous and cruel" savages, and Rae was denied the honours a naval officer would have had. He was paid the £10,000 reward in 1856 and never given anything else. Modern forensic work on the bones from [[place:Erebus Bay]] and elsewhere — cut marks, pot-polishing on bone ends — has shown the Inuit account to be accurate in detail. It took a hundred and forty years.

## Anderson, 1855

The government's response was to check the story as cheaply as possible. Instead of a ship, it asked the Hudson's Bay Company; the Company sent [[person:James Anderson]], chief factor of the Mackenzie River District, with James Green Stewart and a party of voyageurs, down the Back River by canoe in the summer of 1855.

They reached [[place:Montreal Island]] and Point Ogle, found wood and copper from a ship's boat, and were told by Inuit there of the starving men. They had no interpreter worth the name, no boat that could face salt water, and no means of crossing to King William Island, which is where the bodies actually were. They found no papers and no remains. The government declared itself satisfied and closed the file.

## The Fox, 1857–59

[[person:Jane Franklin|Lady Franklin]] applied once more for a ship and was refused. So she bought one. The Fox was a 177-ton screw yacht; she raised what she could by subscription and put in the rest herself, and gave the command to [[person:Francis Leopold McClintock]], the best sledge traveller in the Navy, who took no pay.

[[artifact:franklin-search-029|The voyage]] began badly. The Fox was beset in [[place:Melville Bay]] in late August 1857 and spent **242 days** in the pack, drifting 1,194 geographical miles south before breaking out off Greenland on 25 April 1858 — "the longest drift I know of." McClintock refitted at Holsteinborg and started the whole season again from the beginning.

In August 1858 he put the memorial tablet Lady Franklin had had made in New York on the flagged square at Beechey Island, beside the cenotaph to Belcher's dead and the small tablet to [[person:Joseph Rene Bellot|Bellot]]. Then he went for [[place:Bellot Strait]] — the passage [[person:William Kennedy]] had found six years earlier (see [[page:prince-albert-and-bellot]]) — forced it at the fourth attempt against six-knot tides, and wintered at its eastern end in an anchorage he named [[place:Port Kennedy]].

In April 1859 three sledge divisions went out from Port Kennedy: McClintock down the east side of King William Island and round to the Back River, [[person:William Hobson]] down the west side, and Allen Young over Prince of Wales Island. McClintock's party met Inuit at Cape Victoria and at Uqsuqtuuq (Gjoa Haven) who sold them silver, buttons and a naval knife and told them, again, that one ship had been crushed and one driven ashore, and that the white men had fallen down and died as they walked.

On **6 May 1859** Hobson opened a cairn at [[place:Victory Point]] on the north-west coast of King William Island and found a single sheet of Admiralty printed form, written round the margins. It is the only document the expedition ever left, and it is the subject of its own page: [[page:franklin-1845-victory-point-record]].

McClintock also found, at [[place:Erebus Bay]], a twenty-eight-foot boat mounted on a heavy sledge, with two skeletons in it and a load of silver, books and boots that has been argued over ever since; and, near Cape Herschel, a skeleton lying face down where the man had fallen.

The Fox got out of Port Kennedy in August 1859 and was in the English Channel on 20 September. McClintock landed at Portsmouth and reached London on the evening of the 21st. The search, as a British national undertaking, ended that evening.

## What closed, and what did not

Three things are worth keeping straight.

**The record trail was opened by Inuit testimony, not by searching.** Rae found the answer in April 1854 by asking a question at Pelly Bay. Every subsequent expedition — Anderson, McClintock, [[person:Charles Francis Hall|Hall]], [[person:Frederick Schwatka|Schwatka]] — was following the same testimony, and every one of them confirmed it. The ships and the sledges of 1848–54 covered tens of thousands of miles and found nothing, because the men who knew were not being asked.

**Nothing was closed in 1859.** The Victory Point record accounts for the expedition only to 25 April 1848. Everything after that date rests on Inuit accounts, on bones, and on the two wrecks — Erebus found in 2014 in Wilmot and Crampton Bay, Terror in 2016 in [[place:Terror Bay]], both within a few miles of where Inuit had said they were.

**The search did not stop when the government stopped paying for it.** Hall spent most of two decades with Inuit at [[place:Naujaat]] and in [[place:Frobisher Bay]] collecting testimony (see [[topic:kane-hall-polaris]]), and Schwatka made the longest sledge journey then on record in 1878–80 looking for papers that had already rotted. The record trail is still open; the Franklin Expedition National Historic Site is co-managed with the Inuit Heritage Trust, and the guardians at Uqsuqtuuq work on the wrecks every summer.


## The people who actually knew

McClintock's two seasons of sledging found the record at Victory Point, a boat, and skeletons. Almost everything else he brought home he was told, through his Danish-Greenlandic interpreter [[person:Carl Petersen]], and the topic owes those people their names.

On the way west in August 1858 the Fox's officers sat in a tent near Navy Board Inlet with [[person:Noo-luk]] and [[person:A-wah-lah]], the two leading men of Kaparoktolik, who drew charts of the coasts they knew and marked the wrecks on them; [[person:Ow-wang-noot]], an old man who had lived at Iglulik in his youth and drew the coast-line to the north; and [[person:A-ra-neet]], who described where the coast ends. In February and March 1859 McClintock met about forty-five people at Cape Victoria on the [[place:Boothia Peninsula]], among them [[person:Oo-na-lee]], who knew the coast to [[place:Bellot Strait]] by name although he had never been there, and [[person:Ooblooria]], who turned out to be the same man [[person:James Clark Ross]] had employed as a guide from the Victory thirty years before and who asked after Ross by his Inuktitut name, Agglugga. [[artifact:franklin-search-100|When McClintock asked after another man of that time and got no answer]], Petersen explained that the silence itself was the answer: the man was dead, and they do not speak of the dead.

The single most valuable thing anyone told him came on 7 May 1859 at the village near what is now [[place:Uqsuqtuuq]] (Gjoa Haven), from an old woman whose name he did not record. She and a boy had been the last to visit the wreck, in the winter of 1857-58. [[artifact:franklin-search-099|"She said many of the white men dropped by the way as they went to the Great River; that some were buried and some were not."]] Three weeks later McClintock found a man lying face down on a gravel ridge and wrote that it was a melancholy truth she had spoken when she said "they fell down and died as they walked along."

This is the same body of testimony, from the same people, that [[person:John Rae]] had gathered at Pelly Bay five years earlier and been abused for repeating. It was accurate then and it is accurate now, and the archaeology of the last seventy years has confirmed it point by point. See [[topic:inuit-oral-history]].
