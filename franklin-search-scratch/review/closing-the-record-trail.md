By the spring of 1854 the Admiralty had had enough. On 31 March the officers and crews of the Erebus and Terror were struck off the Navy's books as dead; in April the order went out to withdraw everything from the Arctic (see [[page:belchers-five-ships]]). Six years, some forty expeditions and four ships left in the ice had produced one winter camp on [[place:Beechey Island]] and not a line of writing.

The answer came four months later, from a man who was not looking.

## Rae at Pelly Bay, April 1854

[[person:John Rae]] was surveying the west coast of [[place:Boothia Peninsula]] for the Hudson's Bay Company — closing the last gap in the continental coastline, which was his own work and had nothing to do with Franklin. In April 1854, on the ice near [[place:Kugaaruk|Pelly Bay]], he met Inuit hunters, and from one of them — [[person:In-nook-poo-zhee-jook]] — he learned that a party of white men had starved to death some distance to the west, beyond a large river full of falls and rapids.

Over that spring and summer, at Pelly Bay and then at [[place:Repulse Bay|Naujaat (Repulse Bay)]], he took down the rest and bought the relics: silver forks and spoons with officers' crests and initials, a small silver plate engraved *Sir John Franklin, K.C.B.*, watches, telescopes, part of an order of merit. [[artifact:franklin-search-058|His report to the Admiralty of 29 July 1854]] laid it out plainly — about forty men seen dragging a boat south over the ice off [[place:King William Island|King William's Land]] in the spring of 1850, and later the bodies of some thirty on the mainland and five on an island near the mouth of the Back River. And then [[artifact:franklin-search-059|the sentence that ruined him]]:

> "From the mutilated state of many of the corpses and the contents of the kettles, it is evident that our wretched countrymen had been driven to the last resource — cannibalism — as a means of prolonging existence."

Rae himself reached England on Sunday 22 October 1854, and the despatch was in print within days. What followed is told in [[topic:inuit-oral-history]] and [[topic:franklin-1845]]: Lady Franklin refused to accept it, and Charles Dickens wrote two essays in *Household Words* attacking the Inuit witnesses on the general principle that [[artifact:inuit-oral-history-025|"we believe every savage to be in his heart covetous, treacherous, and cruel"]]. Rae was denied the honours a naval officer would have had. He was paid the £10,000 reward in 1856 and never given anything else. Modern forensic work on the bones from [[place:Erebus Bay]] and elsewhere — cut marks, pot-polishing on bone ends — has shown the Inuit account to be accurate in detail. It took a hundred and forty years.

## Anderson, 1855

The government's response was to check the story as cheaply as possible. Instead of a ship, it asked the Hudson's Bay Company; the Company sent [[person:James Anderson]], chief factor of the Mackenzie River District, with James Green Stewart and a party of voyageurs, down the Back River by canoe in the summer of 1855.

They reached [[place:Montreal Island]] and Point Ogle, found wood and copper from a ship's boat, and were told by Inuit there of the starving men. They had no interpreter worth the name, no boat that could face salt water, and no way of crossing to King William Island, which is where the bodies were. They found no papers and no remains, and the government declared itself satisfied and closed the file.

## The Fox, 1857–59

[[person:Jane Franklin|Lady Franklin]] applied once more for a ship and was refused. So she bought one. The [[vessel:Fox]] was a 177-ton screw yacht; she raised what she could by subscription and put in the rest herself, and gave the command to [[person:Francis Leopold McClintock]], the best sledge traveller in the Navy, who took no pay.

[[artifact:franklin-search-029|The voyage]] began badly. The Fox was beset in [[place:Melville Bay]] in late August 1857 and spent **242 days** in the pack, drifting, in McClintock's own accounting, "1194 geographical or 1385 statute miles" south before breaking out off Greenland on 25 April 1858 — "the longest drift I know of." [[artifact:franklin-search-101|The second of the two maps at the front of his book]] traces it. McClintock refitted at Holsteinborg and started the whole season again from the beginning; [[artifact:franklin-search-104|the officers taking an observation on the floe]] is a picture of those months.

On 11 August 1858 he anchored inside Cape Riley and [[artifact:franklin-search-108|brought the Fox in to Beechey Island]], where he set the memorial tablet Lady Franklin had had made in New York on the raised flagged square, beside the cenotaph to Belcher's dead and the small tablet to [[person:Joseph Rene Bellot|Bellot]]. Then he went for [[place:Bellot Strait]] — the passage [[person:William Kennedy]] had walked six years earlier (see [[page:prince-albert-and-bellot]]). It took five attempts. Four times in August the ice at the western end turned him back, "the same obstruction... in precisely the same place"; on the morning of **6 September 1858** the strait was clear and the Fox "steamed through the clear waters of Bellot Strait" and made fast to the ice at its western outlet. The tides there run hard enough that at one point, off the table-land, "the ship hardly moved over the ground, although going 6½ knots through the water!" She wintered at the eastern end, in an anchorage McClintock named [[place:Port Kennedy]], with [[artifact:franklin-search-111|an observatory built ashore]].

In April 1859 three sledge divisions went out from Port Kennedy: McClintock down the east side of King William Island and round to the Back River, [[person:William Hobson]] down the west side, and Allen Young over Prince of Wales Island. [[artifact:franklin-search-113|They travelled with dogs as well as men in harness]], which is a large part of why they succeeded where the naval campaigns of 1851 and 1853 had not.

On **6 May 1859** Hobson opened a cairn at [[place:Victory Point]] on the north-west coast of King William Island and found a single sheet of Admiralty printed form, written round the margins. It is the only document the expedition ever left, and it is the subject of its own page: [[page:franklin-1845-victory-point-record]].

McClintock also found, at [[place:Erebus Bay]], a twenty-eight-foot boat mounted on a heavy sledge, with two skeletons in it and a load of silver, books and boots that has been argued over ever since; and, near [[place:Cape John Herschel|Cape Herschel]] on the south coast of King William Island, a skeleton lying face down where the man had fallen.

The Fox got away from Port Kennedy under steam at eleven in the morning of 9 August 1859, was in the English Channel on 20 September, and McClintock landed at Portsmouth and reached London on the evening of the 21st. The search, as a British national undertaking, ended that evening.

## The people who actually knew

McClintock's two seasons of sledging found the record at Victory Point, a boat and some skeletons. Almost everything else he brought home he was told, through his Danish-Greenlandic interpreter [[person:Carl Petersen]], and the people who told him have names.

On the way west in August 1858 the Fox's officers sat in a tent near Navy Board Inlet with [[person:Noo-luk]] and [[person:A-wah-lah]], the two leading men of Kaparoktolik, who drew charts of the coasts they knew; [[person:Ow-wang-noot]], an old man who had lived at Iglulik in his youth and drew the coast-line to the north; and [[person:A-ra-neet]], who described where the coast ends. On 1 and 2 March 1859 McClintock met four men and then about forty-five people — "from aged people to infants in arms" — at Cape Victoria on the [[place:Boothia Peninsula]], among them [[person:Oo-na-lee]], who knew the coast to [[place:Bellot Strait]] by name although he had never been there, and [[person:Ooblooria]], who turned out to be the same man [[person:James Clark Ross]] had employed as a guide from the Victory thirty years before and who asked after Ross by his Inuktitut name, Agglugga. [[artifact:franklin-search-100|When McClintock asked after another man of that time and got no answer]], Petersen explained that the silence itself was the answer: the man was dead, and they do not speak of the dead. [[artifact:franklin-search-106|A plate in the book shows Inuit hunters mimicking game]] to draw the ship's people towards them — the approach that produced most of what the Fox learned.

The single most valuable thing anyone told him came on **7 May 1859**, at the village near what is now [[place:Uqsuqtuuq]] (Gjoa Haven), from an old woman whose name he did not record. She and a boy had been the last to visit the wreck, in the winter of 1857–58. [[artifact:franklin-search-099|"She said many of the white men dropped by the way as they went to the Great River; that some were buried and some were not."]] Three weeks later McClintock found a man lying face down on a gravel ridge and wrote that it was a melancholy truth she had spoken.

This is the same body of testimony, from the same people, that Rae had gathered at Pelly Bay five years earlier and been abused for repeating. It was accurate then and it is accurate now, and the archaeology of the last seventy years has confirmed it point by point. See [[topic:inuit-oral-history]].

## What closed, and what did not

**The record trail was opened by Inuit testimony, not by searching.** Rae found the answer in April 1854 by asking a question at Pelly Bay. Every subsequent expedition — Anderson, McClintock, [[person:Charles Francis Hall|Hall]], [[person:Frederick Schwatka|Schwatka]] — was following the same testimony, and every one of them confirmed it. The ships and the sledges of 1848–54 covered tens of thousands of miles and found nothing, because the men who knew were not being asked.

**Nothing was closed in 1859.** The Victory Point record accounts for the expedition only to 25 April 1848. Everything after that date rests on Inuit accounts, on bones, and on the two wrecks — Erebus located in 2014 in Wilmot and Crampton Bay, Terror in 2016 in [[place:Terror Bay]], both within a few miles of where Inuit had said they were.

**The search did not stop when the government stopped paying for it.** Hall spent most of two decades with Inuit at [[place:Naujaat]] and in [[place:Frobisher Bay]] collecting testimony (see [[topic:kane-hall-polaris]]), and Schwatka made the longest sledge journey then on record in 1878–80: [[page:schwatka-and-the-last-search]]. The record trail is still open; the Franklin Expedition National Historic Site is co-managed with the Inuit Heritage Trust, and the guardians at Uqsuqtuuq work on the wrecks every summer.
