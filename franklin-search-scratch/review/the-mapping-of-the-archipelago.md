Nobody set out to survey the Canadian Arctic Archipelago. It was surveyed because fifty expeditions spent eleven years looking under every stone in it for 129 men, and the chart is what they had to show for it. This page is about that by-product: who put which coasts on paper, how much there was of it, and how much of it was wrong.

## The arithmetic

The search kept its own accounts. [[artifact:franklin-search-060|M'Dougall printed the totals]] in the appendix to the Resolute's narrative in 1857, under the heading "Land discovered and searched from 1848 to 1854". Geographical miles:

| | searched on foot | new land |
|---|---|---|
| [[person:James Clark Ross]], 1848–49 | 990 | 200 |
| [[person:Horatio Thomas Austin|Austin]], 1850–51 | 6,087 | 1,076 |
| [[person:William Penny|Penny]], 1850–51 | 1,700 | 500 |
| [[topic:mcclure-investigator|M'Clure]], 1850–53 | 2,350 | 1,591 |
| [[person:John Rae|Rae]], 1851 | 1,030 | 617 |
| [[person:Edward Augustus Inglefield|Inglefield]], 1852 | — | 180 (from the ship) |
| [[person:Henry Kellett|Kellett]], 1852–54 | 9,432 | 1,618 |
| **total** | **21,589** | **5,782** |

That is about 25,000 statute miles walked, and 6,600 statute miles of coastline drawn for the first time — most of it by men in harness, on foot, hauling their own food. [[person:Edward Belcher|Belcher's]] division is missing from the table; no aggregate for it appears in his own book.

M'Dougall breaks the Kellett line down on the same sheet: in 1853 alone, out of [[place:Dealy Island]], [[person:Francis Leopold McClintock|McClintock]] laid down 768 geographical miles of new coast, [[person:George Frederick Mecham|Mecham]] 680 and [[person:Richard Vesey Hamilton|Hamilton]] 170 — "Total - - 1618 = 1870 statute miles" — in a single spring, by three sledge parties.

## Who drew what

**[[place:Port Leopold]] and north Somerset, 1848–49.** James Clark Ross's sledges laid down the north and west coasts of Somerset Island as far as about 72° 38′ N. His depot at Whaler Point was still standing and useful in 1850, 1852 and 1859.

**Cornwallis, Bathurst, Melville and the Cape Walker approaches, 1851.** Austin's fifteen sledges did more surveying in ten weeks than any comparable operation before or since. [[artifact:franklin-search-021|McClintock]] ran the south coast of [[place:Melville Island]] to [[place:Winter Harbour]] and Cape Dundas; Bradford took its eastern side; Aldrich followed the west coast of [[place:Bathurst Island]] north to 76° 15′ N and reported the channel there still twenty miles wide; M'Dougall's sledge Beaufort proved [[place:Bathurst Island|Bathurst]] and [[place:Cornwallis Island|Cornwallis]] joined round the head of the bay between them; [[artifact:franklin-search-022|Ommanney and Osborn]] traced the north coast of [[place:Prince of Wales Island]] west to about 103° W; and Browne went south down [[place:Peel Sound]] from [[place:Cape Walker]].

The one look that mattered came on 14 May 1851, when Ommanney and Osborn saw from a table-land "a wide break in the continuation of the land… a channel full twenty-five miles wide" — [[place:McClintock Channel]]. Nobody followed it. The heavy multi-year ice that comes down it is what stopped the Erebus and Terror.

**Wellington Channel and the Grinnell Peninsula, 1851–54.** [[artifact:franklin-search-067|Penny's]] dog sledge and then his whale boat laid down both shores of [[place:Wellington Channel]], [[place:Queens Channel]], and the coast to Cape Sir John Franklin beyond 77° N in 1851. Belcher redrew much of it in 1852–53 from [[place:Northumberland Sound]], correcting Penny's placing of Cape Sir John Franklin and the coast between Cape Osborn and Cape Becher, and adding Exmouth Island, Table Island, Pell Point, the "Land's End of North Cornwall", North Kent, Britannia Cliffs and the group he called the Victoria Archipelago. Almost all the [[place:Grinnell Peninsula]] is theirs.

**The western islands, 1853–54.** Kellett's division out of Dealy Island put [[place:Prince Patrick Island]], Eglinton Island and the northern coast of Melville Island on the chart — McClintock's furthest was Point McClintock at 77° 23′ N, 118° 20′ W. This is the emptiest quarter of the archipelago and it was mapped by men walking.

**Boothia, Victoria Island and the mainland coast, 1851–54.** Rae's work was the most efficient of all: 617 geographical miles of new coast in 1851 for 1,030 miles walked, along the south and east shores of Victoria Island. In 1854 he closed the last gap in the continental coastline and showed that King William Land is an island — which is the fact that makes the North-West Passage navigable at all, and the fact Franklin's orders had not had.

**[[place:Bellot Strait]], 1852.** [[artifact:franklin-search-027|Kennedy and Bellot]] found the strait, and so found the true northern edge of the continent at [[place:Murchison Promontory]]. See [[page:prince-albert-and-bellot]].

**[[place:King William Island]] and Prince of Wales Island, 1859.** McClintock's three sledge divisions out of [[place:Port Kennedy]] completed the circuit of King William Island — the last important coastline in the region to be drawn — and Allen Young laid down the western side of Prince of Wales Island. See [[page:closing-the-record-trail]].

## What they got wrong

A chart made by men in a hurry, looking for something else, is not a survey.

- Belcher found himself "sailing over a great deal of hard land of the published charts, without injury to the Pioneer or ourselves." M'Dougall, drifting in the pack in 1853, corrected the coast between Consett Head and Point Griffiths and shortened Byam Martin Island by eleven miles.
- [[person:William Kennedy|Kennedy]] took the south shore of his own new strait for an island and named it Levesque Island, went home believing Somerset and Prince of Wales Land were joined, and concluded — wrongly, and with real consequences for where people looked next — that Franklin could not have passed south of Cape Walker.
- Penny's two meridian altitudes at Point Surprise, taken eight weeks apart, differed by four miles, which he said in print was to be regretted.
- Whole coastlines were sketched from a hill-top at twenty or thirty miles and drawn in as though walked.

[[artifact:franklin-search-077|Arrowsmith's chart of 1860]], drawn for the Royal Geographical Society while the argument was still warm, is the best single summary of what the search had settled and what it had not; [[artifact:franklin-search-115|the folding map at the end of McClintock's narrative]] does the same job for the western archipelago. The land was re-mapped in the twentieth century from the air and then from orbit. What has not been corrected everywhere is what lies under the water.

## What Inuit knew already

None of this was new country to the people living in it. The searchers' best geography came repeatedly from Inuit informants, and their maps were sometimes copies of Inuit maps: Iligliuk drew the coast of Melville Peninsula and the strait through it for Parry thirty years earlier, the Inughuit man Erasmus York could sketch the coast north of Cape York as far as Whale Sound from tradition alone, and the search's whole record trail was opened by a conversation at Pelly Bay in 1854. See [[page:inuit-drawn-maps]] and [[topic:inuit-oral-history]].

The names on the modern chart record the searchers and almost none of the people who lived there. The Inuit Heritage Trust's place-names programme has been restoring Inuktitut names since the 1990s; the ship's chart carries both where that work has been done.

## What this means on the bridge

Two practical things follow for anyone working here now.

**The skeleton of the chart in these channels is sledge work.** The coasts of Bathurst, Cornwallis, Prince of Wales, Prince Patrick, Melville and the Grinnell Peninsula were first drawn by men walking them with a sextant and a pocket chronometer between 1851 and 1854, and the sequence of headlands and the names on them are still theirs.

**The soundings are much thinner than the coastlines.** Outside the designated shipping corridors a great deal of the archipelago is still charted from scattered lead-line and single-beam soundings, some of them a century old or more, with wide unsurveyed gaps between the lines; the source diagrams on a modern chart of these waters show it plainly. The coastline on the screen is generally good. The depth between the coastlines often is not. That is why a ship like this one, running multibeam along her track, is still adding to the chart in the same way and for much the same reason as the sledge parties did.
