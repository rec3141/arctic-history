Twenty years after [[person:Francis Leopold McClintock|McClintock]] came home, an American cavalry lieutenant went back to King William Island to look for the papers of the [[topic:franklin-1845|Franklin expedition]] — not the ships, not the bodies, the paper. He did not find any, and establishing that none survived is what the journey is for. It was also, by a long way, the longest sledge journey anyone had then made.

## What sent it north

The prompt was a story told by Thomas F. Barry, master of a New Bedford whaler, who said an Inuk chief had described a cairn on King William Island holding papers, and that silver spoons had since been found near it. On that, the American Geographical Society raised the money and the New York Herald put up the rest.

Five men sailed from New York in Barry's whaling schooner Eothen on **19 June 1878**: Lieutenant [[person:Frederick Schwatka]] of the Third United States Cavalry, [[person:William Henry Gilder]] of the Herald as second in command, [[person:Heinrich Klutschak]] as artist and surveyor, [[person:Frank Melms]], and [[person:Ebierbing|Joseph Ebierbing]] — "Esquimau Joe" — who had guided [[person:Charles Francis Hall]] for fifteen years and was going back to the ground a third time. They reached Depot Island, on the west shore of Hudson Bay, on 7 August, and built [[artifact:franklin-1845-024|a camp on the mainland nearby]] which they named Camp Daly, at 63° 51′ N, 90° 26′ 15″ W.

Barry's story collapsed there. In January 1879 Gilder and Klutschak sledged to Marble Island and put the question to Captain Potter of the Abbie Bradford: the spoon had come from [[person:Nutargeark]], had been given by him to [[person:Sinuksook]]'s wife, and by her to Potter. Nobody had said anything about a cairn full of papers. "In this crucible of fact the famous spoon melted. So far as Captain Barry and his clews were concerned, we had come on a fool's errand."

What saved the expedition was that Nutargeark had something better: his own father had opened a cairn on the north shore of Washington Bay and taken from it a tin box with a written paper in it. That was worth going for.

## The route — and what it was not

The expedition left Camp Daly on **1 April 1879** with three sledges, forty-two dogs, about 5,000 lb, four qallunaat and thirteen Inuit, and roughly a month's civilised rations for seventeen people. Everything after that they killed: 522 caribou, [[artifact:franklin-1845-032|besides musk oxen]], bears and seals.

The line they took is often described wrongly, so it is worth setting down as Gilder printed it. They went inland from Camp Daly up the **Connery River** and the **Lorillard River**, across unmapped country north-west, and down the **Hayes River** — a river nobody had charted, which they named for the President and followed some ninety miles — into Cockburn Bay on the east shore of Back's (Great Fish) River. They did **not** go by Chesterfield Inlet, which appears in Gilder only as a geographical reference; and Lake Franklin and Lake Garry, which are sometimes put on this route, are never mentioned in his book at all.

From Back's River they worked to Montreal Island, to Richardson Point and Starvation Cove on the Adelaide Peninsula, across Simpson Strait to [[place:Cape John Herschel|Cape Herschel]] in mid-June, up the west coast of King William Island past Erebus Bay and Franklin Point to Cape Felix, and back. They camped near Gladman Point through the autumn, started south on 1 November, and came down Back's River in the worst cold of the journey — on **3 January 1880** the thermometer read −70°F in the morning and "at five o'clock in the afternoon the extraordinary mark of −71 degrees." They struck the Connery River on 2 March and reached **Depot Island on 4 March 1880** — not Camp Daly, which had been given up as a base a year before.

The whole journey was, in Gilder's accounting, "eleven months and twenty days" and "2,819 geographical, or 3,251 statute miles," most of it over ground no outsider had mapped, and "the only one ever made through the entire course of an Arctic winter."

## What they found

Bones, mostly, and the ground itself. Two graves between Franklin Point and Collinson Inlet. Crozier's landing camp, with cooking stoves, clothing, and an open grave holding blue cloth from a heavy overcoat and canvas stitched "as though the body had been incased as if for burial at sea." The grave of Lieutenant John Irving of the Terror, identified by a silver medal Klutschak found on a stone at its foot — the Royal Naval College's second mathematics prize, awarded to John Irving at midsummer 1830 — with "a figured silk pocket-handkerchief, neatly folded" under the head; his remains were carried out and buried in Edinburgh. At the boat place on the south side of [[artifact:franklin-1845-039|Erebus Bay]], the boat McClintock had seen, its surviving clinkered boards 28 ft 6 in. long, and parts of four skeletons, reburied the next day.

And one piece of paper, found by [[person:Toolooahelek]], Toolooah's wife, in the ruins of Hobson's cairn at the Victory Point camp. It was McClintock's own pencil copy of the Crozier record, left there in 1859. It is the only document the Schwatka search recovered, and it was not Franklin's.

Schwatka dug a trench four feet wide and twenty feet long north from the cairn looking for McClintock's 1859 deposit and found nothing. The tall cairns near Cape Felix "had no records in them."

## What the Inuit said about the papers

The expedition's real work was interviewing, and Gilder names the witnesses.

[[person:Ikinnelikpatolok]], the old Ookjoolik, met on Back's River on 15 May 1879, had as a boy shaken hands with ten white men in a boat under a leader his people remembered as Tos-ard-e-roak — [[person:Ebierbing|Joe]] identified the name as Back. Later he had gone aboard a big ship frozen in the ice about five miles west of Grant Point, and found a dead man in a bunk; his people cut a hole in her side and she filled and sank the next summer. "They also saw books on board, and left them there."

[[person:Peowat]], his son-in-law, had opened a stone monument on Montreal Island and taken a pocket-knife, scissors and fish-hooks from it. "He saw no papers anywhere about it."

[[person:Seeuteetuar]], an old Netsilingmiut man at Richardson Point, had seen skeletons at the water-line in the cove to the west, and "books and papers scattered around among the rocks along the shore and back from the beach."

[[person:Ahlangyah]], a Netsilingmiut woman of about fifty-five and the best witness of the whole search, had met ten white men dragging a sledge with a boat on it in Washington Bay and camped five days beside them, and had seen the tent full of dead bodies at the head of Terror Bay the following spring: "There were knives, forks, spoons, watches, many books, clothing, blankets, and such things. The books were not taken notice of."

[[person:Ogzeuckjeuwock]], who had been a boy of about twelve at the Starvation Cove boat, gave the passage the whole expedition turned on: "he said he saw books at the boat place in a tin case, about two feet long and a foot square, which was fastened, and they broke it open. The case was full. Written and printed books were shown him, and he said they were like the printed ones." What became of them: "Some of the books were taken home for the children to play with, and finally torn and lost, and others lay around among the rocks until carried away by the wind and lost or buried beneath the sand."

[[person:Ockarnawole]], who had walked the Victory Point ground five years before, answered the same question the other way: "Saw no books."

That is the answer. The records were carried as far as Starvation Cove in a sealed tin case, the case was broken open there, and the contents were destroyed within a generation. McClintock had thought the papers had perished; Schwatka established it. Gilder puts the claim plainly: "Next in importance to their recovery must be considered the knowledge of their irrecoverable loss."

## Two things this page has to say plainly

**Puhtoorak is not one of Gilder's witnesses.** The Ookjoolik testimony about the ship that sank west of Grant Point is often credited to Puhtoorak. In the printed narrative of Schwatka's search the old Ookjoolik who gave it is Ikinnelikpatolok. Puhtoorak's version of the same story belongs to Klutschak's separate German narrative, *Als Eskimo unter den Eskimos* (Vienna, 1881), which is not among the books used here; a citation of Puhtoorak should go to Klutschak. Whether the two men are the same person under different spellings is not settled.

**The expedition was thirteen Inuit and four qallunaat.** The roster Gilder prints names them all: [[person:Toolooah]], the dog-driver who killed most of the 522 caribou and brought the party off rotten ice more than once; his wife [[person:Toolooahelek]]; [[person:Equeesik]] and [[person:Kutcheenuark]]; [[person:Owanork]], about thirteen, who found the cache below Cape Maria Louisa; [[person:Mit-colelee]]; [[person:Ishnark]], Joe's father-in-law, who had been on Hall's King William Land journey a decade earlier; [[person:Karleko]]; [[person:Koomana]], about thirteen, Gilder's servant; [[person:Neepshark]], Joe's wife; and, engaged on Back's River, [[person:Narleyow]] and [[person:Innokpizookzook]]. Behind them stood the Iwillik and Kinnepatoo people of Depot Island who kept the stores and fed the party when it came back starving: [[person:Armow]], [[person:Te-wort]], [[person:Ik-omer]], [[person:Eeglee-leock]], [[person:Nanook]] and others. Gilder uses the same name, Toolooah, for three different people — his own dog-driver, a Netsilingmiut man of about forty-five who pointed out on a map where the ship had gone down eight miles west of Grant Point, and an aged Netsilingmiut woman met near Sherman Inlet who had been with the party that found the boat and the skeletons at Starvation Cove.

When the party broke up in 1880, Toolooah was to have sailed to the United States and the elders of his people forbade it; rather than say goodbye he hid, weeping, in Armow's boat. Schwatka watched from the bow and said one thing on the way out: "I was not prepared for this."
