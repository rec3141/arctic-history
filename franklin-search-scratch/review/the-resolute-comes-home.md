Four of the Royal Navy's five Arctic ships were standing empty in the ice of Barrow Strait and Wellington Channel in the autumn of 1854 (see [[page:belchers-five-ships]]). Their officers and crews came home in the Phoenix and the Talbot in the first week of October. What happened afterwards is the strangest ending any Franklin search had: a court martial that acquitted everybody without praising the man in command, and a ship that sailed a thousand miles by herself and was given back as a present.

## The court martial, October 1854

A court martial sat aboard HMS Waterloo at Sheerness on 18–20 October 1854 to inquire into the loss of the ships. It took the abandonments in turn.

[[person:Henry Kellett]] was honourably acquitted, having pleaded the written orders produced from [[person:Edward Belcher]], and had his sword returned with a compliment. Robert McClure, whose [[vessel:HMS Investigator]] had been left in [[place:Mercy Bay]] the year before, was acquitted too; the Admiral, restoring his sword, said that "the court are of opinion that your conduct throughout your arduous exertions, has been most meritorious and praiseworthy" (see [[topic:mcclure-investigator]]).

Belcher was acquitted as well — the loss of the ships was covered by the Admiralty's own withdrawal order of 28 April, which had reached Beechey Island the day after he got there — but the finding added that he should have consulted Kellett if circumstances had allowed. [[person:George Frederick M'Dougall]], the Resolute's master, was in the court, and what he records of that moment is one flat sentence: "the president then returned Sir Edward Belcher his sword, and the court broke up." No compliment. Belcher never went to sea again, though he rose by seniority to admiral.

## The ship that came back

Then the Arctic delivered its own verdict on the argument.

[[vessel:HMS Resolute]] had been left on 15 May 1854 in latitude 74° 41′ N, longitude 101° 22′ W, battened down, with her stores and instruments taken out and Belcher's instruction that she should be fit to re-occupy in July or August. She was not re-occupied. She drifted.

On 10 September 1855, sixteen months later, Captain James Buddington of the New London whaler [[vessel:George Henry]] — herself beset and drifting, with a crew of seventeen — sighted a ship about twenty miles from Cape Mercy, off the east coast of Qikiqtaaluk (Baffin Island). It was the Resolute, derelict, upright and undamaged, more than a thousand miles from where she had been left, and afloat exactly as Kellett had said she would be. [[artifact:franklin-search-023|The last leg of her track]] is a straight line between two points on the map, because nobody was aboard to take a position.

Buddington divided his men, took the Resolute over himself with no chronometer, an untrustworthy compass and a rough outline chart, started for home on 20 October and made the New London light on 24 December 1855. Congress then did something handsome: it bought the ship from the salvors for 40,000 dollars, had her taken to a United States navy yard and repaired and refitted with every care — M'Dougall, who went over her afterwards, found her fittings restored to their original positions — and in December 1856 she was sailed across and given back to Queen Victoria, who came aboard at Cowes. It was a deliberate piece of diplomacy in a bad year between the two countries.

The Resolute was never sent to sea again. She was broken up in 1879, and the Queen had a desk made from her timbers and sent it to the President of the United States in 1880. It has stood in the Oval Office, on and off, ever since — the one object from the Franklin search that most people have seen without knowing what it is.

## What it settled

Three things, none of which was Franklin.

**Kellett was right and Belcher was wrong about the ice.** Belcher had written that he was confident the ships "would cease to float before the autumn gales conclude" and that "no hope of their southern drift can be entertained." The Resolute floated for sixteen months and drifted a thousand miles south and east.

**The Navy was finished with the Arctic.** No British government expedition went back into the archipelago for twenty years, until [[topic:nares-greely|Nares]] in 1875. Everything between 1854 and 1859 — Anderson's canoes down the Back River, [[person:Jane Franklin|Lady Franklin's]] Fox — was paid for by the Hudson's Bay Company or by private subscription. See [[page:closing-the-record-trail]].

**The search's own losses were small, and its ships' losses were not.** Six vessels were lost looking for Franklin against his two, all six within about two hundred miles of [[place:Beechey Island]]: the Investigator in Mercy Bay, the Resolute and Intrepid in Barrow Strait, the Assistance and Pioneer in Wellington Channel, and the transport [[vessel:Breadalbane]], crushed off Beechey Island in fifteen minutes on 21 August 1853. Of those six, one came back on her own.
