The 1819 expedition was the reply to the argument of 1818. The Admiralty gave
[[person:William Edward Parry]], aged twenty-eight, the bomb vessel
[[vessel:HMS Hecla]] of 375 tons and the gun-brig [[vessel:HMS Griper]] of 180
tons under Lieutenant [[person:Matthew Liddon]], and sent him back to
[[place:Lancaster Sound]] to see whether the Croker Mountains were there.
[[person:Edward Sabine]] came again as astronomer; Alexander Fisher was
assistant surgeon of the Hecla and kept his own published journal
([[source:fisher1821journal]]); Frederick Beechey and
[[person:Henry Parkyns Hoppner]] were lieutenants; a midshipman named James
Clark Ross, nephew of [[person:John Ross]], was aboard. They sailed in May 1819.

## Through the mountains, and west

Parry pushed through the middle ice of Baffin Bay in the last week of July,
landed at [[place:Possession Bay]] on 31 July — where Ross's flagstaff of the
previous year was still standing — and entered Lancaster Sound on 1 August. The
mountains were not there. He ran west without ceremony, naming as he went —
Prince Regent Inlet, Barrow Strait, Wellington Channel, Cornwallis Island,
Bathurst Island, Melville Island — and on the evening of 4 September 1819, in
latitude 74° 44' 20", the ships crossed the meridian of 110° west:

> by which His Majesty's ships, under my orders, became entitled to the sum of
> five thousand pounds, being the reward offered by the King's order in
> council.

The men named the bluff headland they had just passed Bounty Cape, and Parry
put the name on the chart. See [[artifact:ross-parry-022|the excerpt]]. He got
about sixty miles further west before the ice stopped him. In one week Parry
had made more westing than the entire preceding two centuries of search. The
coast he was running past is in the book as a set of
[[artifact:ross-parry-122|profile views of Cape Warrender and the north shore
of Lancaster Sound]], drawn by Beechey for pilots who would come after —
[[artifact:ross-parry-124|Prince Leopold's Islands and Cape York]],
[[artifact:ross-parry-123|Hobhouse Inlet and Cape Fellfoot]],
[[artifact:ross-parry-125|Cape Hotham, Caswall's Tower and Cape Ricketts]],
[[artifact:ross-parry-121|Byam Martin Island]] — and as the two halves of the
large folding chart, [[artifact:ross-parry-120|east]] and
[[artifact:ross-parry-119|west]]. Earlier, off the Baffin Island coast, they
had met people of the inlet the chart calls the River Clyde, and Parry had that
engraved too: [[artifact:ross-parry-128|Esquimaux of the Inlet called the River
Clyde]].

## Nearly losing seven men, 10-13 September 1819

Before the winter began there was a warning. At daylight on 10 September a
shooting party from the Griper — Mr Fife and six men — went inland after tracks
of caribou and muskoxen with a small quantity of provisions and no plan, and
lost themselves within hours. Liddon reported them missing on the morning of
the 11th. A search party sent after them lost itself too in thick snow and came
back guided in by rockets. Parry had the Hecla's fore-royal-mast carried four
or five miles inland and set up as a flagstaff with a large ensign, then sent
out parties planting pikes with bottled directions at intervals.

The last of the men came aboard at ten in the evening on 13 September, after
ninety-one hours out and three nights in the first wintry weather of the
season. Almost the whole party were exhausted; several were severely
frostbitten in toes and fingers; none died. The wind rose to a hard gale at
half past eleven that night with the thermometer at 15°, and Parry, writing
that they could not have survived it, named the headland west of the ships
Cape Providence.

## Cutting into Winter Harbour, 24-26 September 1819

On 23 September Parry examined two harbours on the south coast of Melville
Island. Fife's Harbour had a bar with too little water on it; the westernmost
had five to six fathoms, and a continuous new floe four or five inches thick
over the whole of it. He chose the deep one and set every officer and man in
both ships to sawing.

Two parallel lines were marked out a little more than the breadth of the larger
ship apart, cut with ice-saws, then cross-cut at ten to twenty foot intervals
and the rectangles halved diagonally so the pieces could be floated out. The
seamen, "who are always fond of doing things in their own way," rigged boats'
sails on the floating slabs and let a northerly breeze carry them out of the
cut. By the second day the lower part of the canal had frozen hard behind them
and the pieces had to be sunk under the floe instead — officers standing on one
end of a block while parties hauled the other end under, several of them up to
their knees in water with the thermometer at 12°.

The canal was finished at noon on the 26th, in a morning that began at 6°. Its
whole length was four thousand and eighty-two yards, nearly two miles and one
third, and the average thickness of the ice was seven inches. At a quarter past
three the ships reached their winter quarters and the event was hailed with
three cheers from both companies. They lay in five fathoms, a cable's length
from the beach on the northwestern side of the harbour, which Parry named
[[place:Winter Harbour]]. See [[artifact:ross-parry-012|the plate of the
cutting-in]] and [[artifact:ross-parry-013|Westall's view of the two ships in
the harbour]]; the following June the officers surveyed the whole anchorage,
soundings, leading marks and all
([[artifact:ross-parry-126|Survey of Winter Harbour, June 1820]]).

Near the southwest point of the harbour stands a block of sandstone "somewhat
resembling the roof of a house." Mr Fisher engraved the ships' names on it.
Parry's Rock is still there, and is a National Historic Site of Canada.

## Ten months in the ice

Nobody in the Royal Navy had deliberately wintered a ship in the Arctic before.
What Parry improvised became the template. The upper decks were housed over
with wagon-cloths and banked with snow. The men ran laps round the deck for
exercise every morning. Rations were kept full, with lemon juice and sugar
served out daily under an officer's eye, plus spruce beer, pickles, preserved
soups, currants and gooseberries. Every man's gums were inspected on a schedule.
The surgeons' own account of how it went is the last of the appendices,
[[artifact:ross-parry-118|Remarks on the State of Health and Disease on Board
the Hecla and Griper]].

The enemy turned out to be damp rather than cold. Breath and cooking condensed
on the ships' sides and froze in the bed-places, and it was an officer who broke
first: in early January 1820 Mr Scallon, the Hecla's gunner, came down with
scurvy, and the surgeon traced it to bedding soaked through against the hull.
Parry started growing mustard and cress in shallow boxes of mould laid along
the stove pipe in his own cabin, cropping them on the sixth or seventh day and
issuing nearly an ounce of salad a day to the scorbutic patients — in a ship
where the coal ration would not let the fire burn overnight.

For the dark he prescribed occupation. From 5 November 1819 the officers put on
a play on the Hecla's deck once a fortnight, Beechey elected stage manager,
Parry taking a part himself because "an example of cheerfulness ... was not the
least essential part of my duty." The stock of playbooks ran to one or two
volumes, so the officers wrote a Christmas musical entertainment of their own
about the voyage. Plays were performed with the thermometer below zero on the
stage. In parallel Sabine edited a weekly newspaper, the *North Georgia Gazette
and Winter Chronicle*, filled with anonymous contributions from the officers of
both ships; it was printed in London in 1821 and is here as
[[source:anon1821north]]. See [[artifact:ross-parry-023|the excerpt]].

The winter's worst day was 24 February 1820, with the thermometer between -43°
and -44°. At a quarter past ten in the morning, while the men were running
round the decks, the observatory house on shore was found to be on fire. Both
ships' companies ran up, pulled the roof off with ropes and knocked in the
sides to get snow onto the flames, and had it out in three quarters of an hour,
just short of the clocks and transit instrument. Sixteen men went onto the sick
lists; noses and cheeks went white in five minutes and the surgeons walked the
fire line rubbing faces with snow. John Smith of the Royal Artillery, Sabine's
servant, ran out of the burning house carrying the dipping needle without time
to put his gloves on. When his hands were plunged into a basin of cold water it
froze at the surface. He lost "a part of four fingers on one hand and three on
the other," so that Sabine's magnetic instrument survived intact and its keeper
did not.

One man died in ten months: William Scott, boatswain's mate of the Hecla, who
had gone delirious at the end of June and died at two in the morning on
30 June 1820. Parry had the body opened, against the seamen's prejudice, and
recorded the surgeon's finding that the disease had "no immediate connexion
with our present peculiar situation." Scott was buried on 2 July on level
ground a hundred yards from the beach, attended by every officer and man in
both ships, with a headstone carved by Mr Fisher.

## The winter as a measurement

The ten months at Winter Harbour were also the first long instrumental record
kept anywhere in the Canadian Arctic archipelago, and most of it is in this
volume. The monthly abstracts of the Hecla's meteorological journal run from
[[artifact:ross-parry-038|July 1819]] at sea through the
[[artifact:ross-parry-042|December]] and
[[artifact:ross-parry-043|February]] of the wintering to
[[artifact:ross-parry-048|September 1820]] on the way home — air temperature in
the shade, barometer, prevailing winds and weather, day by day. The ships' chronometers were rated from the
[[artifact:ross-parry-050|Calton Hill Observatory in Edinburgh]] through
[[artifact:ross-parry-055|nearly seven months of winter at Winter Harbour]] and
back to [[artifact:ross-parry-057|London in December 1820]]; against them Parry
set six hundred and ninety-two sets of lunar distances, six thousand eight
hundred and sixty-two individual observations in all
([[artifact:ross-parry-073|the summary is here]]).
There is a [[artifact:ross-parry-113|tide table]] kept through 1820, an
[[artifact:ross-parry-117|account of pendulum experiments]] to measure gravity
at that latitude, and an [[artifact:ross-parry-090|appendix of magnetic
observations]] — dip, variation and force — taken so close to the magnetic pole
that the compass had become an instrument requiring correction before it could
be believed. Sabine's readings at the fixed shore station are
[[artifact:ross-parry-096|here]]. Two hundred years on, this is a usable
baseline, which is the unglamorous reason a ship like this one still has cause
to open the book. What it all came to, in Parry's own summary of the anchorage:
latitude 74° 47' 19".36 N by the mean of thirty-nine meridian altitudes,
longitude 110° 48' 29".2 W, magnetic dip 88° 43', variation 127° 47' 50" east.

## Out, and home

The ships left Winter Harbour on 1 August 1820 and worked west along Melville
Island's south coast in heavy old ice; from the 17th to the 23rd they were
beset, a situation Parry had drawn
([[artifact:ross-parry-127|Situation of H.M. Ships Hecla and Griper, August
1820]]). In the middle of the month they lay at latitude 74° 26' 25" N,
longitude 113° 46' 43" W by chronometer, off Cape Dundas — "the westernmost
point to which the navigation of the Polar Sea to the northward of the American
continent has yet been carried." Parry's chapter heading rounds the voyage's
farthest to 113° 48' 22"; the record stood for a generation either way. Land was
in sight to the southwest, which he named Banks Land. Between it and Melville
Island lay ice he correctly judged impassable: that is M'Clure Strait, still
among the hardest water in the archipelago and navigable only in exceptional
seasons. He turned for home and reached England in November 1820.

Parry had settled three things at once: that Lancaster Sound was open, that the
route west was blocked by multi-year ice pouring in from the polar basin, and
that a British ship's company could sit out an Arctic winter and come home
almost whole. The third proposition is why later expeditions kept trying — and,
in the case of [[topic:franklin-1845]], why so few people worried early enough.
[[topic:mcclure-investigator]] came at the same blockage from the other side
thirty years later and lost a ship to it.
