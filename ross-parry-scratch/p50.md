In March 1822 an Iglulingmiut woman in her twenties sat down in a cabin on
[[vessel:HMS Fury]], at [[place:Winter Island]], and drew for the Royal Navy a
map of a coast none of them had seen. It was substantially right. Parry sailed
north on it the following summer and found the strait she had put at the top
of it.

## Who she was

[[person:Iligliuk]] was born on Amitioke, an island she told Parry was sixteen
days' journey to the north — Amitturmiut country, near [[place:Iglulik]]. She
was married to [[person:Okotook]], whom Parry describes as "a fine active manly
fellow of about two and thirty"; [[person:Togolat]] was Okotook's sister. She
came to the ships from the village of five snow houses found two miles off on
1 February 1822 (see [[page:two-winters-among-the-iglulingmiut]]).

She learned faster than anyone else in either community. Within weeks the
quartermasters at the Fury's gangway had stopped thinking of refusing entrance
to "the wise woman," as they called her, and Parry writes that "whenever any
explanation was necessary between the Esquimaux and us, Iligliuk was sent for
quite as an interpreter; information was chiefly obtained through her." She was,
in practice, the expedition's interpreter, ethnographic informant and
cartographer at once, and no one gave her a rank or a wage.

## The charts

Parry describes the process carefully, because he wanted his readers to judge
"the kind and degree of dependence that is to be placed in geographical notices
thus obtained."

**First attempt, 4 March 1822.** Parry laid down on a large sheet the coast he
already knew — Repulse Bay, Lyon Inlet, Winter Island — and asked Iligliuk to
continue it northward. Drawing the known coast first was, in his view, an
indispensable precaution: it gave her a scale and an orientation to work into
rather than a blank page, so that she would be assisted instead of confused. She
took a pencil and carried the line on, naming the places as she went, until the
sheet ran out; another was tacked on, and another, until ten or twelve sheets
lay across the table and Winter Island — *Ne-yu-ning Eit-du-a*, as Parry printed
it in [[artifact:ross-parry-281|his list of Iglulingmiut place names]] — had gone
off the far end.

**Chart No. 1.** Within a week other people drew charts too, mostly at the
request of Lyon and Mr Griffiths. No two much resembled each other, and most
were poor where the officers could check them. The exception was one Iligliuk
drew for Lyon, engraved in Parry's book at about one-twelfth of the original's
scale: [[artifact:ross-parry-011|Eskimaux Chart No. 1]]. Its small circles are
sleeping places a day's journey apart and its dotted line a sledge track — it is
a travel chart, drawn in days rather than degrees, which is precisely why its
proportions did not satisfy a naval surveyor. Parry noticed that it made the
bearing from Repulse Bay to Winter Island about the same as that from Winter
Island to Amitioke, "though they are in fact exactly at right angles."

**The decisive chart.** So Parry tried again, smaller, and did something rather
good. He had by now learned the Inuktitut words for the four cardinal points. He
ruled them on the paper as lines, laid down the known coast, and made Iligliuk
"box the compass" repeatedly until she was fluent in his convention — then asked
her to draw the rest, and to do it *mikhee*, small. What followed is one of the
few passages where Parry's prose gives way completely:

> never were the tracings of a pencil watched with more eager solicitude.

She drew the continental coast running nearly north from Winter Island rather
than east. Then, without lifting the pencil from the paper, she brought it round
to the westward and afterwards to the south-southwest, to within three or four
days' journey of Repulse Bay. The land on the far side she called Akkoolee,
inhabited by numerous people. Halfway between it and Repulse Bay she put a large
lake with streams running to the sea on each side, where her people went every
summer for fish of the salmon kind, with caribou on the banks. Westward of
Akkoolee, from hills she described as high, there was nothing but one
wide-extended sea.

Parry then set her a trap. He asked her to continue the coast south of Akkoolee,
to see whether she would invent a Wager River he knew was there. She dropped the
pencil and said she knew no more about it. A few days later [[person:Ewerat]]
drew a chart corresponding with hers in every material point.

## What she was right about

In outline, this: Melville Peninsula is a peninsula; a strait runs through the
top of it; the coast then turns west and south; there is a large body of water
beyond. All correct. The strait is [[place:Fury and Hecla Strait]], which the
ships reached in the summer of 1822 exactly where the chart put it — and could
not enter, because it was full of fixed ice, as it is most years still. What
they brought back from it were Lyon's coastal profiles of a passage they never
made: [[artifact:ross-parry-244|Cape Penrhyn and the mouth of the strait]] and
[[artifact:ross-parry-245|Liddon Island and the Narrows]]. The "wide-extended
sea" to the west is the Gulf of Boothia. The refusal to draw south of Akkoolee
marked the honest edge of her people's knowledge.

Set her outline against the volume's own
[[artifact:ross-parry-246|General Chart of the whole voyage]], engraved two
years later from the ships' surveys, and the correspondence is the point: the
shape of the Melville Peninsula on the Admiralty chart is the shape she drew.

There are two ways to read this and only one of them is right. Parry read the
errors of proportion as the measure of what such information was worth. But the
distortions in Chart No. 1 are what you get when travel time is converted to
distance on paper, and they largely vanished as soon as Parry supplied a compass
convention — which is to say the geography was in her head and only the
projection had to be negotiated. The 1822 season's plan was made on her
information.

## Afterwards

Parry is unsparing about what the attention did. "Iligliuk in February and
Iligliuk in April were confessedly very different persons." As the presents and
the deference accumulated she came, he complains, to consider admission to the
ships "no longer as an indulgence but a right," ceased to return the slightest
acknowledgment for any kindness, and became "listless and inattentive in
unravelling the meaning of our questions." He allows that a "too sudden accession
of good fortune" would do the same to any "child of Adam from the equator to the
poles," which is more self-awareness than most of his contemporaries managed, and
then goes on complaining.

The plainer reading is that a woman who had made herself indispensable stopped
performing gratitude for it. She had been paid in needles and beads for
navigational intelligence that redirected a Royal Navy expedition; when the
officers wanted more, the price went up. Parry wrote of "the superior
understanding of this extraordinary woman" and never quite worked out what to do
with the fact. The same stretch of the journal that calls her spoiled also
records that he often heard other officers say what he agreed with: that for
Iligliuk alone, of all the women there, they felt a kind of respect they did not
extend to anyone else in the camp.

Her name is on the engraving — "Drawn by Iligliuk at Winter Island 1822. The
Original in the possession of Capn. Lyon." — which makes her one of the first
named Inuit cartographers whose work was published in Europe, alongside the maps
later given to Hall and to Rae by Inuit informants
([[topic:kane-hall-polaris]], [[topic:inuit-oral-history]]). Iglulik and Naujaat
are still there; so are the routes she drew, which are the routes people
travelled.
