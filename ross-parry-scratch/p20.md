The Admiralty's 1818 expedition had two objects: find the entrance to a
Northwest Passage, and settle whether William Baffin's 1616 chart of Baffin
Bay — quietly dropped from the maps as a fabrication — was true. It settled
the second question emphatically and got the first one badly wrong.

Two whalers were bought and strengthened: the [[vessel:Isabella]] of 385 tons
under [[person:John Ross]], and the [[vessel:Alexander]] of 252 tons under
Lieutenant [[person:William Edward Parry]]. [[person:Edward Sabine]] of the
Royal Artillery came as astronomer. They sailed from the Thames in April 1818
and were home in November. The route is
[[artifact:ross-parry-001|reconstructed here from the marginal positions in
Ross's own narrative]], and the shipwrights' work on the Isabella is in the
book too, as a [[artifact:ross-parry-200|profile of her bow and midship
section]] showing how far the hull was doubled and strengthened for ice.

## Meeting the Inughuit, 10 August 1818

Working north up the Greenland coast, on 10 August the ships were beset in
latitude 75° 55' N, longitude 65° 32' W, off [[place:Cape York]], when eight
sledges appeared and halted a mile off. The expedition carried
[[person:John Sacheuse]], a young West Greenlander who had reached Leith as a
stowaway two years earlier, learned English there, and volunteered now to go
out onto the ice alone and unarmed with a white flag and a bundle of presents.

A chasm in the ice, not passable without a plank, separated the two parties.
Sacheuse tried the Humooke dialect and found it close enough. The first man
drew a knife from his boot and told him to go away — "I can kill you."
Sacheuse threw an English knife across, and they picked it up. Then, said
Ross, they pointed at the ships and asked
[[artifact:ross-parry-030|what great creatures those were]]: did they come
from the sun or the moon? Sacheuse said they were houses made of wood. "No,"
they answered, "they are alive, we have seen them move their wings." A plank
was laid over the chasm, and two of the sledges were drawn across it.

These were the Inughuit of northwest Greenland, perhaps two hundred people,
cut off from the rest of Greenland and from the rest of the world's knowledge
of them; Ross called them "Arctic Highlanders," and the book carries
[[artifact:ross-parry-208|a full-length plate under that title]]. They had
iron, hammered cold from the Cape York meteorites, and no wood to speak of.
See [[topic:greenland-inughuit]] for what became of them and their meteoric
iron. Further south the ships had already met West Greenlanders, one of whom
sat for [[artifact:ross-parry-204|a portrait of an inhabitant of the west
coast of Greenland]].

Sacheuse painted the encounter himself, and the picture was engraved for
Ross's book: it is here both as the
[[artifact:ross-parry-207|plate in the volume]] and as the
[[artifact:ross-parry-010|separately published print]]. It is among the
earliest published works by a Greenlandic Inuk artist. Sacheuse died at
Edinburgh in 1819, aged about twenty-two, before the argument over the rest of
the voyage had properly begun and before Parry's second expedition sailed.
The full passage is at
[[artifact:ross-parry-020|Ross's account of the first communication]].

## The Crimson Cliffs, 17 August 1818

A week later, running along the same coast, the ships passed cliffs whose snow
was stained [[artifact:ross-parry-032|a deep crimson colour]]. Bird droppings
were ruled out on the spot, because thousands of birds were sitting on clean
snow and ice all around. At two in the afternoon Ross sent a boat with Mr Ross,
midshipman, and Mr Beverley, assistant surgeon, to bring off some of the snow;
the party found the cliffs climbable where they landed but was recalled by a
breeze before reaching the top. The plate Ross had engraved from the scene,
[[artifact:ross-parry-211|the Crimson Cliffs with coloured snow]], prints the
position as latitude 76° 25' N.

The samples went to the botanist Robert Brown, librarian to Sir Joseph Banks,
who was given the whole expedition's botany to work up. His verdict is the last
entry in [[artifact:ross-parry-226|his list of the plants collected on the
voyage]], and it is a careful non-answer: a genus of the algae, doubtfully,
akin to the simplest confervae — "minute globules, the colouring matter of the
Red Snow, of which extensive patches were seen in Lat. 76° 25' N., and Long.
65° W." He would not name it. It is now identified as the snow alga
*Chlamydomonas nivalis*. The cliffs are still the [[place:Crimson Cliffs]] on
the chart, and red snow is still measured on that coast, now as a feedback that
darkens melting ice and speeds it up. Brown's
[[artifact:ross-parry-225|botanical appendix]] is here whole.

## The Croker Mountains, 31 August 1818

Ross crossed the head of Baffin Bay and confirmed Baffin's bays and sounds one
by one. On 30 August the ships lay at the mouth of a wide opening, and the
question of what it was divided the wardroom. The general opinion was that it
was only an inlet. Sabine produced Baffin's account and argued that they were
off [[place:Lancaster Sound]], and that there was no hope of a passage before
Cumberland Strait: "no indication of a passage," "no appearance of a current,"
"no driftwood," and "no swell from the north-west."

Soon after midnight the wind shifted and the Isabella stood directly up the
bay, leaving the Alexander astern. At four in the morning the officers of the
watch saw land at the bottom of the inlet; before Ross got on deck the fog had
closed over part of it. Ross's position for the day was 74° 03' N, 81° 28' W.
At half past one, the Alexander being six or seven miles off, they made all
sail again. At half past two Ross went below to dinner, leaving orders to be
called if land or ice showed ahead. At three the officer of the watch, relieved
for his own dinner by Mr Lewis, reported that it was clearing at the bottom of
the bay. Ross went on deck; it cleared completely for about ten minutes; and he

> distinctly saw the land, round the bottom of the bay, forming a connected
> chain of mountains with those which extended along the north and south sides.

The land appeared to be eight leagues off. He had Mr Lewis, the master, and
James Haig, a leading man, sent for to take its bearings for the log, and at
the same moment saw a continuity of ice seven miles away, stretching from one
side of the bay to the other. He named the capes on either hand after
Warrender and Castlereagh, the ice-filled southwest corner Barrow's Bay, and
[[artifact:ross-parry-031|the mountains in the centre Croker's Mountains,
after the Secretary to the Admiralty]]. The deep inlet in the north corner, he
wrote, "answered exactly to the latitude given by Baffin of Lancaster Sound,"
which he called "a most remarkable instance of the accuracy of that able
navigator" — the rehabilitation of a two-hundred-year-old chart in a sentence.
See [[topic:davis-baffin-hudson]]. At a quarter past three it was thick again.
"Being now perfectly satisfied that there was no passage in this direction,"
he tacked and stood back for the Alexander.

There are no mountains there. Lancaster Sound opens west into Barrow Strait and
runs, ice permitting, most of the way across the archipelago. It is
[[place:Lancaster Sound|Tallurutiup Imanga]], now Canada's largest marine
protected area, and ships including this one pass through it every season.

## Possession Bay, 1 September 1818

The landing came the day after, not before. On 1 September the ships stood for
Cape Byam Martin, and at one in the afternoon boats went in under Mr Skene and
Mr Ross to a small bay north of the cape, with Sabine, the surgeon and his
assistant to collect natural history and Parry directed to follow and take
command of the party ashore. They landed about two o'clock in latitude 73° 37'
N, longitude 77° 25' W, took possession of the country with the usual forms,
raised a flagstaff and buried a bottle containing the ships' proceedings at its
foot, on a conical mount near the centre of the bay. Ross called it
[[place:Possession Bay]]. While they were ashore Ross sounded from the ship in
a flat calm: a thousand fathoms of line, soft mud with worms in it, and, caught
on the line at eight hundred fathoms, a caput medusae — a basket star. They
were preserved and described in the appendix. It is one of the earliest
deep-sea biological samples taken anywhere, and it was taken because the
captain had nothing else to do while his officers were on the beach claiming
the country.

## The quarrel

Nobody on the Alexander saw the mountains. Parry, astern, had seen the sound
still open. Sabine, aboard the Isabella, said publicly that he had been on deck
and had seen nothing, and that Ross had put words in his mouth; he published a
pamphlet saying so in 1819, and Ross published a reply. John Barrow, whose
project the whole thing was, reviewed Ross's book anonymously and destroyed it.

The most likely explanation is not fraud. Ross saw something in ten minutes of
clear weather at the end of a long thick day: superior mirage, common in the
Arctic, will lift a distant coast into a wall across an open channel, and the
land on both sides of Lancaster Sound is genuinely mountainous. The failure was
one of method — he did not sail on to check, and he did not send the Alexander
in — and it was a failure of exactly the kind that a service which had never
wintered in the ice was liable to.

## What the voyage did bring back

The narrative is a poor guide to the geography and a good one to almost
everything else. Ross's chart of the coast is here
([[artifact:ross-parry-210|facing p. 116]]), with
[[artifact:ross-parry-228|panoramic coastal profiles]] for recognising the land
from seaward; so are the ships'
[[artifact:ross-parry-227|meteorological register]], a full sheet of daily
barometer, thermometer, wind and weather; the
[[artifact:ross-parry-223|table of latitudes and longitudes]];
[[artifact:ross-parry-224|pendulum observations furnished by Mr J. C. Ross]],
the captain's nephew, then a midshipman of eighteen; and diagrams of the
deviation of the compass in the [[artifact:ross-parry-218|Isabella]] and the
[[artifact:ross-parry-219|Alexander]], made because so close to the magnetic
pole the compass was becoming an instrument that had to be corrected before it
could be believed. The [[artifact:ross-parry-220|zoological]] and
[[artifact:ross-parry-221|geological memoranda]], and the
[[artifact:ross-parry-222|specimens taken at Agnes Monument]], are the first
systematic collections from these shores to reach European science.

The consequence of the mountains was that the Admiralty gave the next voyage to
Parry, who landed at Possession Bay on 31 July 1819, found Ross's flagstaff
still standing there, and sailed on through the mountains the next day without
ceremony: see [[page:parry-at-melville-island-1819-20]]. Ross never held another Admiralty
command. When he went north again in 1829 it was in a private steam vessel paid
for by a gin distiller, and the voyage ended with his crew walking to
[[place:Fury Beach]] to live on the stores Parry had left there
([[topic:ross-victory]]) — a coda in which the man who invented a mountain
range was saved by another man's shipwreck.
