Melville Island had shown Parry that the way west along Barrow Strait was
blocked by heavy old ice. So in 1821 he tried the other door: through Hudson
Strait and north up the west side of Foxe Basin, hunting for a channel out of
the top of Hudson Bay. He took [[vessel:HMS Fury]] himself and gave
[[vessel:HMS Hecla]] to [[person:George Francis Lyon]], who kept a second and
much more personal journal ([[source:lyon1824private]]). They were away from
May 1821 to the autumn of 1823. The whole track is on the volume's own
[[artifact:ross-parry-246|General Chart of the voyage]], and the route as
reconstructed here is [[artifact:ross-parry-003|Fury and Hecla, 1821-23]].

Geographically the voyage was a long defeat. They shut door after door: the
Duke of York Bay, Repulse Bay, Hoppner Inlet, Lyon Inlet all turned out to be
closed. The one real opening was [[place:Fury and Hecla Strait]], named for the
two ships — a narrow gut between Melville Peninsula and Baffin Island that they
could see led to the western sea and could not sail through, because a fixed
barrier of ice held in it through both summers. It still does most years. They
had been told it was there before they saw it, by an Iglulingmiut woman with a
pencil (see [[page:iligliuk-and-her-chart]]).

What the voyage did produce was the fullest first-hand British account of Inuit
life recorded in the nineteenth century, gathered over two winters of daily,
ordinary contact — and the largest single body of images and words. Lyon drew
constantly; Parry measured and wrote down. Both are here.

## Winter Island, 1821-22

The ships were secured for the winter in a bay on the south side of
[[place:Winter Island]] in early October 1821, sawn in through the new ice as at
Melville Island two years before — the scene Lyon drew as
[[artifact:ross-parry-230|Cutting into Winter Island, October, 1821]]. Parry
repeated the Melville Island regime — housing over the decks, an observatory and
house on shore, fortnightly theatricals — and added something new: schools,
where officers taught the men to read and write through the dark months. The
weather was written down every day; the monthly abstracts survive from
[[artifact:ross-parry-251|November 1821]] and
[[artifact:ross-parry-252|December]] through the coldest weeks of
[[artifact:ross-parry-254|February 1822]] to
[[artifact:ross-parry-258|June]], the last full month before they got out. They
are among the earliest sustained instrumental weather records kept anywhere in
Foxe Basin or on the Melville Peninsula, and the reason the prose about the cold
on this page can be checked against numbers.

On the morning of 1 February 1822 strangers were reported coming over the ice
from the westward. Parry and Lyon walked out to meet twenty-five Inuit — "to
the number of five-and-twenty" — drawn up abreast in a line, who stood still and
waited. What had looked like spears were blades of whalebone brought for trade.
Their "quiet and orderly behaviour," Parry wrote, contrasted sharply with the
noisy crowds of Hudson Strait. Some of the women began stripping off their outer
clothes to sell, with the thermometer at 23° below zero, which alarmed the
officers until they realised everyone was wearing a complete double suit of
caribou skin.

Then the visitors led them home, and the officers found, two miles from ships
that had been watched from all winter,

> an establishment of five huts, with canoes, sledges, dogs, and above sixty
> men, women, and children, as regularly and, to all appearance as
> permanently fixed, as if they had occupied the same spot for the whole
> winter.

Not a single material had been used in building them but snow and ice. Parry's
description of creeping through two low arched passages into a domed antechamber,
and from it into three lit rooms with a window of clear ice set in each roof, is
one of the classic passages of Arctic writing — the more so because he understood
at once that he was looking at engineering: each block "cut into the shape
requisite to form a substantial arch ... having no support whatever but what this
principle of building supplied." Lyon drew the inside of one
([[artifact:ross-parry-231|Interior of an Esquimaux Snow-hut, Winter Island,
1822]]) and, at Igloolik the next year, the building of one
([[artifact:ross-parry-240|Esquimaux building a Snow-hut]]). He also drew a
house made of clear ice slabs rather than snow
([[artifact:ross-parry-237|Esquimaux House built of ice]]).

The people of that village were the Iglulingmiut, and the record names them:
[[person:Iligliuk]] and her husband [[person:Okotook]];
[[person:Arnaneelia]] and [[person:Aneetka]]; the old man
[[person:Kaoongut]] and his son [[person:Toolooak]], a hunter Parry admired
without reserve; [[person:Ewerat]], the only man at Winter Island claiming the
title of angakkuq, and his wife [[person:Togolat]], who was Okotook's sister.
Lyon's portraits are the best visual record of the voyage:
[[artifact:ross-parry-014|Arnaneelia]], and from the second winter
[[artifact:ross-parry-239|Nakahu and his wife Oomna]] together and
[[artifact:ross-parry-015|Oomna]] alone.

It was a hungry winter. Sealing failed repeatedly; the men came back from
excursion after excursion with nothing. Parry, on Lyon's suggestion, began
issuing bread-dust from the ships' casks, which the Iglulingmiut took straight
home to their children. In February and March, in return, Iligliuk drew the
charts that changed the expedition's plan — see [[page:iligliuk-and-her-chart]].

The hunting the ships were watching is the other half of Lyon's portfolio: four
plates of seals and of hunters at the breathing-holes
([[artifact:ross-parry-232|seals on the ice]],
[[artifact:ross-parry-233|hunters at the breathing-holes]],
[[artifact:ross-parry-234|a hunter waiting]],
[[artifact:ross-parry-235|a struck animal and the line]]), the fan-hitched
[[artifact:ross-parry-236|Sledges of the Esquimaux]] — a team the officers timed
over two miles in five-and-twenty minutes — and, from the Igloolik summer,
[[artifact:ross-parry-241|Esquimaux killing Deer in a Lake]], hunters in kayaks
spearing caribou at a crossing.

Three of the Fury's men died at Winter Island: James Pringle, who fell behind a
walking party and lay down on the snow; and the quartermaster William Souter
and, days later, John Reid, the carpenter's mate, whom the surgeon thought was
hastened by the depression of spirits at Souter's death. Their graves were built
over with stone tombs before the ships left on 2 July 1822.

## Iglulik, 1822-23

The ships worked north along the Melville Peninsula coast, exactly as Iligliuk's
chart had said they should, reached the eastern entrance of
[[place:Fury and Hecla Strait]] in the summer of 1822 and were stopped by fixed
ice. Sledge and walking parties went through by land instead and saw the open
water beyond, confirming an outlet to the Polar Sea. The ships could not follow.
Lyon's coastal profiles of the strait — [[artifact:ross-parry-244|Cape Penrhyn
and the mouth]], [[artifact:ross-parry-245|Liddon Island, the Narrows and the
views from Whyte Inlet and Fire Rock]] — are what they brought out of it instead
of a passage. The August and September weather they were fighting is in the
registers ([[artifact:ross-parry-259|August 1822]],
[[artifact:ross-parry-260|September 1822]]).

Cutting into the winter station off [[place:Iglulik]] took thirteen days. The
canal was four thousand three hundred and forty-three feet long through ice
generally twelve to fourteen inches thick and in places several feet, with the
thermometer often at zero and once at -9°; it was finished at noon on
30 October 1822, and in all that work only one man was added to the sick list,
by falling in. The Iglulingmiut came down daily on sledges while it went on and
took a hand — hauling on the ropes, heaving at the windlass, sawing ice for an
hour at a stretch, and imitating the sailors' way of "singing out" when hauling.
Parry singled out two young men of eighteen or twenty, Noogloo, an adopted son
of [[person:Toolemak]], and Kongolek, who had the windlass mastered after a
couple of hours' practice. The ships lay in rather less than five fathoms, a
cable's length from the eastern shore of the bay.

The Iglulik winter is Lyon's book more than Parry's. He recorded a full account
of an angakkuq's séance, given to him voluntarily in his own cabin by a man named
[[person:Ooyara]] — the drawn knife, the darkened hut, the voice travelling away
underground and returning with a yell, the convulsions, the blood of Tornga on
the blade — as a description of Toolemak, the angakkuq of the Iglulik people, at
work. Lyon reported it as a performance whose techniques Ooyara was frank about,
and did not pretend to know what it was for. It remains one of the earliest
detailed outside descriptions of a shamanic séance in the eastern Arctic; the
Iglulingmiut oral tradition on the same practices is a separate and better
source, and belongs with [[topic:inuit-oral-history]].

The second winter was also an epidemic. Coughs and catarrhs went through the
Iglulik camps and turned into something worse. The Fury's sick bay filled with
Inuit patients until the ship's own men had to be moved almost entirely out of
it, so Parry had Lieutenant Nias build a hospital inside the walls of the ships'
shore square: twelve feet square, of spars, turf, snow and canvas, with a passage
and two doors, five bed-places and a small warming stove in the centre, put up in
a couple of days at "no expense but that of labour." Messrs Edwards and Skeoch
arranged the medical attendance and Mr Hooper laid in a stock of walrus meat for
the patients. Innooksioo, one of the most vigorous men in the camp, was brought
in on old Nannow's sledge so reduced that his face could scarcely be recognised;
Nannow went on visiting him. Kaga was nursed by Lyon. Kooeetseek recovered and
was sledged home. Others died, among them Togolat. The winter's cold is in the
registers from [[artifact:ross-parry-261|December 1822]] to
[[artifact:ross-parry-266|July 1823]].

It is worth being plain about this. The record does not let us say what the
disease was, and both captains wrote about it as an act of God. But respiratory
epidemics arriving with ships, in camps with no immunity, are a pattern that
repeats through the whole history of contact in this region, from the whaling
years ([[topic:whaling]]) through to the tuberculosis evacuations documented by
the Qikiqtani Truth Commission ([[topic:relocations-qtc]]). The Fury and Hecla
were among the first ships to overwinter beside an Inuit community, and an
epidemic followed them.

The crews suffered too. Scurvy appeared among the seamen and marines in the
spring of 1823. Alexander Elder, Greenland mate of the Hecla and a veteran of
all three of Parry's expeditions, died at Iglulik on 15 April 1823. Parry decided
the passage could not be forced, sent the Hecla home, and brought both ships
south; the Fury was beset and drifted back up Lyon Inlet on the way out, and
George Fife died before they reached England in the autumn of 1823. Five men dead
in two years, against one in the ten months at Melville Island.

## The words they brought back

The other thing the two winters produced was a language record. At the end of his
second volume Parry printed a
[[artifact:ross-parry-269|note on the pronunciation of Esquimaux words]] and then
eleven pages of vocabulary compiled with Lyon, running from
[[artifact:ross-parry-270|Air to Bite]] to
[[artifact:ross-parry-280|Wait to Young]], and two pages of
[[artifact:ross-parry-281|Iglulingmiut place names]] set beside the English ones
the expedition had just imposed. Winter Island is *Ne-yu-ning Eit-du-a*.
[[artifact:ross-parry-282|Liddon Island and Amherst Island]] are both
*Seag-glur-ru-ok*; Whyte Inlet is *Ewee-ta-oke-toke*.

The vocabulary is a working list, not a grammar, and it shows what the officers
needed to say and hear. The seal-hole is *Agloo*, and the seals are named
separately by size and species — *Oguke* for the large bearded seal, *Neitiek*
for the small ringed seal, *Kairolik* for the middle-sized, *Ibleeow* for the
young ([[artifact:ross-parry-277|that page is here]]); there are distinct spears
for the large seal, the walrus and the whale
([[artifact:ross-parry-278|here]]). One page carries, within a few lines of each
other, "Instrument of bone for discovering seals under ice . . . Keip-kut-tuk,"
one of the earliest printings in English of the word *igloo*, glossed simply
"Hut, or house," and "Lamp of stone . . . Kood-le-ook" — the qulliq, the
soapstone lamp ([[artifact:ross-parry-274|here]]). Parry's own warning at the head of the
list is the most honest thing in it: ask an Iglulingmiut what a pair of sealskin
mittens is called, he says, and you will be told "neitiek" (seal) or "amia"
(skin) before anyone bothers to tell you the word for mittens.

## What the Iglulingmiut knew

Parry left convinced of two geographical facts he had been given rather than
worked out: that Melville Peninsula was a peninsula and the coast turned west
beyond it, and that a strait ran through to a western sea. Both were Inuit
knowledge, drawn on paper by Iligliuk and corroborated by Ewerat, and both were
right. See [[page:iligliuk-and-her-chart]].

He also brought back a report he could not check, and neither can we: the
Iglulingmiut told him that two fishing ships had been wrecked somewhere to the
northward. Whalers were already working the Baffin Bay grounds by 1823
([[topic:whaling]]), and this may be the earliest Inuit testimony about a
European wreck in the region recorded by a naval expedition.
