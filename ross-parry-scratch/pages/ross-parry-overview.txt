# Ross and Parry reopen the passage, 1818-1825
[page · ross-parry · draft]

In 1817 the whaler William Scoresby reported to the Admiralty that the ice
of the Greenland Sea had unaccountably slackened. John Barrow, Second
Secretary to the Admiralty, had a navy with nothing to do after Waterloo and
an old idea to revive: a navigable sea route from Baffin Bay to the Pacific.
Between 1818 and 1825 the Admiralty sent five expeditions into the eastern
Canadian Arctic under [[person:John Ross]] and [[person:William Edward Parry]].
They did not find the passage. They did something else: they put the
Canadian Arctic Archipelago on a chart, they proved that a British ship could
winter in the ice and bring her people home alive, and they spent two winters
living alongside the Iglulingmiut of Winter Island and [[place:Iglulik]] —
producing, among much else, the first map of the Melville Peninsula coast,
drawn by an Inuk woman named [[person:Iligliuk]].

## The five voyages

**1818 — Isabella and Alexander.** Ross commanded, with Parry as lieutenant
in charge of the Alexander and [[person:Edward Sabine]] as astronomer. They
crossed Baffin Bay, met the Inughuit near [[place:Cape York]] on 10 August
through the Greenlandic interpreter [[person:John Sacheuse]], and entered
[[place:Lancaster Sound]] on 31 August. There Ross saw a chain of mountains
closing the head of the sound, named them Croker's Mountains after the First
Secretary of the Admiralty, and turned back. The mountains did not exist. See
[[page:ross-1818-and-the-croker-mountains]].

**1819-20 — Hecla and Griper.** Parry, given his own command, sailed straight
through Ross's mountains, ran west along Barrow Strait and Viscount Melville
Sound, and on 4 September 1819 crossed the meridian of 110° W — winning a
£5,000 parliamentary prize. The two ships were sawn into
[[place:Winter Harbour]] on Melville Island on 26 September and stayed frozen
in for ten months. See [[page:parry-at-melville-island-1819-20]].

**1821-23 — Fury and Hecla.** Parry tried a southern door instead: through
Hudson Strait and up the west side of Foxe Basin. Two winters, at
[[place:Winter Island]] (1821-22) and at Iglulik (1822-23), were spent in
daily contact with the Iglulingmiut, with [[person:George Francis Lyon]]
commanding the Hecla and keeping the more candid of the two journals. See
[[page:two-winters-among-the-iglulingmiut]] and
[[page:iligliuk-and-her-chart]].

**1824 — Griper.** Lyon alone, sent to land a party at Repulse Bay and sledge
overland to Point Turnagain. He never got there; the Griper was nearly lost in
a September gale in Roes Welcome Sound and came home having achieved nothing
but a very good account of a bad storm. See [[page:lyon-and-the-griper-1824]].

**1824-25 — Hecla and Fury.** Parry's third voyage, down Prince Regent Inlet.
A miserable season of ice, a winter at Port Bowen, and then on 21 August 1825
the Fury was driven ashore and had to be abandoned. Her stores, stacked on the
beach, sat there for years. See [[page:the-loss-of-the-fury]].

## What was actually found

Everything Parry sailed past in 1819 he named, and most of those names are
still on the chart: Barrow Strait, Prince Regent Inlet, Wellington Channel,
Melville Island, Banks Land. The great gap in his 1819 chart — Wellington
Channel to the north, Prince Regent Inlet to the south — set the shape of
every search that followed, including [[topic:franklin-1845]], which used
Parry's own route as far as Beechey Island. [[place:Fury and Hecla Strait]],
which Parry found in 1822 and could not get through because it is choked with
ice most years, is still one of the least navigable straits in the
archipelago.

The negative results mattered as much. Ross's Croker Mountains, and the
argument they started, are the reason Parry got a second ship. The Fury's
abandoned stores at [[place:Fury Beach]] kept [[person:John Ross]]'s own crew
alive through three winters in 1832-33 ([[topic:ross-victory]]) — the single
most improbable survival in the region's history, and it depended on a wreck.

## The Inuit side of the record

Parry and Lyon's second voyage produced the best ethnographic record of any
British Arctic expedition of the century, and it is a record of named
individuals rather than of a "tribe": [[person:Iligliuk]], her husband Okotook
and their family; [[person:Arnaneelia]] and [[person:Aneetka]] at Winter
Island; [[person:Toolooak]], [[person:Kaoongut]], [[person:Oomna]] and
[[person:Nakahu]]. Lyon drew portraits of many of them, some of which are
here: [[artifact:ross-parry-014|Arnaneelia]] and
[[artifact:ross-parry-015|Oomna]].

Iligliuk's chart, [[artifact:ross-parry-011|Eskimaux Chart No. 1]], is the
document that matters most. Parry laid down the coast he already knew and
asked her to continue it; she drew Melville Peninsula, brought the coast round
to the west, and put a strait where [[place:Fury and Hecla Strait]] is. He
sailed north the following summer on the strength of it. British Arctic
geography of the 1820s is, in that stretch, Inuit geography written down.
See [[page:iligliuk-and-her-chart]].

The people of Iglulik and Naujaat did not stop existing when the ships left.
Iglulik is a community of about 2,000 today; the Iglulingmiut place names
Parry and Lyon recorded phonetically — Amitioke for Amitturmiut country,
Neyuning-Eitua for Winter Island, Akkoolee for the Arviligjuarmiut coast to
the west — are living names. See [[topic:inuit-oral-history]].

## Why it matters to this ship

The 1819-20 wintering at Melville Island is the first controlled experiment in
keeping a ship's company healthy through an Arctic winter: Parry's routines
of exercise, ventilation, lime juice, a fortnightly play and a weekly
newspaper are the direct ancestors of every overwintering protocol since. Only
one man died. Sabine's magnetic and pendulum observations at Winter Harbour,
and Alexander Fisher's meteorological series (see
[[source:fisher1821journal]]), are among the earliest instrumental records
from the archipelago and are still occasionally used as a baseline.
