# Ross in 1818, and the mountains that were not there
[page · ross-parry · draft]

The Admiralty's 1818 expedition had two objects: find the entrance to a
Northwest Passage, and settle whether William Baffin's 1616 chart of Baffin
Bay — quietly dropped from the maps as a fabrication — was true. It settled
the second question emphatically and got the first one badly wrong.

Two whalers were bought and strengthened: the Isabella of 385 tons under
[[person:John Ross]], and the Alexander of 252 tons under Lieutenant
[[person:William Edward Parry]]. [[person:Edward Sabine]] of the Royal
Artillery came as astronomer. They sailed from the Thames in April 1818 and
were home in November. The route is
[[artifact:ross-parry-001|reconstructed here from the marginal positions in
Ross's own narrative]].

## Meeting the Inughuit, 10 August 1818

Working north up the Greenland coast, on 10 August the ships were beset in
latitude 75° 55' N, longitude 65° 32' W, off [[place:Cape York]], when eight
dog sledges appeared and halted a mile off. The expedition carried
[[person:John Sacheuse]], a young West Greenlander who had stowed away to
Leith two years earlier, taught himself English, and volunteered now to go
out onto the ice alone and unarmed with a white flag and a bundle of presents.

A chasm in the ice separated the two parties. Sacheuse tried the Humooke
dialect and found it close enough. The first man drew a knife from his boot
and told him to go away — "I can kill you." Sacheuse threw an English knife
across, and they picked it up. Then, said Ross, they pointed at the ships and
asked [[artifact:ross-parry-030|what great creatures those were]]: did they
come from the sun or the moon? Sacheuse said they were houses made of wood.
"No," they answered, "they are alive, we have seen them move their wings."

These were the Inughuit of northwest Greenland, perhaps two hundred people,
cut off from the rest of Greenland and from each other's knowledge of the
world; Ross called them "Arctic Highlanders." They had iron, hammered cold
from the Cape York meteorites, and no wood to speak of. See
[[topic:greenland-inughuit]] for what became of them and their meteoric iron.

Sacheuse painted the encounter himself, and the picture was engraved for
Ross's book: [[artifact:ross-parry-010|First Communication with the Natives of
Prince Regents Bay]]. It is among the earliest published works by a
Greenlandic Inuk artist. Sacheuse died of typhus at Edinburgh in 1819, aged
about twenty-two, before the argument over the rest of the voyage had properly
begun. The full passage is at
[[artifact:ross-parry-020|Ross's account of the first communication]].

## The Crimson Cliffs, 17 August 1818

A week later, running along the same coast, the ships passed cliffs whose snow
was stained [[artifact:ross-parry-032|a deep crimson colour]]. Ross sent a
boat with Mr Ross, midshipman, and Mr Beverley, assistant surgeon, to bring
back specimens; bird droppings were ruled out on the spot, because thousands of
birds were sitting on clean snow all around. The samples went home to the
botanist Robert Brown, who identified the cause as a microscopic alga — what
is now called *Chlamydomonas nivalis*, snow algae. The cliffs are still the
[[place:Crimson Cliffs]] on the chart, and red snow is still measured there,
now as an albedo-lowering feedback on melting ice. See
[[artifact:ross-parry-021|the excerpt]].

## The Croker Mountains, 31 August 1818

Ross crossed the head of Baffin Bay, confirmed Baffin's bays and sounds one by
one, and on 30 August landed at [[place:Possession Bay]] on the north shore of
a wide opening. He noted that it "answered exactly to the latitude given by
Baffin of Lancaster Sound," and called Baffin's accuracy "a most remarkable
instance" — the rehabilitation of a two-hundred-year-old chart in a sentence.
See [[topic:davis-baffin-hudson]].

The next day the ships stood west into [[place:Lancaster Sound]] in thick
weather, Ross's position for the day about 74° 03' N, 81° 28' W. At half past
two Ross went below to dinner, leaving orders to be called if land or ice
showed ahead. At three the officer of the watch reported it clearing at the
bottom of the bay. Ross went on deck; it cleared completely for about ten
minutes; and he

> distinctly saw the land, round the bottom of the bay, forming a connected
> chain of mountains with those which extended along the north and south sides.

He had the master, Mr Lewis, and a leading man, James Haig, take its bearings
for the log. He named the capes on either hand after Warrender and
Castlereagh, the ice-filled southwest corner Barrow's Bay, and
[[artifact:ross-parry-031|the mountains in the centre Croker's Mountains,
after the Secretary to the Admiralty]]. At a quarter past three it was thick
again. "Being now perfectly satisfied that there was no passage in this
direction," he tacked and stood back for the Alexander.

There are no mountains there. Lancaster Sound opens west into Barrow Strait
and runs, ice permitting, most of the way across the archipelago. It is
[[place:Lancaster Sound|Tallurutiup Imanga]], now Canada's largest marine
protected area, and ships including this one pass through it every season.

## The quarrel

Nobody on the Alexander saw the mountains. Parry, six or seven miles astern,
had seen the sound still open. Sabine, aboard the Isabella, said publicly that
he had been on deck and had seen nothing, and that Ross had put words in his
mouth; he published a pamphlet saying so in 1819, and Ross published a reply.
John Barrow, whose project the whole thing was, reviewed Ross's book
anonymously and destroyed it.

The most likely explanation is not fraud. Ross saw something in ten minutes of
clear weather at the end of a long thick day: superior mirage, common in the
Arctic, will lift a distant coast into a wall across an open channel, and the
land on both sides of Lancaster Sound is genuinely mountainous. The failure was
one of method — he did not sail on to check, and he did not send the Alexander
in — and it was a failure of exactly the kind that a service which had never
wintered in the ice was liable to.

The consequence was that the Admiralty gave the next voyage to Parry, who
sailed through the Croker Mountains in the first week of August 1820 without
comment: see [[page:parry-at-melville-island-1819-20]]. Ross never held
another Admiralty command. When he went north again in 1829 it was in a
private steam vessel paid for by a gin distiller, and the voyage ended with
his crew walking to [[place:Fury Beach]] to live on the stores Parry had left
there ([[topic:ross-victory]]) — a coda in which the man who invented a
mountain range was saved by another man's shipwreck.
