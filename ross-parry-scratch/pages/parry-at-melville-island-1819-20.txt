# Parry at Melville Island, 1819-20
[page · ross-parry · draft]

The 1819 expedition was the reply to the argument of 1818. The Admiralty gave
[[person:William Edward Parry]], aged twenty-eight, the bomb vessel Hecla of
375 tons and the gun-brig Griper of 180 tons under Lieutenant Matthew Liddon,
and sent him back to [[place:Lancaster Sound]] to see whether the Croker
Mountains were there. [[person:Edward Sabine]] came again as astronomer;
Alexander Fisher was assistant surgeon of the Hecla and kept his own published
journal ([[source:fisher1821journal]]); Frederick Beechey and Henry Hoppner
were lieutenants; a midshipman named James Clark Ross, nephew of
[[person:John Ross]], was aboard. They sailed in May 1819.

## Through the mountains, and west

Parry pushed through the middle ice of Baffin Bay and entered Lancaster Sound
at the end of July. The mountains were not there. He ran west without ceremony,
naming as he went — Prince Regent Inlet, Barrow Strait, Wellington Channel,
Cornwallis Island, Bathurst Island, Melville Island — and on the evening of
4 September 1819, in latitude 74° 44' 20", the ships crossed the meridian of
110° west:

> by which His Majesty's ships, under my orders, became entitled to the sum of
> five thousand pounds, being the reward offered by the King's order in
> council.

The men named the bluff headland they had just passed Bounty Cape, and Parry
put the name on the chart. See [[artifact:ross-parry-022|the excerpt]].

He got about sixty miles further west before the ice stopped him. In one week
Parry had made more westing than the entire preceding two centuries of search.

## Nearly losing seven men, 10-14 September 1819

Before the winter began there was a warning. At daylight on 10 September a
shooting party of seven from the Griper, under Mr Fife, went inland after
tracks of caribou and muskoxen with a little food and no plan. They lost
themselves within hours. A search party sent after them lost itself too and
came back at ten at night, guided in by rockets. Parry had the Hecla's
fore-royal-mast carried four or five miles inland and set up as a flagstaff
with a large ensign, then sent out four parties planting pikes with bottled
directions at intervals. The lost men reached the flagstaff, ate the provisions
left there, and came in at ten in the evening on 14 September, after
ninety-one hours out in the first wintry weather of the season. Several were
badly frostbitten; none died. A hard gale with the thermometer at 15° came on
before midnight, and Parry named the headland west of the ships Cape
Providence.

## Cutting into Winter Harbour, 24-26 September 1819

On 23 September Parry examined two harbours on the south coast of Melville
Island. Fife's Harbour had barely twelve feet on the bar at high water spring
tide; the westernmost had five to six fathoms, and a continuous new floe four
or five inches thick over the whole of it. He chose the deep one and set every
officer and man in both ships to sawing a canal more than two miles long
through the ice.

Two parallel lines were marked out a little wider than the Hecla's beam,
cut with ice-saws, then cross-cut at ten to twenty foot intervals and the
rectangles halved diagonally so the pieces could be floated out. The seamen,
"who are always fond of doing things in their own way," rigged boats' sails on
the floating slabs and let a northerly breeze carry them out of the cut. The
ships were warped in and secured on 26 September. See
[[artifact:ross-parry-012|the plate of the cutting-in]] and
[[artifact:ross-parry-013|Westall's view of the two ships in the harbour]].

Near the southwest point of [[place:Winter Harbour]] stands a block of
sandstone "somewhat resembling the roof of a house." Mr Fisher engraved the
ships' names on it. Parry's Rock is still there, and is a National Historic
Site of Canada.

## Ten months in the ice

Nobody in the Royal Navy had deliberately wintered a ship in the Arctic
before. What Parry improvised became the template. The upper decks were housed
over with wagon-cloths and banked with snow. The men ran laps round the deck
for exercise every morning. Rations were kept full, with lemon juice and sugar
served out daily under an officer's eye, plus spruce beer, pickles, preserved
soups, currants and gooseberries. Every man's gums were inspected on a
schedule.

The enemy turned out to be damp rather than cold. Breath and cooking condensed
on the ships' sides and froze in the bed-places, and it was an officer who broke
first: in early January 1820 Mr Scallon, the Hecla's gunner, came down with
scurvy, and the surgeon traced it to bedding soaked through against the hull.
Parry started growing mustard and cress in shallow boxes of mould laid along
the stove pipe in his own cabin, cropping them on the sixth or seventh day and
issuing nearly an ounce of salad a day to the scorbutic patients — in a ship
where the coal ration would not let the fire burn overnight.

For the dark he prescribed occupation. From 5 November 1819 the officers put on
a play on the Hecla's deck once a fortnight, Beechey elected stage manager,
Parry taking a part himself because "an example of cheerfulness ... was not the
least essential part of my duty." The stock of playbooks ran to one or two
volumes, so the officers wrote a Christmas musical entertainment of their own
about the voyage. Plays were performed with the thermometer below zero on the
stage. In parallel Sabine edited a weekly newspaper, the *North Georgia Gazette
and Winter Chronicle*, filled with anonymous contributions from the officers of
both ships; it was printed in London in 1821 and is here as
[[source:anon1821north]]. See [[artifact:ross-parry-023|the excerpt]].

The winter's worst day was 24 February 1820, with the thermometer between -43°
and -44°. At a quarter past ten in the morning, while the men were running
round the decks, the observatory house on shore was found to be on fire. Both
ships' companies ran up, pulled the roof off with ropes and knocked in the
sides to get snow onto the flames, and had it out in three quarters of an hour,
just short of the clocks and transit instrument. Sixteen men went onto the sick
lists; noses and cheeks went white in five minutes and the surgeons walked the
fire line rubbing faces with snow. John Smith of the Royal Artillery, Sabine's
servant, ran out of the burning house carrying the dipping needle without time
to put his gloves on. When his hands were plunged into a basin of cold water it
froze at the surface. He lost parts of four fingers on one hand and three on
the other, so that Sabine's magnetic instrument survived intact and its keeper
did not.

One man died in ten months: William Scott, boatswain's mate of the Hecla, at
two in the morning on 30 June 1820, of an illness the surgeon judged unrelated
to the climate. He was buried on 2 July on level ground a hundred yards from
the beach, attended by every officer and man in both ships, with a headstone
carved by Mr Fisher.

## Out, and home

The ships left Winter Harbour on 1 August 1820 and worked west along Melville
Island's south coast in heavy old ice. In the middle of the month they lay off
Cape Dundas at latitude 74° 26' 25" N, longitude 113° 46' 43" W by chronometer
— "the westernmost point to which the navigation of the Polar Sea to the
northward of the American continent has yet been carried." Parry's title page
claims 113° 48' 22" as the expedition's farthest; the record stood for a
generation either way. Land was in sight to the southwest, which he named Banks
Land. Between it and Melville Island lay ice he correctly judged impassable:
that is M'Clure Strait, still among the hardest water in the archipelago and
navigable only in exceptional seasons. He turned for home and reached England
in November 1820.

Parry had proved three things at once: that Lancaster Sound was open, that the
route west was blocked by multi-year ice pouring in from the polar basin, and
that a British ship's company could sit out an Arctic winter and come home
almost whole. The third proposition is why later expeditions kept trying — and,
in the case of [[topic:franklin-1845]], why so few people worried early enough.
[[topic:mcclure-investigator]] came at the same blockage from the other side
thirty years later and lost a ship to it.
