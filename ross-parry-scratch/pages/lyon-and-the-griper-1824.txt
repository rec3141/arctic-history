# Lyon and the Griper, 1824
[page · ross-parry · draft]

The 1824 season was meant to be a pincer. [[person:William Edward Parry]] would
go down Prince Regent Inlet with the Hecla and Fury
([[page:the-loss-of-the-fury]]); John Franklin would come down the Mackenzie and
work east along the continental coast; and [[person:George Francis Lyon]],
promoted captain after his two winters among the Iglulingmiut, would land a
party at Repulse Bay and sledge west overland to Point Turnagain, the farthest
point Franklin had reached in 1821. Between them they would close the gap.

Lyon was given the Griper. She was the same slow, cranky gun-brig that had held
Parry back all through 1819-20, she was loaded far beyond what she could
carry, and she was the only ship the Admiralty would spare. Everything that
followed came from that.

He left Deptford on 10 June 1824 and the Little Nore on the 16th, sailed from
Stromness on 3 July, and parted company with his transport the Snap off Cape
Chidley on 4 August. He was in Hudson Strait through the middle of August,
beset and hanging to floes, and landed on Southampton Island at Point Leyson on
24 August. On the 27th he met Inuit on the south coast who were still striking
fire with flint. The route is [[artifact:ross-parry-005|traced here]].

## The gale of 1 September 1824

By the end of August he was in Roes Welcome Sound, on a coast that was
badly charted, in a ship that could not work off a lee shore, with tides of
twelve to fifteen feet. On 1 September a gale drove him into shoal water and he
anchored because there was nothing else to do.

The tide began to fall. Lyon had the long-boat hoisted out and the four smaller
boats stored with arms and provisions, which meant throwing overboard the stores
the long-boat had been full of, because there was no room for them on decks
that heavy seas were sweeping. Then the officers drew lots for boats.

> it was evident to all, that the long-boat was the only one which had the
> slightest chance of living under the lee of the ship, should she be wrecked,
> but every officer and man drew his lot with the greatest composure, although
> two of our boats would have been swamped the instant they were lowered.

In the afternoon the weather cleared enough to show a low beach astern of them
with surf running to an awful height, "and it appeared evident that no human
powers could save us." At three the tide had fallen to twenty-two feet — six
more than the ship drew — and a sea lifted her and dropped her, and she struck
the whole length of her keel. She went on striking every few minutes for hours,
in water so shallow the seas were breaking rather than passing, and did not
break up. See [[artifact:ross-parry-026|the excerpt]].

She held. Standing out from the anchorage the next day, Lyon named it, in what
he called humble gratitude for their deliverance, the **Bay of God's Mercy**.

## The second gale, and the decision

He tried again. On the night of 11 September the Griper reached 65° 20' N,
her farthest, and was still more than eighty miles from
[[place:Naujaat|Repulse Bay]]. On the 12th another gale caught her at anchor
between Whale Point and Cape Fullerton. At half past four on the morning of the
13th the best bower cable parted; by six every remaining cable had gone, and the
ship lay helpless with no anchors left to bring her up.

Lyon put the question to his officers in writing rather than deciding alone.
After noon on Wednesday 15 September 1824 he bore up — "with a sad heart," he
wrote — and gave up the attempt. He came down Hudson Strait through late
September, was gale-bound in Davis Strait in October, sighted Land's End on
8 November and ran into Portsmouth Harbour on 10 November 1824, having no
anchors to bring up with. The Griper was paid off on 13 December.

## Afterwards

Lyon had achieved nothing he was sent to do and lost every anchor and cable in
the ship. He published [[source:lyon1825brief|A Brief Narrative of an
Unsuccessful Attempt]] the following year — the title is his own, and the book
is unusually honest about the ship, the loading, and his own judgement. It is
the best surviving account of what a small overloaded vessel does on a shoal
Arctic coast, and it should be read next to his
[[source:lyon1824private|Private Journal]] of 1821-23, which is the best
account of Inuit life the expeditions produced.

He never got another command. He left the Navy, lost his money in a Mexican
mining venture, and died at sea in 1832, aged thirty-seven. His portrait,
painted in 1823 between the two voyages, is
[[artifact:ross-parry-018|here]]; his drawings of the Iglulingmiut, including
[[artifact:ross-parry-014|Arnaneelia]] and [[artifact:ross-parry-015|Oomna]],
are what most people have actually seen of him.

The gap he was sent to close stayed open. It was closed on foot, over the next
twenty-five years, by Rae and by the Franklin searchers — and the last piece of
it, the stretch of coast Lyon was aiming for, is where the Erebus and Terror
were lost ([[topic:franklin-1845]]).
