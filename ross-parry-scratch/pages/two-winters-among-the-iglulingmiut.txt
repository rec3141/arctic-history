# Two winters among the Iglulingmiut, 1821-23
[page · ross-parry · draft]

Melville Island had shown Parry that the way west along Barrow Strait was
blocked by heavy old ice. So in 1821 he tried the other door: through Hudson
Strait and north up the west side of Foxe Basin, hunting for a channel out of
the top of Hudson Bay. He took [[source:parry1824journal|HMS Fury]] himself
and gave the Hecla to [[person:George Francis Lyon]], who kept a second and
much more personal journal ([[source:lyon1824private]]). They were away from
May 1821 to the autumn of 1823.

Geographically the voyage was a long defeat. They shut door after door: the
Duke of York Bay, Repulse Bay, Hoppner Inlet, Lyon Inlet all turned out to be
closed. The one real opening they found —
[[place:Fury and Hecla Strait]], named for the two ships — is a narrow gut
between Melville Peninsula and Baffin Island that they could see led to the
western sea and could not sail through, because a fixed barrier of ice held in
it through both summers. It still does most years.

What the voyage did produce was the fullest first-hand British account of
Inuit life recorded in the nineteenth century, gathered over two winters of
daily, ordinary contact.

## Winter Island, 1821-22

The ships were secured for the winter in a bay on the south side of
[[place:Winter Island]] in early October 1821. Parry repeated the Melville
Island regime — housing over the decks, an observatory and house on shore,
fortnightly theatricals — and added something new: schools, where officers
taught the men to read and write through the dark months.

On the morning of 1 February 1822 strangers were reported coming over the ice
from the westward. Parry and Lyon walked out to meet twenty-five Inuit drawn
up abreast in a line, who stood still and waited. What had looked like spears
were blades of whalebone brought for trade. Their "quiet and orderly
behaviour," Parry wrote, contrasted sharply with the noisy crowds of Hudson
Strait. Some of the women began stripping off their outer clothes to sell,
with the thermometer at 23° below zero, which alarmed the officers until they
realised everyone was wearing a complete double suit of caribou skin.

Then the visitors led them home, and the officers found, two miles from ships
that had been watched from all winter,

> an establishment of five huts, with canoes, sledges, dogs, and above sixty
> men, women, and children, as regularly and, to all appearance as
> permanently fixed, as if they had occupied the same spot for the whole
> winter.

Not a single material had been used in building them but snow and ice. Parry's
description of creeping through two low arched passages into a domed
antechamber, and from it into three lit rooms with a window of clear ice set
in each roof, is one of the classic passages of Arctic writing — the more so
because he understood at once that he was looking at engineering: each block
"cut into the shape requisite to form a substantial arch ... having no support
whatever but what this principle of building supplied."

The people of that village were the Iglulingmiut, and the record names them:
[[person:Iligliuk]] and her husband [[person:Okotook]];
[[person:Arnaneelia]] and [[person:Aneetka]]; the old man
[[person:Kaoongut]] and his son [[person:Toolooak]], a hunter Parry admired
without reserve; [[person:Ewerat]], the only man at Winter Island claiming the
title of angakkuq, and his wife [[person:Togolat]], whom Lyon drew. Lyon's
portraits of Winter Island and Iglulik people are the best visual record of
the voyage: [[artifact:ross-parry-014|Arnaneelia]],
[[artifact:ross-parry-015|Oomna]].

It was a hungry winter. Sealing failed repeatedly; the men came back from
excursion after excursion with nothing. Parry, on Lyon's suggestion, began
issuing bread-dust from the ships' casks, which the Iglulingmiut took straight
home to their children. In February and March, in return, Iligliuk drew the
charts that changed the expedition's plan — see [[page:iligliuk-and-her-chart]].

Three of the Fury's men died at Winter Island: James Pringle, who fell behind
a walking party and lay down on the snow; and the quartermaster William Souter
and, days later, John Reid, whom the surgeon thought was hastened by the
depression of spirits at Souter's death. Their graves were built over with
stone tombs before the ships left on 2 July 1822.

## Iglulik, 1822-23

The ships worked north along the Melville Peninsula coast, exactly as
Iligliuk's chart had said they should, reached the eastern entrance of
[[place:Fury and Hecla Strait]] in the summer of 1822, and were stopped by
fixed ice. Sledge and walking parties went through by land instead and saw the
open water beyond, confirming an outlet to the Polar Sea. The ships could not
follow. On 31 October 1822 the Fury was cut in to her winter station off
[[place:Iglulik]], a canal of 4,343 feet, with a line strung between the two
ships on snow pillars the height of a man so nobody would be lost between them
in a storm.

The Iglulik winter is Lyon's book more than Parry's. He recorded a full
account of an angakkuq's séance, given to him voluntarily in his own cabin by a
man named [[person:Ooyara]] — the drawn knife, the darkened hut, the voice
travelling away underground and returning with a yell, the convulsions, the
blood of Tornga on the blade — as a description of [[person:Toolemak]], the
angakkuq of the Iglulik people, at work. Lyon reported it as a performance
whose techniques Ooyara was frank about, and did not pretend to know what it
was for. It remains one of the earliest detailed outside descriptions of a
shamanic seance in the eastern Arctic; the Iglulingmiut oral tradition on the
same practices is a separate and better source, and belongs with
[[topic:inuit-oral-history]].

The second winter was also an epidemic. Coughs and catarrhs went through the
Iglulik camps and turned into something worse. The Fury's sick bay filled with
Inuit patients until the ship's own men had to be moved out of it, so Parry had
Lieutenant Nias build a hospital inside the walls of the ships' shore square:
twelve feet square, of spars, turf, snow and canvas, with two doors, five
bed-places and a warming stove, put up in two days at "no expense but that of
labour." Innooksioo, one of the strongest men in the camp, was brought in on
old Nannow's sledge so reduced that his face could not be recognised. Kaga was
nursed by Lyon. Kooeetseek recovered and was sledged home. Others died, among
them Togolat.

It is worth being plain about this. The record does not let us say what the
disease was, and both captains wrote about it as an act of God. But respiratory
epidemics arriving with ships, in camps with no immunity, are a pattern that
repeats through the whole history of contact in this region, from the whaling
years ([[topic:whaling]]) through to the tuberculosis evacuations documented by
the Qikiqtani Truth Commission ([[topic:relocations-qtc]]). The Fury and Hecla
were among the first ships to overwinter beside an Inuit community, and an
epidemic followed them.

The crews suffered too. Scurvy appeared among the seamen and marines in the
spring of 1823. Alexander Elder, Greenland mate of the Hecla and a veteran of
all three of Parry's expeditions, died at Iglulik. Parry decided the passage
could not be forced, sent the Hecla home, and brought both ships south; the
Fury was beset and drifted back up Lyon Inlet on the way out, and George Fife
died before they reached England in the autumn of 1823. Five men dead in two
years, against one in the ten months at Melville Island.

## What the Iglulingmiut knew

Parry left convinced of two geographical facts he had been given rather than
found: that Melville Peninsula was a peninsula and the coast turned west
beyond it, and that a strait ran through to a western sea. Both were Inuit
knowledge, drawn on paper by Iligliuk and corroborated by Ewerat, and both were
right. See [[page:iligliuk-and-her-chart]].

He also brought back a report he could not check, and neither can we: the
Iglulingmiut told him that two fishing ships had been wrecked somewhere to the
northward. Whalers were already working the Baffin Bay grounds by 1823
([[topic:whaling]]), and this may be the earliest Inuit testimony about a
European wreck in the region recorded by a naval expedition.
