In 1817 the whaler William Scoresby reported to the Admiralty that the ice of
the Greenland Sea had unaccountably slackened. John Barrow, Second Secretary to
the Admiralty, had a navy with nothing to do after Waterloo and an old idea to
revive: a navigable sea route from Baffin Bay to the Pacific. Between 1818 and
1825 the Admiralty sent five expeditions into the eastern Canadian Arctic under
[[person:John Ross]] and [[person:William Edward Parry]]. They did not find the
passage. They did something else: they put the Canadian Arctic Archipelago on a
chart, they showed that a British ship could winter in the ice and bring her
people home alive, and they spent two winters living alongside the Iglulingmiut
of Winter Island and [[place:Iglulik]] — producing, among much else, the first
map of the Melville Peninsula coast, drawn by an Inuk woman named
[[person:Iligliuk]].

## The five voyages

**1818 — Isabella and Alexander.** Ross commanded, with Parry as lieutenant in
charge of the [[vessel:Alexander]] and [[person:Edward Sabine]] as astronomer.
They crossed Baffin Bay, met the Inughuit near [[place:Cape York]] on 10 August
through the Greenlandic interpreter [[person:John Sacheuse]], and entered
[[place:Lancaster Sound]] on 30 August. The next day Ross saw a chain of
mountains closing the head of the sound, named them Croker's Mountains after the
Secretary of the Admiralty, and turned back. The mountains do not exist. See
[[page:ross-1818-and-the-croker-mountains]].

**1819-20 — Hecla and Griper.** Parry, given his own command, sailed straight
through Ross's mountains, ran west along Barrow Strait and Viscount Melville
Sound, and on 4 September 1819 crossed the meridian of 110° W — winning a
£5,000 parliamentary prize. The two ships were sawn into
[[place:Winter Harbour]] on Melville Island through a canal of four thousand and
eighty-two yards, reaching their berth on 26 September, and stayed frozen in for
ten months. See [[page:parry-at-melville-island-1819-20]].

**1821-23 — Fury and Hecla.** Parry tried a southern door instead: through
Hudson Strait and up the west side of Foxe Basin. Two winters, at
[[place:Winter Island]] (1821-22) and at Iglulik (1822-23), were spent in daily
contact with the Iglulingmiut, with [[person:George Francis Lyon]] commanding
the Hecla and keeping the more candid of the two journals. See
[[page:two-winters-among-the-iglulingmiut]] and
[[page:iligliuk-and-her-chart]].

**1824 — Griper.** Lyon alone, sent to land a party at Repulse Bay and sledge
overland to Point Turnagain. He never got there; the Griper was nearly lost in a
September gale in Roes Welcome Sound and came home having achieved nothing but a
very good account of a bad storm. See [[page:lyon-and-the-griper-1824]].

**1824-25 — Hecla and Fury.** Parry's third voyage, down Prince Regent Inlet. A
miserable season of ice, a winter at Port Bowen, and then on 21 August 1825 the
Fury was driven ashore and had to be abandoned. Her stores, stacked on the beach,
sat there for years. See [[page:the-loss-of-the-fury]].

## What was actually settled

Everything Parry sailed past in 1819 he named, and most of those names are still
on the chart: Barrow Strait, Prince Regent Inlet, Wellington Channel, Melville
Island, Banks Land. The great gap in his 1819 chart — Wellington Channel to the
north, Prince Regent Inlet to the south — set the shape of every search that
followed, including [[topic:franklin-1845]], which used Parry's own route as far
as Beechey Island. [[place:Fury and Hecla Strait]], which the ships reached in
1822 and could not get through because it is choked with ice most years, is
still one of the least navigable straits in the archipelago.

The negative results mattered as much. Ross's Croker Mountains, and the argument
they started, are the reason Parry got a second ship. The Fury's abandoned
stores at [[place:Fury Beach]] kept [[person:John Ross]]'s own crew alive
through the winter of 1832-33 ([[topic:ross-victory]]) — one of the most
improbable survivals in the region's history, and it depended on a wreck.

## The Inuit side of the record

Parry and Lyon's second voyage produced the best ethnographic record of any
British Arctic expedition of the century, and it is a record of named
individuals rather than of a "tribe": Iligliuk, her husband
[[person:Okotook]] and their family; [[person:Arnaneelia]] and
[[person:Aneetka]] at Winter Island; [[person:Toolooak]],
[[person:Kaoongut]], [[person:Oomna]] and [[person:Nakahu]]. Lyon drew many of
them and much of what they did, and the plates are here:
[[artifact:ross-parry-014|Arnaneelia]],
[[artifact:ross-parry-239|Nakahu and his wife Oomna]],
[[artifact:ross-parry-231|the inside of a snow house at Winter Island]],
[[artifact:ross-parry-240|the building of one at Igloolik]],
[[artifact:ross-parry-236|a fan-hitched dog team]],
[[artifact:ross-parry-241|the caribou hunt at a lake crossing]], and the seal
and walrus hunt at the breathing-holes
([[artifact:ross-parry-233|one of four plates]]).

Parry also printed a vocabulary of the Iglulingmiut dialect compiled with Lyon
over the two winters, from [[artifact:ross-parry-270|Air to Bite]] through to
[[artifact:ross-parry-280|Wait to Young]], with two pages of
[[artifact:ross-parry-281|Iglulingmiut place names]] set against the English
ones the ships had just imposed. It is not a scholarly grammar and Parry warns
in his [[artifact:ross-parry-269|prefatory note]] how easily it could mislead,
but it is one of the earliest substantial printed records of the language, and
it holds the word *igloo*, glossed "Hut, or house."

Iligliuk's chart, [[artifact:ross-parry-011|Eskimaux Chart No. 1]], is the
document that matters most. Parry laid down the coast he already knew and asked
her to continue it; she drew Melville Peninsula, brought the coast round to the
west, and put a strait where Fury and Hecla Strait is. He sailed north the
following summer on the strength of it. British Arctic geography of the 1820s
is, in that stretch, Inuit geography written down. See
[[page:iligliuk-and-her-chart]].

The people of Iglulik and Naujaat did not stop existing when the ships left.
Iglulik is a community of about two thousand people today; the Iglulingmiut
place names Parry and Lyon wrote down phonetically — Amitioke for Amitturmiut
country, *Ne-yu-ning Eit-du-a* for Winter Island, Akkoolee for the country to
the west — are living names. See [[topic:inuit-oral-history]].

## Why it matters to this ship

The 1819-20 wintering at Melville Island is the first controlled attempt at
keeping a ship's company healthy through an Arctic winter: Parry's routines of
exercise, ventilation, lemon juice, a fortnightly play and a weekly newspaper
are the direct ancestors of every overwintering protocol since, and the surgeons
wrote up [[artifact:ross-parry-118|how it went]]. Only one man died.

These voyages also left the first long instrumental record from the
archipelago, and most of it is in this collection: the monthly abstracts of the
Hecla's meteorological journal from [[artifact:ross-parry-038|July 1819]] to
[[artifact:ross-parry-048|September 1820]], the Fury's from
[[artifact:ross-parry-247|June 1821]] to
[[artifact:ross-parry-268|September 1823]] at Winter Island and Igloolik, a
[[artifact:ross-parry-113|tide table]] for Winter Harbour, an
[[artifact:ross-parry-090|appendix of magnetic observations]] taken close enough
to the magnetic pole that the compass had to be corrected before it could be
believed, [[artifact:ross-parry-117|pendulum experiments]] for the local
strength of gravity, and, from Ross's voyage, the
[[artifact:ross-parry-227|Isabella's own meteorological register]] of 1818.
Two centuries on, that is a baseline, and it is the unglamorous reason a ship
doing science in these waters still has cause to open these books.
