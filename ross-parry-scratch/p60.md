Parry's third voyage, in 1824-25, was his least successful and his most
consequential. He took [[vessel:HMS Hecla]], gave [[vessel:HMS Fury]] to Captain
[[person:Henry Parkyns Hoppner]], and went back to [[place:Lancaster Sound]] to try the one door he had
left open in 1819: Prince Regent Inlet, running south out of Barrow Strait. The
season's route is [[artifact:ross-parry-004|traced here]]. He
lost a ship, added almost nothing to the chart, and left a pile of provisions on
a beach that kept another expedition's crew alive seven years later.

## A bad year for ice

1824 was a brutal ice year in Baffin Bay. In 1819 Parry had been through the
middle ice and into Lancaster Sound by 1 August; in 1824 the ships did not get
into the sound until the middle of September, with young ice already forming
around them and the season effectively over before it had begun.

Rather than be driven back east, Parry took the ships into Port Bowen on the
east side of Prince Regent Inlet at the end of September and made fast in
sixty-two fathoms, running the hawsers far in on the ice in case it broke off at
the margin. He was frank that this was a decision made out of dread. What they
feared was not danger to the ships or the hazard of being shut out of a winter
harbour but a third thing they dreaded as much: "the too obvious probability of
our once more being driven back to the eastward, should we again become hampered
in the young ice." No known place of security lay to the southward on that coast.
On the way in Parry noticed the cliffs on the south side of the harbour glazed
with a layer of blue transparent ice where snow had thawed and been caught again
by the frost — a sign, he wrote, of how cold the summer had been.

## The winter at Port Bowen, 1824-25

By his third wintering Parry had refined the routine considerably, and had
also decided the theatricals of Melville Island had run their course. In their
place he instituted a **masquerade**, once a month, alternately in each ship,
in which officers and men both took part in costume and nobody was obliged to
join. [[artifact:ross-parry-037|"Ours were masquerades without licentiousness —
carnivals without excess,"]] he
wrote, noting with some pride that they never weakened the respect of the men
toward their superiors.

The more serious innovation was schools. Mr Hooper ran the Hecla's and Mr Mogg
the Fury's, teaching the eighteen or twenty men who could not read; Parry
thought the effect spread through the whole lower deck and credited it, along
with the diet, for the "extraordinary state of health" of that winter.

The science was the best of any of his voyages. The magnetic observations grew
until the ground round the observatory "assumed ere long almost the appearance
of a scattered village," seven or eight detached huts each with its own needle,
sited apart to keep the instruments from disturbing one another. Lieutenant
Foster, needing to place a meridian mark on the far shore, posted a man
halfway across the harbour to relay his shouted instructions and found the
relay unnecessary: he could keep up a conversation with the man at the far
station across a measured 6,696 feet — "about one statute mile and two-tenths" —
of frozen harbour, with the thermometer at -18°, the barometer at 30.14 inches,
and the weather nearly calm and quite clear. It is an offhand paragraph and it is
a real measurement of low-temperature sound propagation, made in 1825.

In the spring the crews spread gravel along the line of a canal so the sun would
melt it through, and when the ice at the harbour mouth drifted off they set every
officer and man to cutting a fresh canal in from the open water to meet it,
through ice five to eight feet thick and in places ten or eleven, seven in the
morning till seven at night, dinner cooked on the ice and eaten under a studding
sail. On the afternoon of 19 July 1825 the floe split across the harbour on its
own and put a welcome stop to the work. Every man then worked twenty-six hours
with half an hour off for supper — fifteen of them overboard at one moment, and
several individuals three times over — until the ships got clear at half past six
the next morning; two hours of towing in a calm brought them to sea on 20 July.

## The Fury driven ashore, 21 August 1825

They crossed Prince Regent Inlet to the western shore and found what the shore
of Somerset Island offers in a bad year: a steep beach, no harbour, and heavy
ice driving down on the land. The Fury was forced aground once, got off,
and was found to be leaking so badly she had to be run ashore deliberately to
be repaired. The two crews built a wall of grounded icebergs to shelter her
and unloaded her stores onto the beach to lighten her.

It failed. Through the night of 20-21 August the ice worked in against the
bergs and the cables. At four in the morning on the 21st another mass came in
and threatened to sweep away every remaining security. At two in the afternoon
Parry saw that the Hecla could no longer be held there "with the smallest
chance of safety," and at a quarter past three he cast off and stood off and
on, leaving Hoppner and every man he could spare aboard the wreck. At twenty
minutes past four —

> being an hour and five minutes after the _Hecla_ had cast off, I was informed
> by signal that the _Fury_ was on shore.

She had been driven up the beach by grounded ice pushing her ahead of it, and
was blocked in on the seaward side by the same masses. See
[[artifact:ross-parry-025|the excerpt from Parry's journal]].

For four days the Hecla stood off and on, unable to get near her in a strong
southerly current. On 25 August Parry went aboard with Hoppner, Lieutenants
Austin and Sherer, and Mr Pulfer, the Fury's carpenter, and ordered a survey —
giving the order the instant they came aboard, so that nobody could take a
lead from his own opinion. They spent several hours going over her. Pulfer's
verdict: five days to pump her out; the pumps would not keep her free even
then; twenty days' work hove down to make her seaworthy, with the means they
had. The officers reported that abandonment was an absolute necessity.

Parry made the signal for the Fury's people to be sent for their clothes, most
of which were already on the beach with the stores. The boats were hauled up,
and he left her at two in the morning; Hoppner, Austin and the last of the men
followed half an hour later. Every one of the Fury's stores stayed where it
was — there was no room in the Hecla for anything but two ships' companies.

Parry fixed the spot: latitude 72° 42' 30" N, longitude 91° 50' 05" W by
chronometer, magnetic dip 88° 19' 22", variation 129° 25' west. It is
[[place:Fury Beach]]. The Hecla came home with both crews in the autumn of
1825. Nobody was lost.

## What the beach became

Casks of flour, sugar, cocoa, spirits, preserved meat and vegetables, coal,
canvas, spars, and the Fury's own boats sat on that shingle in a climate that
preserves things. Seven years later they were found by [[person:John Ross]],
whose privately funded steam vessel Victory had been abandoned in the ice
further south. Ross's men walked north to Fury Beach, built a hut they called
Somerset House over the stores, wintered there in 1832-33, and finally got
away in the Fury's boats — to be picked up in Lancaster Sound by the whaler
Isabella, the same ship Ross had commanded in 1818. The whole of that story is
[[topic:ross-victory]].

So the two ends of this topic close on each other. The officer who saw
mountains across Lancaster Sound in 1818, and lost his career for it, was kept
alive through an Arctic winter by the wreck of a ship belonging to the officer
who had sailed through them. Later searchers for Franklin called at
Fury Beach too, and found the stores still there
([[topic:franklin-search]]).
