# What may have happened, and who said so
[page · kruger-1930 · draft]

Nothing of [[person:Hans Krüger]], [[person:Åge Rose Bjare]] or [[person:Akqioq]] has ever been found. Every account of what became of them is inference, and the honest thing is to say who made each inference, when, and on what. The order below is chronological, because each theory was built on what was known at the time, and what was known changed twice: in 1957, when the Meighen Island note was found, and in 2004, when the cache at [[place:Cape Southwest]] was dug ([[page:kruger-1930-cape-southwest]]).

## The searchers, 1932

**[[person:Harry Stallworthy]]**, in his report at the end of the 1932 patrol ([[artifact:kruger-1930-017]]), thought the party had not gone far out on the sea ice from the north-west corner of [[place:Axel Heiberg Island]]; more likely they had hunted for dog food down the west coast and crossed to [[place:Meighen Island]], and perished "in this district or further south in the vicinity of the Ringnes Islands" in the winter of 1930-31, "as a result of their dogs starving". This is the theory that stood for twenty-five years, and it was half right and half wrong: they did come down the west coast, but they came back to Axel Heiberg after Meighen, and Stallworthy did not believe they had.

**The Inughuit of the patrols** — Stallworthy names them as the joint authors of his opinion — thought the men had died in the winter of 1930-31 after losing their dogs, left without matches during the dark period, and so without heat or drinking water. [[person:Nukapinguaq]] and others also judged that Krüger could not have got down the west coast of Ellesmere in time to cross Jones Sound before break-up, and doubted he had reached Devon Island. Their reasoning was about dogs, fuel and ice — the things that actually kill people there — and it remains the most concrete of the early explanations.

**[[person:R. W. Hamilton]]** ([[artifact:kruger-1930-018]]) concluded that the missing party must be north of the ground he had covered, because they could not have passed his cairns without leaving a record in one. The cache at Cape Southwest is the standing refutation of that particular argument: they passed and left nothing.

## The publicity theory, 1931

**[[person:Rudolph Martin Anderson]]** wondered in 1931 whether Krüger might have "gone into seclusion for a year or two to keep people guessing and provide profitable publicity", after the manner of certain explorers of the modern school — a remark aimed sideways at [[person:Vilhjalmur Stefansson]]. Anderson himself thought it more likely that Krüger had got into trouble. It is recorded here because it was said by a serious man, not because it deserves weight.

## Carbon monoxide, 1957

The Meighen Island note ([[artifact:kruger-1930-012]]) changed the problem. **[[person:Raymond Thorsteinsson]]**, who found it, argued from its casual tone and from the party's rate — better than 240 km in eleven days from [[place:Cape Thomas Hubbard]] — that men and dogs were in good condition on 6 May, which rules out slow starvation on Meighen Island. Something sudden killed them instead. His preferred explanation was carbon monoxide poisoning inside a snow house somewhere on the sea ice between Meighen and Amund Ringnes islands; his alternative was that the sledge broke through thin ice, perhaps near the Hell Gate polynya, and all three drowned.

**Stefansson** agreed in full and thought the monoxide the more probable of the two. He wrote to Thorsteinsson: "To my mind, you have solved the mystery of the Krueger expedition."

**[[person:Geoffrey Hattersley-Smith]]** accepted that carbon monoxide in a tent was possible but doubted a thin-ice drowning in early May, when the ice is still sound; he thought a last camp might one day be found.

**Stallworthy**, writing to Thorsteinsson in November 1957 and giving no reasoning ([[artifact:kruger-1930-020]]), expected the remains would turn up: "their bones, remains of the Komatik and some of the equipment, probably including a heavy sounding line and weights... probably on the Ringnes Islands or at least on land somewhere in the general direction of Southern Ellesmere Island."

Barr notes that a consensus formed around carbon monoxide, and it is worth saying plainly what it rests on: a rate of travel, a tone of voice, and the absence of any better idea. There is no physical evidence for it whatever.

## The hidden plan, 1958

**[[person:Kurt Holler]]**, who had shipped Krüger's supplies, reported more than twenty-five years afterwards that Krüger had an agenda he had not published: if conditions allowed he meant to head out into the Arctic Ocean, and had asked Soviet explorers whether he could count on Russian support for a crossing from northern Ellesmere or Axel Heiberg to Severnaya Zemlya or the Taymyr Peninsula. Holler is also the man to whom Krüger wrote, "I authorize you expressly to point out that any search expedition is totally pointless" ([[artifact:kruger-1930-013]]). Krüger's five-year plan of 1924 had indeed ended with a crossing of the Arctic Ocean towards Bering Strait, and his 1925 revision had him wintering on Lougheed Island and then looking for the land Stefansson believed lay north-west of Ellef Ringnes.

Against this: the Cape Thomas Hubbard note says he was going to Meighen Island, not north, and Stallworthy read it at the time as Krüger having given up the polar sea.

## Deliberate wintering, 1994

**[[person:Randall J. Osczevski]]**, in a letter to *Polar Record* after Barr's paper ([[source:osczevski1994disappearance]]), argued that Krüger had a contingency plan to winter in the Canadian islands, had said as much in the note left for the ship ([[artifact:kruger-1930-022]]), and — having failed at his main object after years of planning — may simply have decided not to go back to Greenland in 1930. He proposed Lougheed Island as the destination, by way of Hassel Sound: Stefansson had called Lougheed "an Arctic paradise" and an ideal wintering place for a small party, and had shown the travel was possible in July and early August, so mid-May was not too late. Osczevski also pointed out that the police had not searched the south coast of Axel Heiberg carefully — neither patrol noticed the large fiord there, and Surprise Fiord was not discovered until 1937.

## The discoverers of the cache, 2004

[[person:John H. England]], [[person:Arthur S. Dyke]], [[person:Randall C. Brooks]] and [[person:James Savelle]], publishing the 1999 find ([[source:brooks2004krugers]]), read the site as a camp left in a hurry, and offered two readings of it: either the party went on southward, or "the team may have gone out hunting for food in desperation but never made it back to the camp, being caught by a late winter storm or falling from exhaustion." They also disposed of one old idea — Thorsteinsson had suggested a last camp might be found at or near Hell Gate, which lies far south of any route home to Bache Peninsula and is now implausible.

[[person:Robert W. Park]] and [[person:Douglas R. Stenton]], having excavated it ([[source:park2007hans]]), read the same material as a carefully built cache rather than a camp, made by men who meant to return for it — and raised a possibility nobody had put in writing before: that the clothing, mug and bowl in the cache mean one of the three was already dead when it was built.

## After the cache, 2010

**[[person:Douglas R. Stenton]], Robert W. Park and [[person:Tara Grant]]** ([[source:stenton2010retracing]]) showed from the rock labels that the party was back on the west coast of Axel Heiberg by 12 May, six days after writing that it was bound for [[place:Cape Sverre]]. To have gone to Amund Ringnes and returned in that time would have required Krüger to double his best rate, so they judge it "highly unlikely" that he ever reached the island — which disposes of the whole family of theories that placed the loss on the ice west of the Ringnes group, and of Thorsteinsson's snow house between Meighen and Amund Ringnes.

What is left is a party that turned back from a stated objective, came 400 km south, cached its instruments and every rock it had collected about 20 May 1930 near Cape Southwest, and then disappeared — in late May, on ice that had about four weeks left in it, with somewhere between fifteen and seventeen dogs at the start of the journey and no reported game killed along the way.

## The condition of the men

No theory should be read without it. Both Europeans had been seriously ill in the winter of 1929-30 at Nequi, probably with trichinosis contracted from raw frozen dog meat eaten on a bad autumn journey; [[person:Hans Nielsen]] and [[person:Dr Mogens]] found them in November 1929 emaciated, weak and scarcely able to walk, Krüger trembling in every limb. Constable [[person:N. M. McLean]]'s Bache Peninsula diary for 14 March 1930 has Krüger "suffering with severe cramps & vomiting all day". The returning support drivers said Krüger was often in pain from his stomach and was ill-tempered and impatient, and that Bjare had a frozen foot. Akqioq sent word back that he did not think either man would survive a long journey, and went on anyway ([[artifact:kruger-1930-015]]).

A sick leader, a lame companion, one overloaded sledge and a shrinking dog team account for a great deal without any accident at all.
