# The last notes
[page · kruger-1930 · draft]

Four documents in Krüger's hand are known. One he left behind at the police post; three he put into other men's cairns as he went west, in German, signed by all three of the party. They are the whole archive of the expedition after 19 March 1930, and they were recovered over twenty-seven years by three different men who were not looking for them.

## The practice

Sledge travellers in these islands left records in cairns and copied out what they found, so that a cairn became a slow correspondence between parties years apart. [[person:Robert Edwin Peary]] built cairns on the north-west capes in 1906, [[person:Donald Baxter MacMillan]] added to them in 1914, [[person:Vilhjalmur Stefansson]] built one on Meighen Island at his landfall on 15 June 1916. Krüger followed the etiquette exactly: at [[place:Cape Thomas Hubbard]] he copied out Peary's and MacMillan's records and left the copies with his own note; when Stefansson's paper on Meighen Island proved too rotten to unfold, he said so in writing rather than force it.

The rule has a corollary that mattered enormously in 1932: a party that passes a known cairn and leaves nothing is presumed not to have passed. Both [[person:Harry Stallworthy]] and [[person:R. W. Hamilton]] reasoned from empty cairns to Krüger's absence, and both were wrong at least once ([[page:kruger-1930-searches]]).

## The note left for the ship, 19 March 1930

Before leaving [[place:Bache Peninsula]] Krüger wrote out what the summer ship should think if he was not there ([[artifact:kruger-1930-022]]): they might be at Dundas Harbour, or "prevented by open water", in which case "we will stay the summer somewhere and reach Bache after the freeze-up" and go on to Thule and south with the February mail. It is a plain statement that he was equipped to be out for a second winter, and it is one of the two documents on which the wintering theory rests.

## Near Cape Colgate, 22 April 1930

[[artifact:kruger-1930-010]] — found in 1954, and the last of the three to come to light. Two geologists, [[person:Robert G. Christie]] and [[person:Geoffrey Hattersley-Smith]], sledging south-west from the Defence Research Board base near Ward Hunt Island, opened a cairn Peary had built in 1906 at the most westerly point of Ellesmere Island and found a message soldered into an empty film canister, wrapped in pieces of old kamik and lashed with walrus-hide thongs. They took it and left a copy. It reads, in translation:

> The German Arctic Expedition, coming from Nerke in Northern Greenland via Bay Fiord, reached this cairn built by Peary on 22 April 1930; no message found. We are proceeding on to the northern tip of Heiberg Island. One sledge, 17 dogs and 3 men in good condition.

Signed Åge Rose Bjare, H. K. E. Krüger (Leader), Akqioq. A facsimile is [[artifact:kruger-1930-029]]. "Three men in good condition" is the only medical statement in the record after the illnesses of the winter, and it is Krüger's own. Where exactly the cairn stands is still argued: Barr puts it on a cape south of Cape Colgate just north of Krüger Island, others about 6 km west of the cape.

## Cape Thomas Hubbard, 24 April 1930

[[artifact:kruger-1930-011]] — the only trace either search ever found, taken from the cairn by Stallworthy on 9 April 1932 after [[person:Kahdi]] spotted it from below:

> The German Arctic Expedition reached this cairn on 24 April 1930 and found notes by Peary and MacMillan, copies of which are attached. We have come from Lands Lokk and are proceeding to Meighen Island.

Stallworthy had little German but got the sense of it, copied all three documents, left the copies with a record of his own visit, and carried the originals home ([[artifact:kruger-1930-028]]). It was the first news of the missing men in two years, and it told Ottawa where they had been going — but not, as it turned out, where they went.

## Andersen Point, Meighen Island, 6 May 1930

[[artifact:kruger-1930-012]] — found on 1 June 1957 by [[person:Raymond Thorsteinsson]] of the Geological Survey of Canada, who was going round [[place:Meighen Island]] on Operation Franklin and climbed to Stefansson's cairn ([[artifact:kruger-1930-007]]) expecting Stefansson's message:

> The German Arctic Expedition, coming from the northern tip of Heiberg Land, reached this point on 5 May 1930 and found Stefansson's note. A copy could not be made, since the note is in such poor condition that it could not be opened without destroying it. We are proceeding on to Cape Sverre on Amund Ringnes Island. May 6, 1930.

All three men signed it ([[artifact:kruger-1930-008]]). A cleaner facsimile ([[artifact:kruger-1930-043]]) shows the German as Krüger wrote it ([[artifact:kruger-1930-047]]) and the three signatures at the foot: Åge Rose Bjare in one hand, AKQIOQ in capitals in another, and H. K. E. Krüger with *Leiter* — leader — written under his name. Thorsteinsson wrote to Stefansson from Eureka on 10 July without waiting to get back to Ottawa; Stefansson had by then been waiting twenty-seven years to learn what became of the man he had advised in 1924.

## What the notes do not say

They give no complaint, no shortage, no sick man. They are terse, formal, and in the third person. Krüger recorded dogs and men "in good condition" on 22 April and made about 30 km the next day; on the evidence of the rock labels his rate then halved. The notes were written for other travellers, not for the people who would eventually read them, and that is worth remembering when they are used, as they always have been, as evidence about a state of mind.
