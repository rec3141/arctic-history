# Into the Queen Elizabeth Islands, March to May 1930
[page · kruger-1930 · draft]

The route is [[artifact:kruger-1930-001|track 001]], and every waypoint on it says whether it is known or inferred. It is worth being clear about where the certainty comes from, because for eighty years the map of this journey was drawn from three notes, and since 2010 it has been drawn from something better: the labels Krüger tied to his rocks.

## What is known, and how

Krüger was a field geologist and he worked like one. Every specimen he picked up got a slip of paper about 11 cm by 4.75 cm, folded, with a catalogue number in the upper left, the date in the lower left and the place in the middle, written in pencil, in German, with the month in Roman numerals and existing English place names left in English. Eighty-three specimens survived in his cache at [[place:SbJk-1]]; the Canadian Conservation Institute opened the labels one at a time between 2004 and 2009, and twenty-three of them still carry a legible date ([[page:kruger-1930-cape-southwest]]). Together with the three cairn notes they fix the party at about fifteen points between 3 April and 15 May 1930. Everything between those points is a straight line drawn by a later hand.

## Out from Bache Peninsula

The party reached the police post at [[place:Bache Peninsula]] on 12 March 1930, having crossed Smith Sound from Greenland with two hired support sledges. Constable [[person:N. M. McLean]] inspected the Scientists' and Explorers' Licences, saw that the dogs were in poor condition and asked them to rest. His diary for 14 March says: "Dr. Krueger suffering with severe cramps & vomiting all day." Krüger left a note behind for whoever should come with the summer ship ([[artifact:kruger-1930-022]]), saying that if they were not at Bache when it called they would "stay the summer somewhere" and come out after the freeze-up — the first written sign that he was prepared to be away for more than one season.

They left on 19 March. [[person:Ilkoo]] and [[person:Kahlugnak]] went with them as far as [[place:Depot Point]] in [[place:Eureka Sound]], about 31 March, and turned back with five boxes of rock and the echo sounder. Ilkoo's account of the sledge at that parting ([[artifact:kruger-1930-014]]) lists the load: pemmican for men and dogs, tea, a case of ammunition, two rifles, fifteen gallons of coal oil, primus lamps, deerskin bags, a double tent, snowshoes, skis, heavy scientific instruments, and a deep-sea sounding wire, "very heavy". One komatik. Fifteen dogs, by his count; seventeen by Krüger's own note a month later.

## North and west, April

The labels pick them up two days later. On 3 or 4 April they were at a place Krüger called "Cape Mokka" on the Axel Heiberg side; on 8 April on the [[place:Schei Peninsula]]; some days after that on its west side. Then, on 18 April, three specimens are labelled Grant Land — the north-west corner of Ellesmere Island — from the south side of [[place:Otto Fiord]] and a headland Krüger named "Kap Bjare". He had crossed [[place:Nansen Sound]] eastward, away from his line of travel.

Stenton, Park and Grant offer the plainest explanation: rough ice. When [[person:Harry Stallworthy]] came up Nansen Sound two years later he was forced right over to the mouth of Otto Fiord by the same kind of ice, and had to travel the Ellesmere shore. Krüger's detour, which looked eccentric while the cairn notes were all we had, is what the sound does to a heavy sledge.

On 22 April he was back on the west side at Peary's 1906 cairn near [[place:Cape Colgate]], where he left the first surviving note and collected at least ten specimens from a hill he called the Hegh-Berg. On 24 April, having made about 30 km a day, he was at [[place:Cape Thomas Hubbard]] and left the second note. On 25 April there are labels from the northern tip of Axel Heiberg Island — the cape now named for Stallworthy — and the first appearance of the coordinate he used from then on: *Heiberg West*, kilometres measured south along the west coast.

## Meighen Island, May

"Heiberg West km 116" is dated 1 May: seven days for 116 km, about 16.5 km a day, half his earlier rate. On 4 May he was on [[place:Perley Island]], which nobody suspected until the labels were read. On 5 May the party reached [[place:Andersen Point]] on [[place:Meighen Island]] and opened Stefansson's cairn of 1916 ([[artifact:kruger-1930-007]]); the message inside was too rotten to unfold. On 6 May Krüger left his own note in its place ([[artifact:kruger-1930-012]], [[artifact:kruger-1930-008]]): they were going on to [[place:Cape Sverre]] on [[place:Amund Ringnes Island]].

Then the record does something unexpected. Label 212, dated 12 May, is marked *Heiberg West*: six days after writing that note, the party was back on the west coast of Axel Heiberg Island, somewhere between Middle Fiord and South Fiord. On 15 May two labels put them at [[place:Cape Levvel]]. A label from [[place:Skrugar Point]] has lost its date. The last of them is from Cape (South?)West.

Stenton and his colleagues judge that a round trip to Cape Sverre and back in those six days would have required Krüger to double the best rate he had yet made, and conclude that it is "highly unlikely that they reached Amund Ringnes Island" — that he set out south as he said, met something, and turned. What that something was is the subject of [[page:kruger-1930-theories]].

## The end of the line

About 20 May 1930, some 10 km east of [[place:Cape Southwest]] at the southern end of Axel Heiberg Island, they stopped and cached everything: the transit and compass, canvas, rope, buttons, clothing, a mug, a bowl, and eighty-odd rocks in a wooden box, canvas bags and a large metal can. That is the last position of the German Arctic Expedition that anyone can prove. They were then about 550 km from Bache Peninsula by the route they had come, in the second half of May, with the sea ice about to begin going out.
