# The lost German expedition of Hans Krüger, 1930
[page · kruger-1930 · draft]

Three men left the police post at [[place:Bache Peninsula]] on the east coast of Ellesmere Island on 19 March 1930 with one sledge and about seventeen dogs, and were never seen again. They were [[person:Hans Krüger]], a German geologist of 43; [[person:Åge Rose Bjare]], a Dane in his twenties who had worked in Greenland; and [[person:Akqioq]], an Inughuk hunter from the Thule district who had travelled with Rasmussen's Fifth Thule Expedition. Their route ran west across Ellesmere Island, north up [[place:Nansen Sound]], round the top of [[place:Axel Heiberg Island]] and out to [[place:Meighen Island]] — ground the CCGS Amundsen's own working area touches every season.

What survives of them is four pieces of paper, a scatter of pencilled rock labels, and a cache of instruments on a beach.

## The plan

Krüger was a geologist at the Technische Hochschule at Darmstadt who had published a five-year Arctic plan in *Petermanns Mitteilungen* in the autumn of 1924 and had written to [[person:Vilhjalmur Stefansson]] for encouragement. He got it, along with a warning about the parts of the polar sea where there is nothing to hunt ([[artifact:kruger-1930-019]]). He led two seasons of real geological work in West Greenland, in 1925 and 1929 ([[artifact:kruger-1930-023]]), and was elected a Fellow of the Royal Geographical Society in November 1928. Canada issued him and Bjare Scientists' and Explorers' Licences 141 and 142 on 31 May 1929.

The published aim was geology: a traverse of the Sverdrup Islands collecting rock, with soundings run between the islands to find the edge of the continental shelf. His friend [[person:Kurt Holler]] said afterwards that there was more to it, and that Krüger meant to strike out across the Arctic Ocean. Krüger's own map ([[artifact:kruger-1930-027]]) and his instruction that "any search expedition is totally pointless" ([[artifact:kruger-1930-013]]) have been read both ways ever since; see [[page:kruger-1930-theories]].

He and Bjare wintered with the Inughuit at Nequi in [[place:Robertson Fjord]], where both fell ill in November 1929 and the Thule manager and a doctor had to come north to them. In March 1930 they crossed Smith Sound, hired Akqioq, and started west.

## The journey and the notes

Six weeks of the journey can be followed almost day by day, because Krüger wrote the date and the place on every rock he picked up. The route is drawn as [[artifact:kruger-1930-001|the 1930 track]], with each waypoint marked as known or inferred. The fixed points are three cairn notes: near [[place:Cape Colgate]] on 22 April ([[artifact:kruger-1930-010]]), at [[place:Cape Thomas Hubbard]] on 24 April ([[artifact:kruger-1930-011]]), and at [[place:Andersen Point]] on Meighen Island on 6 May ([[artifact:kruger-1930-012]]), which ends: *We are proceeding on to Cape Sverre on Amund Ringnes Island.* Nothing of the party has ever been found at [[place:Cape Sverre]]. See [[page:kruger-1930-journey]] and [[page:kruger-1930-cairn-notes]].

## The searches

The [[topic:rcmp-high-arctic-patrols|Royal Canadian Mounted Police]] searched from Bache Peninsula and [[place:Dundas Harbour]] for two springs. [[person:Harry Stallworthy]] failed to cross Ellesmere Island in 1931 ([[artifact:kruger-1930-002]]); in 1932 he took eight teams and 124 dogs round the north end of Axel Heiberg Island and down its whole west coast ([[artifact:kruger-1930-003]]), [[person:R. W. Hamilton]] took the south coast and Cornwall Island ([[artifact:kruger-1930-004]]), and [[person:P. Dersch]] tried Devon Island from Dundas Harbour ([[artifact:kruger-1930-005]]). Between them they covered several thousand kilometres on starving dogs, and found one note. Every one of these patrols was driven by Inughuit drivers — [[person:Nukapinguaq]], [[person:Qaavigarsuaq]], [[person:Inuetuk]], [[person:Kahdi]], [[person:Eetookashoo]], [[person:Kahkachoo]], [[person:Seekeeunguaq]] — and Stallworthy's own report says the conclusions were as much theirs as his. See [[page:kruger-1930-searches]].

## What came afterwards

Two of the three notes were found by geologists, not policemen: the Cape Colgate note by [[person:Robert G. Christie]] and [[person:Geoffrey Hattersley-Smith]] in 1954, and the Meighen Island note by [[person:Raymond Thorsteinsson]] on 1 June 1957. In 1999 a site was found about 10 km east of [[place:Cape Southwest]] on southern Axel Heiberg Island, and excavated in 2004: [[place:SbJk-1]], Krüger's cache of instruments, clothing and some eighty rock specimens, with the labels still readable after 74 years ([[page:kruger-1930-cape-southwest]]). It proved that the party had come 400 km back south after Meighen Island, along a coast Stallworthy had searched and thought they never reached.

The names on the chart came later: Krüger Island and Bjare Strait in 1955, Akqioq Point, Cape Stallworthy. The case also left a rule. In 1953, when two Canadian geologists tried to hire a single Inughuk guide, they were told they must take two, because people remembered that Akqioq had gone west with two white men and had not come back ([[artifact:kruger-1930-021]]).
