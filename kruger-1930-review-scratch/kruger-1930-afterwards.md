# What the case left behind
[page · kruger-1930 · draft]

A disappearance leaves things behind even when it leaves no bodies: names on a chart, a rule about how to travel, an argument about who should pay, and a family at Etah that had to go on without a hunter.

## Akqioq's people

[[person:Akqioq]] left a wife, [[person:Arnanguaq]], who had travelled with him from Thule to Repulse Bay and back on Rasmussen's Fifth Thule Expedition and was at [[place:Etah]] when he did not return. As soon as there was light in 1931, [[person:Harry Stallworthy]] sent [[person:Nukapinguaq]] across Smith Sound to ask whether Akqioq had come home some other way — by [[place:Makinson Inlet]], or landed at Disko and carried north by schooner. He had not, and the record notes that his family and friends were anxious. That is very nearly all the archive says about the household that lost him.

Barr took some trouble to establish that Akqioq was not the same man as [[person:Ahkeeoo]], the Bache Peninsula post servant dismissed as unsatisfactory in March 1930. The two names had been run together for decades, and the effect was to leave an impression that Krüger's Inughuit companion was an unreliable man. He was one of the most experienced sledge travellers in the north: Crocker Land, King Christian Island, the Fifth Thule Expedition, a Danish gold medal.

## A rule about travelling

In 1953 [[person:Geoffrey Hattersley-Smith]] and [[person:Robert G. Blackadar]] went to Greenland to hire one Inughuk with a dog team for a sledge journey along the north coast of Ellesmere. They were obliged to take two ([[artifact:kruger-1930-021]]). The reason given was that people remembered Akqioq had gone west with two white men and had not come back, and that it was "not good" for a single Inughuk to travel with two Europeans.

Twenty-three years after the event, the loss had become a piece of practical knowledge, held and enforced by the community that had paid for it — a rule for hiring, and about the position a lone Inuk is put in when the decisions are made by other people. It is one of the clearest instances in this whole database of Inuit oral knowledge outliving and correcting the written record ([[topic:inuit-oral-history]]).

## Names

The chart carries all three men. Krüger Island and Bjare Strait were named in 1955 at Hattersley-Smith's suggestion after the 1954 find; Krüger has a river on [[place:Meighen Island]] and Bjare a bay there. Akqioq has the main icecap of [[place:Axel Heiberg Island]], named at Fritz Müller's suggestion, and Akqioq Point on the east side of Bjare Bay, proposed by [[person:Keith Arnold]] in 1963. [[place:Cape Stallworthy]], at the north end of Axel Heiberg, is named for the man who searched.

## The bill, and the silence

The Royal Canadian Mounted Police costed the two seasons of searching at $288.50 above regular pay and maintenance, and asked to be reimbursed. Neither the German government nor the State of Hesse would contribute. In January 1934 the German Consul-General [[person:L. Kempff]] conveyed "the sincere thanks of the German Government for their unselfishness, readiness and bravery in undertaking the search for the lost explorers", and that was the end of it. Stallworthy, [[person:R. W. Hamilton]] and [[person:P. Dersch]] were recommended for the King's Police Medal in November 1933.

The specimens the support drivers had brought back from [[place:Depot Point]] — five boxes of them, with the Behm echo sounder — were exhibited beside Alfred Wegener's relics at the Treptow Observatory in Berlin in January 1933, and then went to the Technische Hochschule at Darmstadt, which was destroyed in the war.

## Why it still matters here

The ship works the water this happened on. [[place:Eureka Sound]], [[place:Nansen Sound]], [[place:Meighen Island]] and the coasts of Axel Heiberg are the Amundsen's own ground, and the two things that killed the expedition — ice that will not let a party go where it means to, and the absence of game where the maps show land — are still the operative facts of travel there.

There is also unfinished work in it. [[source:stenton2010retracing|Stenton, Park and Grant]] end their paper with a list of places where a Krüger cairn could still be standing unnoticed: [[place:Schei Peninsula]], [[place:Otto Fiord]], [[place:Perley Island]], a point between Middle Fiord and South Fiord, [[place:Cape Levvel]], [[place:Skrugar Point]], possibly Cape Maunday Thursday, [[place:Cape Southwest]]. Anyone ashore at one of those places with an hour to spare is standing in the search area of an eighty-year-old missing-persons case.

And it is a case with living relatives on both sides of Pikialasorsuaq, the North Water: Inughuit families at Qaanaaq and Siorapaluk descend from the men who drove those sledges — [[person:Nukapinguaq]], [[person:Inuetuk]], [[person:Qaavigarsuaq]], [[person:Kahdi]], [[person:Eetookashoo]], [[person:Kahkachoo]], [[person:Seekeeunguaq]], [[person:Ilkoo]], [[person:Kahlugnak]] — and from Akqioq himself. The German geologist's name is on the islands; theirs are mostly on nothing at all.
