# The cache at Cape Southwest: site SbJk-1
[page · kruger-1930 · draft]

On 3 July 1999 three geologists — [[person:John H. England]], [[person:Arthur S. Dyke]] and the undergraduate [[person:Michelle Laurie]] — were surveying raised marine shorelines on the south coast of [[place:Axel Heiberg Island]], halfway between [[place:Cape Southwest]] and the mouth of Surprise Fiord, when they came on a scatter of objects "of considerable antiquity" on the first relict beach above the active one ([[artifact:kruger-1930-039]]). The site is ten metres across, half a metre above high tide, without vegetation, in country with no prominent landmarks. It was given the Borden designation [[place:SbJk-1]].

They took only two things away: a small compass, lying face up and almost entirely buried in the centre of the site, and a wooden box containing a mountain transit ([[artifact:kruger-1930-040]]). [[person:Randall C. Brooks]] identified the instrument as a Max Hildebrand of Freiberg in Saxony, and dated it from the maker's inscription ([[artifact:kruger-1930-041]]) to after 1889 and before about 1921, when Hildebrand absorbed the Wichmann brothers and changed the wording. That was the right bracket for Krüger's outfit; the rock samples said the camp was a geologist's; and the place said whose ([[source:brooks2004krugers]]).

In 2004 [[person:Douglas R. Stenton]], [[person:Robert W. Park]] and [[person:Tara Grant]] excavated the site for the Government of Nunavut, confirmed the attribution ([[source:park2007hans]]), and in 2010 published what the paper in it said ([[source:stenton2010retracing]]). It is the only physical trace of the German Arctic Expedition that has ever been found, and it changed the shape of the case.

## What was there

A compass and a transit. Fragments of canvas and rope, buttons, articles of clothing, pieces of wooden boxes. A mug and a bowl. And eighty-three geological specimens: fifty-seven lying on the surface, where a bear had got at the wooden box and the canvas bags soon after the cache was made, and twenty-six more in better condition inside a large metal can.

Most of the specimens were still wrapped in paper, and inside the wrapping of each was the label Krüger had written when he picked the rock up: a slip about 11 cm by 4.75 cm, folded, with a catalogue number in the upper left, the collection date in the lower left, the locality in the middle. German, with English for names that were already English on the charts, and months in Roman numerals.

## Reading the labels

Seventy-four years of Arctic weather had done their work. The paper was, in the authors' description, "covered with sand, dirt, rust stains and concretions from the tin can and, in some cases, black mould"; dry it was brittle and broke when handled, wet it had no strength and tore, and the repeated wetting and drying had made the fibres reform "like papier mâché" into new layers. All but five labels were damaged.

The Canadian Conservation Institute opened them one at a time, each taking between one and three days. Labels in good condition were opened dry; the worse ones were opened wet, backed with Japanese tissue and an acrylic adhesive, and eased apart under magnification with a scalpel, then dried between blotters under weights. Rust-bound and stained labels were treated in a chelating solution and rinsed; tears were repaired with conservation tissue and wheat starch paste. Then each was photographed in infrared to bring up the pencil.

Twenty-three came out with a legible date. They cover 43 days, from 3 April to 15 May 1930, and they put the expedition at ten places nobody had known it visited.

## What they showed

The three cairn notes ([[page:kruger-1930-cairn-notes]]) had told a simple story: north up the west side, round the top, out to [[place:Meighen Island]], off towards [[place:Amund Ringnes Island]], gone. The labels tell a more complicated one ([[page:kruger-1930-journey]]).

- Krüger crossed [[place:Nansen Sound]] to the Ellesmere shore in mid-April and spent several days there, at [[place:Otto Fiord]] and a headland he named for Bjare. The likely reason is ice: Stallworthy was pushed the same way in 1932.
- He stopped at the [[place:Schei Peninsula]], at [[place:Perley Island]], at points down the west coast of Axel Heiberg he measured as kilometres from the north cape.
- After 6 May on Meighen Island he was back on the Axel Heiberg coast by 12 May, at [[place:Cape Levvel]] on 15 May, at [[place:Skrugar Point]] a day or two later, and at Cape Southwest about 20 May.

That last stretch is the point. [[person:Harry Stallworthy]] searched the whole of that coast in April 1932, found MacMillan's 1916 cairn at Cape Southwest with messages from MacMillan and from [[person:Alfred Herbert Joy]] in it and nothing from Krüger, and concluded that the missing party had never come that way ([[artifact:kruger-1930-017]]). They had. They had camped and cached within 10 km of him, and left no note.

## Camp or cache?

The discoverers read the site as a camp abandoned in a hurry, and wondered whether the party had gone out hunting in desperation and never come back, caught by a late storm or by exhaustion. Park and Stenton, after digging it, disagreed: the material had been placed deliberately and carefully, and what they were looking at was a cache ([[artifact:kruger-1930-044]], [[artifact:kruger-1930-046]]). No bones and no food refuse were found, and no sledge, firearms or tent poles — the party went on.

Read that way it is a decision, not an accident: men lightening a sledge that had been overloaded from the start ([[artifact:kruger-1930-014]]) and putting the heavy, valuable things at a prominent and well-known landmark they could find again. Krüger was a geologist; the specimens were the expedition. Leaving them means he meant to come back for them.

The one detail that will not sit quietly is the clothing. Among the cached material were a garment with its buttons still on ([[artifact:kruger-1930-045]]), a mug and a bowl. Park and Stenton suggest this may mean that one of the three men had already died before the party reached Axel Heiberg Island, and that what was cached included a dead man's kit. It is an inference from a small assemblage, and they say so.

## Where to look now

The paper ends with a list of places any future search should think about, because a cairn at any of them could have been overlooked: [[place:Schei Peninsula]], Otto Fiord, Perley Island, an unknown point between Middle Fiord and South Fiord, Cape Levvel, Skrugar Point, possibly Cape Maunday Thursday, and Cape Southwest. The authors regard it as "highly unlikely" that the party ever reached Amund Ringnes Island, which is where seventy years of searching, from [[person:Raymond Thorsteinsson]]'s 1958 landing at [[place:Cape Sverre]] onwards, has been aimed.

For a ship working these waters, that is the practical residue of the whole story: the ground most likely to hold a last camp is the south-west corner of Axel Heiberg Island and the sea ice between it and [[place:Cornwall Island]] — not the Ringnes islands.
