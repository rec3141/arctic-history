# Krüger, Bjare and Akqioq
[page · kruger-1930 · draft]

The party that left [[place:Bache Peninsula]] on 19 March 1930 was three men, one sledge and about seventeen dogs. Two of them are known in some detail because Germany and Denmark kept their papers; the third is known mostly through other men's reports of him, and is the one who had been furthest north.

## Hans Krüger

[[person:Hans Krüger]] was born at Lissa in Posen — now Leszno, in Poland — on 24 June 1886, the son of a factory director. He read law at Jena because his father wished it, and then left Europe altogether: from about 1910 he was in German South West Africa, prospecting in the Kalahari and the Namib and managing a vanadium mine near Tsumeb. In the war he fought in the colonial forces and afterwards as a guerrilla leader alongside rebel Boers, was condemned to death, escaped, was recaptured and pardoned. He came back to Germany in 1923, took a Heidelberg doctorate on the geology of West Greenland, and attached himself to the Technische Hochschule at Darmstadt.

From 1924 he planned nothing but the Arctic. His five-year scheme appeared in *Petermanns Mitteilungen* that autumn; he wrote to [[person:Vilhjalmur Stefansson]] for advice and got both encouragement and a warning that parts of the polar sea are "badly supplied, or not at all, with animal life" ([[artifact:kruger-1930-019]]). He led the Hessische Westgrönland Expedition of 1925 with the geographer [[person:Fritz Klute]], crossing the Nugssuaq peninsula and working the coal seams, and went back to West Greenland in 1929 ([[artifact:kruger-1930-023]]). The Royal Geographical Society elected him a Fellow in November 1928. He signed his letters to Stefansson, for reasons nobody has explained, "F. C. Krüger".

His photograph ([[artifact:kruger-1930-009]]) is a formal one from his own university's archive. He was 43 when he disappeared, engaged to [[person:Hilda Schad von Mittelbiberach]], a director's secretary at Merck in Darmstadt, and living between expeditions with his mother [[person:Olga Krüger]] at Bensheim.

## Åge Rose Bjare

[[person:Åge Rose Bjare]] joined at Copenhagen on 1 June 1929 as guide, interpreter and boatman; he ran the motor boat of the second Greenland season. Barr calls him a Dane and notes that the name looks Norwegian. What is certain is that he had years of travelling and hunting in West Greenland behind him, which is what Krüger wanted. He is the younger of the two men in the only photograph of them together ([[artifact:kruger-1930-025]]), taken in the summer of 1929.

He left Bache Peninsula with a frozen foot. Bjare Bay on Meighen Island and Bjare Strait off north-west Ellesmere carry his name; nothing else of him has ever been found.

## Akqioq

[[person:Akqioq]] was about 38, from the Thule district, and by 1930 one of the most travelled Inughuit of his generation. He had crossed Ellesmere Island several times with [[person:Donald Baxter MacMillan]]'s Crocker Land Expedition ([[topic:crocker-land-1913]]), reached King Christian Island in the spring of 1916, gone north with Fitzhugh Green to Cape Constitution in 1915 and south to Clarence Head in 1917. MacMillan called him a crack shot and one of the best travellers at Etah. Rasmussen took him and his wife [[person:Arnanguaq]] on the Fifth Thule Expedition from 1921 to 1924, for which the Danish government gave him a gold medal ([[topic:karluk-cae]]); the only picture of him is from those years ([[artifact:kruger-1930-026]]). [[person:Alfred Herbert Joy]] of the police, who knew the country and its people as well as any white man then alive, called him a first-class hunter, an excellent dog driver and a good and cheerful worker.

He is not to be confused with [[person:Ahkeeoo]], the post servant at Bache Peninsula who was dismissed as unsatisfactory that same month. Barr takes some trouble over this, because for sixty years the two names were run together and it left an impression that Krüger's companion was unreliable. He was not.

The one thing we have from Akqioq himself is a message he sent back from [[place:Depot Point]] with the two support drivers, and it is a judgement and a decision at once ([[artifact:kruger-1930-015]]): he did not think either white man would come back from a long journey — Bjare had frozen his toes, and Krüger had been spitting blood on a hill — "but he (Akqioq) would not leave the two men in the lurch."

## The two who turned back

[[person:Ilkoo]] and [[person:Kahlugnak]] drove the support sledges from Smith Sound to Depot Point in Eureka Sound, then turned for home with five boxes of rock specimens and the expedition's Behm echo sounder, reaching Bache Peninsula on 11 April 1930 and leaving for Greenland two days later. Their names are recorded three ways each — Joy has Ilkoo or Ilkloo and Kahlugnak or Kahlagnah, Constable [[person:N. M. McLean]] wrote Ego and Qagartsiaq — which is a fair measure of how the record handled Inughuit names. Joy interviewed Ilkoo in August 1931, and his description of the sledge is the last eyewitness account of the expedition there will ever be ([[artifact:kruger-1930-014]]): so heavily loaded that the men could hardly move it, Krüger walking ahead, Bjare pushing behind, Akqioq pushing at the side and whipping the dogs.

## Afterwards

Krüger's mother went on writing to him. Her short pencilled note and Hilda Schad's letter of nearly three foolscap pages — "I shall never be free of you in my life again" — went north on the Beothic in 1931, could not be delivered, and came south again to be filed in Ottawa, where they are. Olga Krüger died at Bensheim in 1937. Hilda Schad survived the war, did not marry, and took poison in 1946. Arnanguaq was at Etah when Akqioq did not come back.
