# The police searches, 1931-1932
[page · kruger-1930 · draft]

Nobody worried in 1930. Krüger had said he might be out a second winter, had told his agent that "any search expedition is totally pointless" ([[artifact:kruger-1930-013]]), and had left a note at [[place:Bache Peninsula]] saying he might come out by Thule with the February mail ([[artifact:kruger-1930-022]]). When the Beothic reached [[place:Fram Havn]] on 11 August 1930 and found he had not returned, [[person:G. P. Mackenzie]] cabled Ottawa and went on; a week later he cabled from Dundas Harbour, "Dundas nineteenth all well stop Krueger not here." His fiancée [[person:Hilda Schad von Mittelbiberach]] cabled in September asking whether the ship had brought him home, and [[person:O. S. Finnie]]'s reply, Barr writes, was strangely lacking in detail.

The searching was done by the [[topic:rcmp-high-arctic-patrols|Royal Canadian Mounted Police]] out of Bache Peninsula and [[place:Dundas Harbour]], and by Inughuit drivers hired for it. It took two springs, cost twenty-nine dogs and sixteen muskoxen, covered something over three thousand miles, and found one piece of paper.

## 1931: Stallworthy cannot get across

[[person:Harry Stallworthy]] took over the Bache Peninsula post in the summer of 1930. As soon as there was light he sent [[person:Nukapinguaq]] across to [[place:Etah]] to ask whether [[person:Akqioq]] had come home by another road; he had not, and his family were anxious ([[artifact:kruger-1930-002|track 002]]).

On 29 March 1931 Stallworthy left with Nukapinguaq and [[person:Inuetuk]] to cross Ellesmere Island by the Flagler Glacier and search the west coast. The spring was almost snowless; days of hauling over rock and gravel at the head of [[place:Flagler Bay]] ended with every route onto the glacier reconnoitred and abandoned. He turned round, went down the east coast instead as far as [[place:Craig Harbour]], and found nothing — which at least ruled out Krüger having come out that way. On 21 April, hunting a bear on a glacier snout near Boger Point, he broke through a snow bridge and jammed nine metres down; Nukapinguaq and Inuetuk dropped him a harpoon line and hauled him out.

That August, at Fram Havn, [[person:Alfred Herbert Joy]] cancelled the plan to close the Bache Peninsula detachment and ordered a full search for the spring of 1932: two parties of an officer and two Inughuit with three teams each, plus support teams, to cover western Ellesmere, [[place:Eureka Sound]], [[place:Axel Heiberg Island]] and as much of the Ringnes islands as could be reached. He arranged for [[person:Eetookashoo]] and [[person:Qaavigarsuaq]] to come over from Greenland in February, and for the news to be broadcast north to the post's receiver by KDKA Pittsburgh and CKY Winnipeg.

## 1932: the great patrols

The Greenland teams arrived on 15 March 1932 — [[person:Kahkachoo]] and [[person:Kahdi]] bringing Eetookashoo, Qaavigarsuaq and Inuetuk, their wives and seven children, after a detour round an unusually far-north arm of the North Water. With them the post could field eight teams and 124 dogs.

The whole convoy left on 20 March. At the divide west of Flagler Fiord an advancing glacier had closed the old route: loads were carried 800 m up a rocky hillside, sledges hauled up empty by relays of twenty-five to thirty dogs, and everything lined down a 1.2 km descent singly on harpoon lines — twelve hours' work. At the head of [[place:Bay Fiord]] on 24 March the parties divided, taking opposite shores to double their chance of meeting caribou.

**[[artifact:kruger-1930-003|Stallworthy]]** went north-west with Eetookashoo, Qaavigarsuaq and Kahdi. By 4 April the canned dog food was down to three feeds and the drivers were asking whether they would be paid for dogs that starved; Stallworthy, who had let herd after herd of protected muskoxen go by for a fortnight, gave way and allowed six bulls to be shot ([[artifact:kruger-1930-016]]). On 8 April he stood on the northern tip of Axel Heiberg Island, the cape that now carries his name. He wanted to cross to Lands Lokk, 50 km off across [[place:Nansen Sound]]; the Inughuit judged it three days through new pressure ice, and [[person:Eetookashoo]], who had travelled that coast with Frederick Cook in 1908 and with MacMillan afterwards, said it would be a bad year to go farther north — he had thought the old polar pack there was a fixture, and was surprised to find new pressure ice and a water sky ([[artifact:kruger-1930-048]]). On 9 April, looking back from further west, Kahdi saw Peary's cairn on [[place:Cape Thomas Hubbard]], which they had walked past; in it was Krüger's note of 24 April 1930 ([[artifact:kruger-1930-011]]) — the only find of the entire search. He then ran the whole west coast of Axel Heiberg south, reaching [[place:Cape Southwest]] on 21 April after a 43-hour march with six dogs killed to feed the rest, and got home on 23 May: 65 days and by his own estimate not less than 2250 km.

**[[artifact:kruger-1930-004|Hamilton]]** took the south shore with Nukapinguaq and Kahkachoo, supported by Inuetuk and [[person:Seekeeunguaq]]. He searched Cape Southwest, crossed to [[place:Cornwall Island]] through rough ice and fog, found Joy's cairns on its northern point and nothing else, and turned back on Nukapinguaq's advice about bears and thin ice; then home by Bjorne Peninsula, Baumann Fiord and [[place:Makinson Inlet]], about 900 miles. Seventeen dogs starved. On 21 April Nukapinguaq wounded a charging bear off Hoved Island and was clawed deeply in the hip before he killed it.

**[[artifact:kruger-1930-005|Dersch]]** left [[place:Dundas Harbour]] on 22 March with [[person:Kellekte]] and [[person:Keepoomee]] to search [[place:Devon Island]] in case Krüger had come out that way. Two hundred kilometres west of the post, hard drifts running from the cliffs into brash ice and open water stopped him; he cut steps up a 400 m slope, saw three worse ahead, and turned the sledges by tipping them end over end. He never reached Beechey Island or Cornwallis Island as instructed, and for over a year his negative result was the only news Krüger's family had.

## What the searchers concluded

Both officers reasoned from empty cairns. Stallworthy, having watched the whole west coast of Axel Heiberg without seeing a sign, decided the party had never come that way and had died to the west or south, in the district of the Ringnes islands ([[artifact:kruger-1930-017]]); Hamilton decided they must be north of his own ground, since they could not have passed his cairns without leaving a record ([[artifact:kruger-1930-018]]). The Inughuit who had known the missing men thought they had died in the winter of 1930-31 after losing their dogs, left without matches in the dark, and so without heat or water.

The 1999 discovery of Krüger's cache 10 km east of Cape Southwest ([[page:kruger-1930-cape-southwest]]) shows that Stallworthy's reasoning was wrong, though his search was not careless: the party did come down that coast, and left no note. The chain of inference that everyone used for seventy years — no note, therefore no visit — had a hole in it.

A full account of both patrols, drawn from the officers' own reports and printed by permission of Commissioner [[person:J. H. MacBrien]], appeared in *Polar Record* in July 1934 ([[source:polarrecord1934kruger]]) — the first published narrative of the search, and the version the world had for the next sixty years. Its closing paragraph puts the loss on Meighen Island ([[artifact:kruger-1930-049]]). It also calls Cape Thomas Hubbard "Cape Norman Hubbard" and dates Krüger's note there 30 April 1930 rather than the 24th, which is a warning about how much of this story reached print at second hand.

The word reached Ottawa slowly. The Ungava could not get into Smith Sound in August 1932, so the reports stayed north another winter; Stallworthy's telegram, sent by mail sledge to Thule and cabled from Godhavn, reached Ottawa on 4 May 1933. Stallworthy, [[person:R. W. Hamilton]] and [[person:P. Dersch]] were recommended for the King's Police Medal that November. The force costed the searches at $288.50 beyond regular pay and asked, without success, to be reimbursed; neither the German government nor the State of Hesse would contribute.
