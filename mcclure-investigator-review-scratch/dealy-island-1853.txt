# The Dealy Island surveys of 1853
[page · mcclure-investigator · draft]

[[place:Dealy Island]] is a low island in Bridport Inlet on the south coast of
[[place:Melville Island]]. HMS Resolute and the steam tender Intrepid were
hauled into a canal cut through the fast ice beside it, in [[place:Bridport Inlet]],
on 10 September 1852 and
stayed eleven months. M'Dougall observed the position as 74° 56' 25" N,
108° 48' 33" W.

Nothing about the place recommends it except that it was as far west as
[[person:Henry Kellett]] could get. He had come within six miles of
[[place:Winter Harbour]] on 7 September and found six miles of solid floe
between his bows and Parry's anchorage; as he put it, cutting through it was
too absurd to attempt. So he turned back east and wintered here instead. It is
one of the plainer illustrations of what the ice in this part of the
archipelago does to plans.

The men built a great stone depot on the beach — provisions and gear left in
case Franklin's people ever came this way. They never did. Travellers were
still drawing on that depot seventy years later.

## Why the 1853 season mattered

Two things came together at Dealy Island. First,
[[person:George Frederick Mecham]] had found McClure's record at Parry's Rock
on 12 October 1852, so Kellett knew there was a ship to reach across
[[place:Viscount Melville Sound]] (see [[page:the-sledge-link]]). Second, he
had in [[person:Francis Leopold McClintock]] the officer who had done more than
anyone alive to work out how to travel long distances over sea ice on foot —
the man-hauled sledge with depots laid the previous autumn, supported by
auxiliary sledges that turn back in stages.

The result was the most productive single season of Arctic sledging in the
nineteenth century.

## 4 April 1853

A flag went up on the summit of Dealy Island at five in the morning. The
sledges left in two divisions at seven and a quarter past seven, Mecham's
western division making sail first. Kellett himself went out with them as far
as a week's travel and came back on 11 April having, in twenty-five years of
service, never seen work like it.

| Cdr McClintock, *Star of the North* | 4 Apr | 18 Jul | 105-106 | [[place:Point McClintock]], Prince Patrick Island, 77° 23' N 118° 20' W | 1,030 geog. / 1,328 statute | 768 geog. miles |

The two authorities for that table do not quite agree. M'Dougall's appendix,
compiled afterwards, gives McClintock 106 days and 1,328 statute miles and names
his furthest point *Point M'Clintock*. McClintock's own abstract in the
[[source:greatbritain1855further|Parliamentary blue book]] says 105 days absent,
1,030 geographical miles in 99 marches, and 22 cairns built — and never uses that
name at all. His journal for 13 June 1853 says only: "Saw two ivory gulls.
Started at a quarter past eight, at half-past ten the fog cleared off and we saw
the land, which is evidently an island." Where the officer's own journal and the
compiler disagree, the journal is the better authority.

The country they charted they named after each other. Mecham put
[[place:Mould Bay]] on Prince Patrick Island and the Hill of Barra on
[[place:Eglinton Island]] on the map at the request of the mate
[[person:George Strong Nares]] — the same Nares who would command the Alert
twenty-two years later — and Cape Russell after his first captain. McClintock
named Purchase Bay for the Intrepid's senior engineer, Green's Bay for the
ice-quartermaster who had surveyed it, and a point for his boatswain's mate.
[[person:Richard Vesey Hamilton]] named Point Roche for the man who had laid his
depots. Mould Bay became a Joint Arctic Weather Station a century later and is
still about the only place on Prince Patrick Island anyone has lived: see
[[topic:science-stations]].

They also left, in their journals, some of the best ice description in the
record. Mecham on the far side of Prince Patrick on 27 May 1853:
[[artifact:mcclure-investigator-064|"The pack here is much heavier than any yet
seen, being almost entirely composed of very old broken up floes"]] — the edge of
the Beaufort gyre, seen from a sledge. And the arithmetic of the work:
[[artifact:mcclure-investigator-063|184 lb a man]] at the start of a hundred-day
journey.

[[person:Francis Leopold McClintock]]'s hundred and five days
([[artifact:mcclure-investigator-009|track]]) was the longest man-hauled
journey made to that date, and it charted the north-eastern side of
[[place:Prince Patrick Island]]. Mecham ([[artifact:mcclure-investigator-010]])
went round [[place:Eglinton Island]] and up the outer, Beaufort Sea coast of
Prince Patrick to [[place:Land's End]] — the westernmost British survey in the archipelago for
the rest of the century.

[[person:Richard Vesey Hamilton]] ([[artifact:mcclure-investigator-011]]) went
the other way, north-east up [[place:Byam Martin Channel]] towards
[[place:Bathurst Island]], carrying the Resolute's half of a promise Kellett and
[[person:Edward Belcher]] had made to each other on the floe at
[[place:Beechey Island]] in August 1852: to communicate at 77° N, 105° W.
Commander Richards of the Assistance had left Belcher's despatch near the
rendezvous on 30 April. Then the two parties met by accident, and Hamilton's own
journal tells it better than any summary:

> "Soon after supper we were all astonished by hearing foot-steps approaching
> the tent; this proved to be Commander Richards and three men from his sledge.
> They had passed our sledge tracks about 3' off and followed them up."

His printed journal dates that 18 May 1853; M'Dougall's summary says the 17th.
Either way it is as close to co-ordination as
[[page:belchers-five-ships|Belcher's squadron]] ever managed — two parties
finding each other in fog by tripping over a sledge track.

Meanwhile [[person:Bedford Pim]] had gone west on 10 March and
[[person:Robert McClure]]'s sixty-one people arrived on 17 June.

## The numbers, and what they were worth

M'Dougall's appendix totals the season for Kellett's division: 1,618
geographical miles (1,870 statute) of new coastline, out of roughly 13,337
geographical miles of sledge travel by the two ships between autumn 1852 and
June 1854.

Set against the whole search from 1848 to 1854, Kellett's ships did more
sledging than anybody: 9,432 geographical miles on foot, against Austin's
6,087 and McClure's 2,350.

They did not find a trace of Franklin. What they found was the western Queen
Elizabeth Islands — Prince Patrick, Eglinton, the north and west coasts of
Melville — the last large land in the Canadian Arctic to be added to the map,
and the ground the Amundsen's own transects now cross. That coastline was drawn
by cold, hungry men walking, and the surprising thing when it is checked
against modern charts is how nearly right they got it.

## Afterwards

Both of the great sledgers came back. Mecham made an even longer journey in
1854 and died at thirty-two, at Honolulu, in 1858. McClintock went on to
command the *Fox* and to find the Victory Point record on King William Island
in 1859 — see [[topic:franklin-search]]. [[person:Richard Vesey Hamilton]]
became Admiral of the Fleet and First Sea Lord. [[person:George Frederick
Mecham]] has an island, a bay and a cape named after him and no monument at
all.


## The two tracks rebuilt, and the names on the chart

The routes of [[artifact:mcclure-investigator-009|M'Clintock]] and
[[artifact:mcclure-investigator-010|Mecham]] on the map are now built day by day from
their own printed journals rather than from four or five endpoints. Neither officer
carried a chronometer he could trust for longitude, so the journals give almost no
positions — M'Clintock's whole 105 days yield two observed fixes that survive the
printing, on 19 and 26 April — but they name landmarks constantly, and most of those
names are still on the Canadian chart. The tracks are therefore pinned to the modern
official coordinates of the features the officers name, from the Canadian
Geographical Names Database, with the interpolated stretches marked as such in each
waypoint. The full day-by-day tables, and the gazetteer of 111 names they use, are
[[artifact:mcclure-investigator-070|kept with the topic]].

Reading them in order shows how the season worked. M'Clintock hauled north across
Melville Island by Crooked and Separation Ravines, crossed [[place:Hecla and Griper Bay]]
under sail for [[place:Cape Fisher]], worked west past Long Point, Grassy Cape and
[[place:Depot Island]], rounded [[place:Cape De Bray]] — which he named for the
Frenchman [[person:Emile de Bray]] — and crossed to [[place:Wilkie Point]] on
[[place:Prince Patrick Island]] on 14 May. He searched [[place:Intrepid Inlet]], came
back for his depot, and then went north-west along the outer islands until on 18 June
he walked over into [[place:Satellite Bay]], built a cairn and turned for home. Mecham
went the other way round, west along the south coast past [[place:Winter Harbour]],
[[place:Cape Providence]] and [[place:Cape Russell]], and on 29 April sighted new land
to the westward: [[place:Eglinton Island]], which he landed on three days later. He
carried on round Prince Patrick's Land to the main pack edge, where the printed
observation reads Lat. 77° 00′ N., and wrote of it that "the pack here is much heavier
than any yet seen, being almost entirely composed of very old broken up floes"
([[artifact:mcclure-investigator-064]]).
