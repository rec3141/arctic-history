# The abandonment of 1854 and the Resolute's return
[page · mcclure-investigator · draft]

Five Royal Navy ships went into the archipelago with
[[person:Edward Belcher]] in 1852. Four of them were left in the ice in 1854 on
his order, and a fifth — [[person:Robert McClure]]'s Investigator — had been
left the year before. This page follows [[person:Henry Kellett]]'s western
division: HMS Resolute and the steam tender Intrepid. For the squadron as a
whole see [[page:belchers-five-ships]].

## Out of Dealy Island, and straight back into the ice

The Resolute and Intrepid had lain at [[place:Dealy Island]] since 10 September
1852. On 17 August 1853 a north-easterly gale blew them out of winter quarters
after eleven months — the same gale in which Lieutenant Bellot was lost in
Wellington Channel and two days before which the transport Breadalbane was
crushed off [[place:Beechey Island]].

They got about a hundred and fifty miles east. On 10 September 1853, off Point
Griffiths, the Resolute slipped from the fast ice at ten to four in the
afternoon — M'Dougall captions the plate "'Resolute' under sail for the last
time" — and was beset in young ice by eight that evening. She never sailed
again.

What followed is one of the better-recorded drifts in the archipelago, because
the ship's officers had nothing else to do. The two vessels went 180 miles with
the floe, north as far as 75° 04' N on 13 October, then south and east, and
became permanently fixed on 5 November 1853 at 74° 41' N, 101° 22' W, twenty-
eight miles south-west by south of [[place:Cape Cockburn]] on
[[place:Bathurst Island]]. [[artifact:mcclure-investigator-012|The drift track
is here]], from the ship's own table.

Aboard were the crews of three ships: Resolute, Intrepid, and the Investigator's
people who had walked over from [[place:Mercy Bay]] in June. One hundred and
thirty-eight men in two hulls, most of the Investigators still scorbutic. Mate
H. H. Sainsbury died there on 14 November.

## The order

On 7 February 1854 Belcher wrote to Kellett "requesting" that he bring his
officers and crews to Beechey Island before 26 August.

Kellett would not do it on a request. He replied the same day that
[[artifact:mcclure-investigator-021|"nothing contained in his orders would
justify him in abandoning the ships under his command"]] and asked for "final,
decided, and most unmistakeable orders." In a private letter he added that it
was the unanimous opinion of everyone aboard the Resolute that she would not
suffer from the first break-up: the ice around her was only one season old.

Belcher's answer of 21 April directed him to withdraw everything valuable and
bring his people in. On 28 April [[person:Francis Leopold McClintock]] came
back to the ship — seven days from the Assistance, five from Beechey — with the
decided orders. Kellett told the assembled crews on 30 April that he would take
them all to Beechey Island, and then ordered clothing packed "so as to avoid
confusion, in the event of re-occupying the ship." He clearly expected to come
back for her.

The Admiralty, as it happened, had written on the same 28 April 1854 directing
Belcher to withdraw the whole force from the polar seas, but that letter did not
reach Beechey Island until the Phoenix arrived in August.

## Leaving

The Investigator's men went first, in April: Haswell, Paine, Newton, Ford and
nineteen men on the 10th; [[person:Bedford Pim]],
[[person:Alexander Armstrong]], Kennedy and seventeen men on the 11th. Then the
Resolute's own people, in stages, [[person:William Thomas Domville]] taking the
invalids on 8 May.

The last four sledges left at seven in the evening on 15 May 1854:

> "After advancing about a quarter of a mile, the crews of the various sledges
> halted simultaneously; unharnessed, and gave three hearty cheers for the
> 'Resolute' and 'Intrepid;' but though the ice is a good conductor of sound,
> we heard no response."

([[artifact:mcclure-investigator-022|M'Dougall 1857, p. 391]].)

Forty-two people crossed Barrow Strait in thirteen days
([[artifact:mcclure-investigator-013|track]]), by the Cape Cockburn depot, the
mid-strait islands, Cape Martyr, [[place:Assistance Bay]] and Cape Hotham, and
were aboard the North Star at Beechey Island at seven in the evening on 28 May.
Sledge loads averaged 215 lb a man; officers were allowed 45 lb of personal
effects, men 30 lb.

Thomas Morgan, late of the Investigator, died aboard the North Star on 22 May
and was buried on Beechey Island on the 24th, beside the graves of Franklin's
men — see [[topic:franklin-1845]].

On 26 August 1854, an hour and a half after Belcher reached the North Star with
news that he had abandoned the Assistance and Pioneer too, the Phoenix and the
Talbot appeared off [[place:Cape Riley]] with news of the war with Russia. The
crews of five abandoned ships were divided among three, and on 27 August they
sailed.

## The court martial, and the answer

A court martial sat aboard HMS Waterloo at Sheerness on 18-20 October 1854 for
the loss of the ships. McClure was acquitted and told his conduct had been "most
meritorious and praiseworthy." Kellett was honourably acquitted, the order
having released him from responsibility, and the president returned his sword
with a compliment. Belcher was acquitted too, but the finding added that he
ought to have consulted Kellett; M'Dougall records that his sword was handed
back without a word.

Kellett's judgement was then vindicated in the most public way available.

On 10 September 1855, sixteen months after she was left and about twelve
hundred miles from where she was left, the Resolute was found adrift in Davis
Strait off the Baffin Island coast by the New London whaler George Henry,
Captain James Buddington. The men he sent aboard — one of them
[[person:George Emory Tyson]], who would later drift on the ice floe from the Polaris,
see [[topic:kane-hall-polaris]] — found nobody, and the wine decanters still on
the cabin table. Thirteen men made her seaworthy in under a week and Buddington
sailed her into New London on Christmas Eve.
[[artifact:mcclure-investigator-014|Her drift is here]], though only the two
ends of it are real: nobody watched the middle.

The salvage law was tangled, the salvage money went to the firm and not to
Buddington, and Henry Grinnell — the New York merchant who had already paid for
two American Franklin expeditions — saw a use for the ship. Congress voted
$40,000 on 24 June 1856 to buy her, refit her and give her back to Britain as an
act of goodwill between two countries that were not getting on. She was fitted
out at New London exactly as she had been left, sailed from New York on
13 November 1856 under a United States Navy crew, reached Portsmouth on
12 December and was handed over as a gift; the Queen came aboard to see her.
(The New London County Historical Society gives those two dates; other accounts
put the formal presentation on 16 December, and the naming of the officer who
took her across is not in the source used here.)

She was never used again. She was broken up in 1879, and in 1880 a desk made
from her timbers was given to the President of the United States. The Resolute
desk is in the Oval Office. Of everything the Franklin search produced — the
charts, the cairns, the graves, the two wrecks on the bottom off King William
Island — the object the world sees most often is a piece of furniture made from
a ship that a captain did not want to abandon and that came home by herself.


## A winter's instruments from the middle of Barrow Strait

The drift was also a nineteen-month meteorological station, and the record of it
survives. George Frederick M'Dougall, the Resolute's master, printed the ship's
monthly meteorological journals in the appendix to his own book of 1857, and they
run from September 1852 in Bridport Inlet through to April 1854 in the pack. The
headings give the ship's position each month in her own words: beset on 10 September
1853 "off Pt. Griffiths ... in Lat. 75° N., Long. 106° W.", and from October to April
"wintering in the Pack, S.W. by S., 28 miles from C. Cockburn, Lat. 74° 41′ N.,
Long. 101° 22′ W." She moved barely a mile all winter. Every day carries the aneroid
barometer, the maximum, minimum and mean air temperature, two interior temperatures,
wind direction and force, the weather letters, and a remarks column that is mostly
aurora. On 9 December 1853, with the mean at −37.9°F, the remark is "1 a.m., mercury
frozen".

That journal is [[artifact:mcclure-investigator-067|transcribed in this layer]] with
its month-by-month positions, and the December 1853 rows day by day; the rest of the
winter is legible in the scan but needs a better optical reading than the archive's
own. It is worth a scientist's attention because it is a whole instrumental winter
from a fixed point in mid-[[place:Barrow Strait]], taken at a latitude the Amundsen
works today, a hundred and seventy years before the ship's own sensors. One month is
simply absent: there is no page for January 1854 in the printed appendix.
