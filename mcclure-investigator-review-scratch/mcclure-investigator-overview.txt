# McClure, Collinson and the western search, 1850-1855
[page · mcclure-investigator · draft]

The North-West Passage was not sailed through in the nineteenth century. It was
found by a man standing on a hill, and it was finished on foot, by a starving
ship's company walking across the ice of Viscount Melville Sound from a ship
that had come in from the Pacific to two ships that had come in from the
Atlantic. That walk is the centre of this topic, and it happened within a
hundred miles of where the Amundsen now is.

## The two ships from the west

In January 1850 the Admiralty sent [[person:Richard Collinson]] in HMS
Enterprise and [[person:Robert McClure]] in HMS Investigator round Cape Horn to
look for [[topic:franklin-1845|Franklin]] from the Pacific side. They were
meant to work together. They never met again after Bering Strait, and
Collinson's own five seasons — two winters inside the archipelago, and two near
misses with the Franklin evidence — are told in
[[page:collinson-enterprise]]. McClure
parted company off Cape Lisburne on 31 July 1850 and pushed east alone along
the American coast — past Point Barrow, past the Mackenzie, past
[[place:Cape Bathurst]] — with the Moravian interpreter
[[person:Johann August Miertsching]] talking for him to the Inuvialuit whose
country it was — [[page:inuvialuit-and-copper-inuit|the people the Investigator
met]] are a page of their own, because Miertsching's German diary is the only
real record of them.

On 6 September 1850 he raised new land to the north: [[place:Banks Island]],
which he named Baring Island. He turned up
[[place:Prince of Wales Strait]] between it and Victoria Island, and on
17 September got to within about thirty miles of Barrow Strait before the ice
stopped him. He was beset for the winter on 30 September.

## The hill, 26 October 1850

He would not wait for spring. On 21 October he took a sledge north up the
strait, and on the 26th, from a six-hundred-foot hill he called
[[place:Mount Observation]], he saw the water of [[place:Viscount Melville Sound]] and beyond
it the coast of [[place:Melville Island]] — the land Parry had reached from the
Atlantic in 1819 (see [[topic:ross-parry]]). There was no unknown ground left
between the two oceans. Osborn's edition of the journals puts it plainly:
[[artifact:mcclure-investigator-015|"The North-west Passage was discovered!"]]

What remained was to get out, and that took four more years and cost five
lives.

## Three winters, and the paper under the rock

Two attempts to force Prince of Wales Strait failed. In August 1851 McClure
turned south, ran round Nelson Head and north up the exposed west coast of
Banks Island — three hundred miles in three days through water no ship can
count on — and was nearly crushed against the northern cliffs on 20 August
(Cresswell drew it: [[artifact:mcclure-investigator-004]]). On 24 September
1851 the ship took shelter in a bay he named [[place:Mercy Bay]]. She never
came out.

In April 1852 McClure sledged across the sound to [[place:Winter Harbour]] on
Melville Island, hoping for a ship or a depot at Parry's old winter quarters.
There was neither. He left his journal and his chart under Parry's Rock — the
sandstone boulder Parry's men had carved in 1820 — and walked back.

On 12 October 1852 [[person:George Frederick Mecham]], laying autumn depots
from [[place:Dealy Island]], stopped at that rock and
[[artifact:mcclure-investigator-017|could hardly credit his senses]]. That
paper is the reason anybody survived. See [[page:the-sledge-link]].

## Kellett's western division

[[person:Henry Kellett]] had brought HMS Resolute and the steam tender
Intrepid to Dealy Island in September 1852 as the western half of
[[person:Edward Belcher]]'s five-ship squadron — see
[[page:belchers-five-ships]] for the squadron as a whole. Mecham's find turned
Kellett's spring into a rescue. [[person:Bedford Pim]] left on 10 March 1853,
the earliest long spring journey then on record, and walked into the Bay of
Mercy on 6 April, nine days before McClure meant to abandon his ship. The men
digging the grave and the carpenters making the first coffin dropped what they
were doing ([[artifact:mcclure-investigator-019]]).

The same spring sent [[person:Francis Leopold McClintock]], Mecham and
[[person:Richard Vesey Hamilton]] out from the same beach, and they came back
having put [[place:Prince Patrick Island]] and the western coasts of Melville
Island on the chart: 1,618 geographical miles of new coastline in one season.
See [[page:dealy-island-1853]].

## The end of the ships

[[person:Alexander Armstrong]] and [[person:William Thomas Domville]] surveyed
the Investigator's crew on 23 May 1853 and found almost every man scorbutic
([[artifact:mcclure-investigator-020]]). She was abandoned on 3 June and her
people walked to the Resolute, arriving on 17 June — the first crossing of the
passage, completed on foot. See [[page:three-winters-mercy-bay]].

It did them limited good. The Resolute was blown out of Dealy Island in August
1853, caught in the pack in September, and drifted 180 miles before stopping
for the winter off [[place:Cape Cockburn]]. In 1854 Belcher ordered Kellett to
leave her. Kellett thought she would come out on her own and made Belcher put
the order beyond doubt ([[artifact:mcclure-investigator-021]]); then he left
her, and everyone marched to [[place:Beechey Island]] and came home in the
North Star, the Phoenix and the Talbot. The Resolute drifted twelve hundred
miles by herself and was found sound in Davis Strait in 1855 by an American
whaler. See [[page:abandonment-and-the-resolute]].

## Why it matters here

The Investigator lies on the bottom of Mercy Bay, upright in about eleven
metres of water; Parks Canada found her by sonar in July 2010, dived her in
2011, and went back through two metres of sea ice in May 2026 at the request of
the [[place:Aulavik National Park]] Cooperative Management Board. Ashore are
McClure's Cache and the graves of three of his men. For a century and a half
after 1853 Inuvialuit families travelled to Mercy Bay for the iron, copper and
wood the Navy left there, and Investigator material has been found at sites
right across the western Arctic; that salvage is part of the ship's history,
not a footnote to it.

And the ice in this story is the ice this ship works in. McClure was stopped
twice at the same place by multi-year floes coming south out of the Beaufort
into Viscount Melville Sound. Kellett could not reach Winter Harbour in 1852
because six miles of solid floe lay between him and it. The 1851 run up the
west coast of Banks Island was a fluke of a season. Anyone reading modern
Beaufort ice charts is reading the same system these men were reading, badly,
with a telescope. The dated ice observations these voyages left — where they
were stopped, what freeze-up looked like at Dealy Island hour by hour in
September 1852, how far the Resolute drifted with nobody aboard — are collected
in [[page:the-ice-that-stopped-them]].
