# The people the Investigator met
[page · mcclure-investigator · draft]

HMS Investigator carried one man who could talk to the people whose country she
was sailing through: [[person:Johann August Miertsching]], a Saxon Moravian who
had spent years at the Labrador mission and whose Inuttitut turned out to be
close enough to be understood from Point Barrow to Victoria Island. HMS
Enterprise, following a year behind, carried nobody who could. The difference
runs through both records.

Miertsching kept a diary in German. It was published at Gnadau in 1855, the
year after he came home, [[source:miertsching1855reise|Reise-Tagebuch des
Missionars Joh. Aug. Miertsching]], and the whole text is held here; the English
most readers meet is L. H. Neatby's *Frozen Ships* (1967), which is still in
copyright. The extraction from the German is in
`mcclure-investigator/notes/miertsching1855-inuit.md`.

Nothing on this page comes from the people it describes. It is a missionary's
diary and two naval officers' books, written for readers in Saxony and London,
and it is the only record there is of these meetings. The names below are three
Europeans' attempts to spell what they heard.

## The Alaskan coast, August 1850

**8 August, off Point Drew.** Three unarmed men waited on the beach and led the
landing party a little way inland. Later fifteen people came out by umiak, sang
and danced on deck, and told Miertsching they called themselves Kogarmiut and
traded with Russian traders on the great river. Boatloads that came later sold
fish for tobacco and began taking things, and were sent away.
[[person:Alexander Armstrong]] remembered the greeting:
[[artifact:mcclure-investigator-024|"in a few minutes we were rubbing noses"]].

**11 August, at the mouth of the Colville.** Arrows were shot towards the boats
before anyone landed. Then the nose-touch greeting, repeated with each person,
settled it. [[person:Attua]] — Armstrong spells him Aitawa —
[[artifact:mcclure-investigator-049|"a very handsome and sensible man"]], had
three wives and thirteen children, wintered three days inland, and traded each
summer at a place called Nuniwokingak. He had never heard of Franklin. McClure
gave him a flag and a letter to carry to the Russian posts, and Miertsching
noted drily that among Eskimos promising and keeping are two different things.
Whether the letter arrived is not known.

**24 August, near Point Warren.** Spears, drawn bows, and blank pistol shots,
then trade in caribou hides and fox and bear furs for knives and saws.
[[person:Kairoluak]] kept his camp, Miertsching thought, in better order than
any he had seen. His son lay with a freshly broken leg the ship's doctor could
not help in one afternoon. And Kairoluak told a story about strangers who had
come without boats, built a driftwood house on a point nearby, driven off the
seals and caribou and birds simply by being there, and starved one by one; the
last of them was found dead demanding the game back, and Kairoluak had buried
him.

McClure had every reason to want that story to be about Franklin. The next day
he and Armstrong and Miertsching walked to the point and found two collapsed
driftwood winter houses, broken stone lamps and a wrecked kayak — ordinary Inuit
ruins, and nothing European at all. Miertsching guessed the tale was older than
any European in the country.

## Nuvuak, Cape Bathurst, 30 August to 1 September 1850

The camp at [[place:Cape Bathurst]] was about thirty tents and nine winter
houses. Two women, [[person:Kunatsiak]] and [[person:Kenalik]], were found alone
on the beach ten miles off, the men being away whale-hunting; they showed rings,
earrings and beads got from three boatloads of Europeans who had stayed two days
on their land some years before.

At the camp the landing party was rushed by an armed, shouting crowd.
Miertsching walked out alone to meet them, blank shots were fired, and he drew a
line in the snow that neither side was to cross. Within the hour the crowd had
brought its women and children and was putting infants into the visitors' arms.

These people traded with the Loucheux inland, who carried goods on to Fort Good
Hope on the Mackenzie. They knew nothing of Franklin. They gave Miertsching
their names for the country: Nuvuak for the cape, Akkunek and Tuppeliosak for
the islands, Koruk for the water between. And [[person:Kenalualik]], the elder
who took McClure's letters for the Hudson's Bay post, explained the sky:
[[artifact:mcclure-investigator-050|"above us is a very large, blue box, the
house of the sun"]], the stars being light coming through holes in it. He pressed
Miertsching to stay the winter, offering a sledge, dogs and a tent, and at last
offered him his daughter, about sixteen —
[[artifact:mcclure-investigator-053|"takka unna! — take this one!"]] Miertsching
declined, saying he owed duty to his own angajuga. Fifteen kayaks escorted the
boats back to the ship.

The next day, aboard, a man described in exact physical detail a party of
Europeans who had camped ashore — two boats, a polar bear shot on the ice, a
stout sea-captain who paced twenty steps and turned — and said it had happened
the day before yesterday. McClure worked out that he was describing
[[person:John Richardson]]'s boat expedition from the Mackenzie to the
Coppermine, about two years earlier. A woman said she had seen Europeans with
masted skin boats three summers ago, and another backed her. That is what
British intelligence about British ships looked like coming back through Inuit
memory: exact in substance, elastic about when.

It is also the reason the Franklin search took so long. Everything the searchers
most needed to know was, by the 1850s, in Inuit heads somewhere between the
Mackenzie and Iglulik. Almost nobody in the Navy could ask for it. See
[[topic:inuit-oral-history]].

## The Copper Inuit of Prince Albert Land, 3 June 1851

A sledge party in May 1851 reported five tents on a headland about a hundred
miles from the beset ship, whose people were friendly but could only be
understood by signs. McClure went himself, with Miertsching.

They cried [[artifact:mcclure-investigator-051|sivoravogut, erksidlarpogut — we
are afraid]] — nobody there had seen an outsider, and there were no weapons to
hand. Then Miertsching, dressed as they were, spoke, and was understood. They
were astonished to learn that other inhabited lands existed; they had believed
they were the only people.

What followed is the single most useful hour the expedition spent. On paper laid
over a sealskin, the band drew McClure the coast: correctly extended to Point
Parry, correctly marking Sutton and Liston Islands in Dolphin and Union Strait,
with the further points named and the families who lived on them. Miertsching
did not write those names down, which is the loss of the century in this
material. They spoke of a large land to the south, *nunavaksuaraluk*; of caribou
(*Tuktut*) and musk-ox (*Umingmak*) inland, hunted with bow and throwing-spear;
and of a high inland mountain where their ancestors had lived during a very
great water.

Their harpoons, knives, axes, arrowheads and needles
[[artifact:mcclure-investigator-052|were all native copper]], traded from people
further east for seal oil and narwhal horn — a working account of the Coppermine
trade, taken down by a Saxon missionary on Victoria Island in 1851.

McClure tied his own red shawl round the neck of a young mother, who tried at
first to hand over her baby in payment, not understanding the idea of a gift.
The party had to leave in a hurry: a seaman named Hewlett had badly frostbitten
feet.

## Collinson, without an interpreter

[[person:Richard Collinson]] met people too. Near Point Barrow in July 1851
Inupiat came out over the ice, gave him the local place names and drew him a
chart of a coast the Navy had been guessing at. At [[place:Walker Bay]] in
October 1851 an old man named [[person:Ip-pee-ra]] came aboard with his wife and
two sons-in-law from a camp of twenty-five, and
[[artifact:mcclure-investigator-042|chose a knife over a boarding pike]]. At
[[place:Iqaluktuuttiaq]] the Copper Inuit visited all winter and took nothing,
though the stores lay open on the ice. And at [[place:Camden Bay]] in July 1854
a boy was wearing buttons engraved with the movements of the search squadrons.

But Collinson had no interpreter, and the conversations stop there. He was
within a hundred miles of the Franklin evidence twice and did not know it. See
[[page:collinson-enterprise]].

## What it meant afterwards

McClure landed his stores on the beach at [[place:Mercy Bay]] on 3 June 1853 and
walked away. For a century and a half Inuvialuit families travelled to
[[place:McClure's Cache]] for the iron, copper, wood and canvas, and Parks
Canada records that material from the Investigator has been documented at sites
right across the western Arctic and that the cache shaped travel routes in the
region. The wreck lies in Inuvialuit waters, the cache and the graves are inside
[[place:Aulavik National Park]], and the survey of May 2026 was made because the
park's Cooperative Management Board asked for it.

The ship's most lasting contribution to the country she was mapping was her
scrap.
