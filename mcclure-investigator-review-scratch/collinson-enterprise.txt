# Collinson and the Enterprise: five seasons and two near misses
[page · mcclure-investigator · draft]

[[person:Richard Collinson]] was [[person:Robert McClure]]'s senior officer. He
took HMS Enterprise into the Arctic from the Pacific five times, wintered twice
inside the archipelago, sledged to within sight of the ground where Franklin's
people died, and came home in 1855 having met nobody, found nothing decisive
and quarrelled with his officers. His journal was not published until 1889, six
years after he died. He is the counter-example to McClure in almost every
respect, and the more interesting of the two commanders for anyone who has to
work ice.

Most of the Enterprise's story belongs to the western Arctic and to Victoria
Island rather than to the Queen Elizabeth Islands, so this page is short. The
track is [[artifact:mcclure-investigator-041|here]] and the detail is in
`mcclure-investigator/notes/collinson1889-notes.md`.

## A year behind

Both ships cleared Plymouth on 20 January 1850. The Enterprise, the slower
sailer, came through [[place:Bering Strait]] on 11 August, a fortnight after
McClure, found the season gone, and went back out — to Sitka, to Hawaii, and to
Hong Kong for the winter, a passage of 35,225 miles from Plymouth. That winter
of waiting is the whole difference between the two voyages.

In February 1851 he learned that Lieutenant John Barnard, detached towards the
Yukon, had been killed at the Russian post at Nulato. Barnard was the only
officer aboard with previous Arctic service.

Coming north again in the summer of 1851, Collinson did exactly what McClure had
done a year before: he entered [[place:Prince of Wales Strait]] and worked
north up it. On 29 August he landed on the
[[place:Princess Royal Islands]] and found two copper cylinders on a flagstaff,
one holding a Union Jack and the other McClure's notice: the Investigator had
wintered four miles to the north-east, and a party from her had reached the
north end of the strait in October. On 31 August the ice stopped him at 73° 30'
N, 114° 35' W — a few miles short of where McClure had stood — and he could not
even see [[place:Melville Island]].

He wintered at [[place:Walker Bay]] on the west coast of Victoria Island, 1851-52.

## Iqaluktuuttiaq, 1852-53

In the summer of 1852 he was driven back out of Prince of Wales Strait, went
south instead through Dolphin and Union Strait and Coronation Gulf, and on 3
October 1852 settled for the winter at [[place:Iqaluktuuttiaq]] (Cambridge
Bay) — the Amundsen's own most frequent western port of call, and at that date
a place no European ship had wintered.

Two things came out of that winter, and both were near misses.

On 8 May 1853 a sledge party found [[person:John Rae]]'s record of 13 August
1851 on the Victoria Island shore, telling Collinson the coast he was searching
had already been searched. Two days later, on 10 May, the same party built a
cairn on an island in Victoria Strait at 70° 26' N, 100° 47' W, saw no land
beyond, and turned back for want of provisions and because the ice was too
rough for their strength. Ahead of them, across the strait, lay King William
Island and the line of the retreat from the Erebus and Terror — see
[[topic:franklin-1845]] and [[topic:franklin-search]]. Six years later
[[person:Francis Leopold McClintock]] found the record at Victory Point.

Then in early July 1853, at the Finlayson Islands near Cambridge Bay, one of
the crew picked up at the high-water mark two pieces of fir nailed together,
with a copper door-hasp bearing the Queen's broad arrow. Collinson described it
carefully and called it
[[artifact:mcclure-investigator-043|"almost the only article we have met with
which could have belonged to the missing ships"]] — and did not know what he
had. It was, in all likelihood, wreckage from one of Franklin's ships carried
south, and it was picked up more than twenty years before anybody looked
seriously in that quarter.

## What the Inuit told him, and what he did not ask

Collinson's journal is worth reading for the encounters. Near Point Barrow in
July 1851 Inupiat came out over the ice, gave him the local place names —
Noo-na-hoo, Too-na-mut, Oo-fe-la — and drew him a chart of a coast the Navy had
been guessing at. At [[place:Walker Bay]] in October 1851 an old man named
[[person:Ip-pee-ra]] came aboard with his wife and two sons-in-law from a camp
of twenty-five at Isthmus Lake ([[artifact:mcclure-investigator-042]]). At
Cambridge Bay the Copper Inuit visited repeatedly through the winter and, as
Collinson recorded with some surprise, took nothing, though the stores lay open
on the ice.

And at [[place:Camden Bay]] on the Alaskan coast in July 1854, forty-one people
came out in two umiaks, and a boy among them was wearing buttons engraved with
a record of the movements of the search squadrons in 1850-52. That is how the
Royal Navy's own despatches were travelling along that coast: through Inuit
hands, faster and more reliably than through its ships.

Nobody aboard the Enterprise could speak the language. The Investigator had
[[person:Johann August Miertsching]]; Collinson had nobody. It is a plain
reason why the men who could have told him where Franklin's ships were did not
tell him.

## Home, and silence

A third winter at Camden Bay, then out through Bering Strait in August 1854 —
[[artifact:mcclure-investigator-044|"it was not until the 5th of May that we got
a sight of Old England"]], five years and four months out.

His brother's 1889 edition adds a note headed "Personal Troubles in the
Enterprise", saying that by the spring of 1853 some of the officers were
suspended from duty, and expressly declining to give particulars. The journal as
printed gives no hint of it. What happened aboard that ship is not in the record
this layer holds, and it should not be guessed at here.

McClure got the knighthood and the £10,000. Collinson got the Founder's Medal of
the Royal Geographical Society, eventually, and the reputation of a man who had
been very nearly everywhere that mattered.
