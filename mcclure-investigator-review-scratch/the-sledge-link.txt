# The sledge link across Viscount Melville Sound
[page · mcclure-investigator · draft]

The distance from [[place:Dealy Island]] on the south coast of
[[place:Melville Island]] to the Bay of Mercy on the north coast of
[[place:Banks Island]] is about 160 statute miles in a straight line across
[[place:Viscount Melville Sound]]. In 1852 and 1853 that stretch of ice was
crossed four times, in both directions, by men on foot. Those crossings are
what actually joined the Pacific and the Atlantic, and they are the reason
sixty-one men came home.

## The record at Parry's Rock

In April 1852 [[person:Robert McClure]], twenty months in the ice and with his
crew already on two-thirds rations, sledged from the Bay of Mercy to
[[place:Winter Harbour]] on Melville Island. Parry's Hecla and Griper had
wintered there in 1819-20 — see [[topic:ross-parry]] — and Parry's men had cut
their ships' names into a great block of sandstone above the beach: [[place:Parry's Rock]]. McClure
hoped to find a ship, or at least a depot. He found neither, only a notice
McClintock had left in 1851.

So on 28 April 1852 he put his own journal of proceedings and a chart of his
discoveries under Parry's Rock, and walked the 160 miles back to his ship. It
was the only message he could send. Nobody had any reason to look for it.

## Mecham reads it, 12 October 1852

[[person:Henry Kellett]]'s HMS Resolute and the steam tender Intrepid had
hauled into winter quarters at Dealy Island on 10 September 1852, after failing
to get the last six miles into Winter Harbour through solid floe. In October
[[person:George Frederick Mecham]] went out laying depots for the next spring,
went round by Liddon Gulf and back to Winter Harbour, and looked at Parry's
rock more closely than usual.

> "Lieut. Mecham could hardly credit his senses when he discovered a document
> upon its summit, detailing the accomplishment of the north-west passage, and
> the position of H. M. S. 'Investigator' in Banks Land!"

([[artifact:mcclure-investigator-017|Osborn 1856, p. 287]].) A rock carved by
one expedition in 1820 had served as a letter-box for another thirty-two years
later. Kellett's people now knew that a ship from the Pacific lay about 180
miles to the south-west, and that she might be abandoned before they could
reach her.

## Pim's journey, 10 March to 6 April 1853

Kellett sent [[person:Bedford Pim]] off absurdly early — 10 March 1853, at
half past eight in the morning, with the sledge *John Barrow* and the motto
"Hope on, Hope ever", seven men and eighty-eight days' provisions, and
[[person:William Thomas Domville]] with a dog sledge alongside.
M'Dougall's footnote calls it "the earliest extended spring journey on record".
Mr Roche's sledge went as far as Cape Providence in support and turned back on
20 March.

What happened next is told two ways. M'Dougall, writing in 1857, says Pim's own
sledge broke down on 29 March some twenty-odd miles south-west of
[[place:Cape Dundas]], that he handed his gear to
[[person:William Thomas Domville]] — who then had to shift fifteen
hundredweight twenty-five miles over rough ice by double-tripping — and went on
with the dogs and two men. Pim's own journal, printed in the
[[source:greatbritain1855further|blue book of January 1855]], instead has him
send Domville ahead with the *John Barrow* on 20 March, near
[[place:Cape Providence]], and press on past him again on the 29th. The two
accounts of the same fortnight are not reconcilable and both are recorded here.

What is not in doubt is the surface. Pim, 25 March, in mid-sound:
[[artifact:mcclure-investigator-061|"The floe, if floe it can be called, proved
bad indeed: hummocks after hummocks followed each other in apparently endless
succession... the surface was so glassy and uneven that the men could scarcely
stand."]]

He was at the Bay of Mercy on 6 April: twenty-eight days out, 160 miles direct,
427 statute miles actually travelled. McClure had fixed 15 April to abandon the
ship. [[artifact:mcclure-investigator-006|The track is here]], redrawn from
Pim's own journal.

Pim's own version of the meeting, written up for Kellett, is the flattest and
the best: [[artifact:mcclure-investigator-062|"Arrived within 100 yards without
being observed; then, however, two persons taking exercise on the ice discovered
that I did not belong to their ship. Upon beckoning, they quickly approached,
and proved to be Capt. M'Clure and Lieut. Haswell."]] He gives the time as five
in the afternoon.

M'Dougall's telling, from hearsay three years later, is the one everybody
quotes, and it is the best-told moment in the whole search.
[[person:Robert McClure]] and Lieutenant Haswell were pacing the floe beside
the ship, discussing where to dig a grave for the man who had died the day
before, when a figure came running at them out of the rough ice, face black
with soot and grease, gesturing. Taking him for an Inuk, McClure shouted in
English: [[artifact:mcclure-investigator-018|"In the name of God, who are
you?"]] The answer came back in English too.

Below decks a man had caught the words and roused all hands. The sick came up
with the healthy; [[artifact:mcclure-investigator-019|the carpenters dropped
their tools — they were making the first coffin]] — and the burial party ashore
left the half-dug grave and ran.

## Back and forth across the sound

McClure went straight back with Pim to argue his case
([[artifact:mcclure-investigator-007]]). His own despatch to the Admiralty says
he left the ship on 8 April and was aboard the Resolute on the 19th; under Cape
Bounty on the way he overtook Domville, still double-tripping the depot back to
Cape Dundas. Kellett and both ships' companies came out over the floe to meet
him. He wanted to keep his ship, send
the weakest men home overland, and take her out in the summer of 1853 with a
skeleton crew — which would have made the Investigator, not the Fox or the Gjøa,
the first ship through.

[[person:Henry Kellett]] would not take his word for the state of the crew.
Instead he wrote a letter to be read to the ship's company, offering them the
choice of staying another season for the chance of getting the Investigator out,
or of quitting her at once without discredit, and he sent Domville back across
the sound to survey them. McClure left the Resolute on the evening of 5 May and
was back in Mercy Bay on the 21st; the letter was read the next morning, and
besides the officers exactly four men volunteered to stay. Then Domville and the
ship's surgeon [[person:Alexander Armstrong]] signed the certificate that ended
the argument: the crew's health was
[[artifact:mcclure-investigator-020|"such as renders them utterly unfit to
undergo the rigour of another winter in this climate"]].

Meanwhile [[person:Samuel Gurney Cresswell]] had already taken the invalids
away over the ice on 15 April — he drew the scene himself
([[artifact:mcclure-investigator-005]]) — and, going on from Melville Island to
[[place:Beechey Island]] and home in the Phoenix that autumn, became the first
person to travel the North-West Passage from the Pacific side to the Atlantic.

## The last crossing, June 1853

HMS Investigator was abandoned on 3 June 1853. A depot was landed on the beach
— what Parks Canada now calls McClure's Cache — the three new graves were
marked, and sixty-one officers and men started walking
([[artifact:mcclure-investigator-008]]).

They were aboard the Resolute and Intrepid at Dealy Island on 17 June. The
united ships' companies now numbered 138 souls, which is a large part of why
the next winter went as badly as it did.

That fortnight's walk is the North-West Passage, in the only sense in which
anybody had completed it by 1853: men who had entered the Arctic from the
Pacific arriving, alive, on ships that had entered it from the Atlantic.
Parliament thought so too, and voted McClure £10,000. It would be sixty-eight
years before a single vessel — Amundsen's Gjøa in 1903-06, then the
[[topic:st-roch-modern|St. Roch]] — did the whole thing on her own bottom.
