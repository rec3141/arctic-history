# The ice that stopped them: an 1850s record of the western Parry Channel
[page · mcclure-investigator · draft]

This page is for people on the Amundsen. Everything below is an ice observation
made by a sailing ship in the same waters the ship works now, between 1850 and
1854, with the position and the date attached. Read as a set, these voyages are
a long, expensive, involuntary experiment in what the ice of the western Parry
Channel does to shipping.

## The barrier that stopped everybody in the same place

McClure was turned back twice in [[place:Prince of Wales Strait]] at almost
exactly the same point:

- **17 September 1850**, 73° 10' N, 117° 10' W — about thirty miles short of
  Barrow Strait.
- **16 August 1851**, 73° 43' 43" N, 115° 32' 30" W — twenty-five miles short.

[[person:Richard Collinson]], a year behind him and without knowing it, was
stopped at **73° 30' N, 114° 35' W on 31 August 1851** — between McClure's two
positions. Three attempts by two ships in two seasons, all halted within about
forty miles of one another.

What stops a ship there is not the strait's own ice. It is old, thick,
multi-year floe driven south and east out of the Beaufort Sea into
[[place:Viscount Melville Sound]] and jammed against the islands. The same
material is why [[person:Henry Kellett]] could not get his ships the last six
miles into [[place:Winter Harbour]] in September 1852 — the fast ice was, in
M'Dougall's words,
[[artifact:mcclure-investigator-057|"too absurd to be long entertained"]] as
something to saw through — and why McClure, forced to go round the outside of
[[place:Banks Island]] instead, was nearly crushed against the cliffs on
20 August 1851 ([[artifact:mcclure-investigator-004]]).

This is the same ice regime that keeps the northern route of the passage
unreliable today while the southern route through Victoria Strait opens most
summers. It is why [[topic:st-roch-modern|the St. Roch]] went the southern way
eastbound and needed a whole season the northern way westbound, and why
[[topic:amundsen-gjoa|Amundsen]] never considered the northern route at all.

## The one open season, and what it was worth

In three days from 17 to 19 August 1851, the Investigator ran about three
hundred miles from Nelson Head north up the *west* coast of Banks Island in
clear water. That coast faces the Beaufort pack directly and is normally the
worst ice in the archipelago. It happened once, and it took McClure to a place
he could not leave.

Then, on 10 September 1851, drifting into the pack:
[[artifact:mcclure-investigator-016|"Thus we launch into this formidable frozen
sea."]] Thirteen days later he was aground in
[[place:Mercy Bay]] and the voyage was over.

The lesson a modern reader can take from that pair of facts is not that the
Arctic was worse then. It is that a single anomalous open season on an
exposed coast is a trap for anyone with no way out, and that the ice which
matters is the ice at the far end of the passage, in the season after the one
you are sailing in.

## Freeze-up, in a navigator's words

M'Dougall on 8 September 1852, off the south coast of Melville Island:
[[artifact:mcclure-investigator-058|"the whole sea was covered with a thick
sludge, which the seamen not inaptly likened to thick pea-soup"]] — grease ice,
making the ship unmanageable without a strong breeze. By two the next morning
they were pushing through bay ice an inch and a half thick, formed overnight;
by 10 September the Resolute had been warped into a canal sawn through the fast
ice at [[place:Dealy Island]] and was in winter quarters.

Two days from open water to a ship immobilised for eleven months. The Resolute
was blown out of that berth by a gale on 17 August 1853, got about a hundred
and fifty miles east, and was caught again in young ice within six hours of
casting off on 10 September ([[artifact:mcclure-investigator-012]]).

## Drift

The [[artifact:mcclure-investigator-012|Resolute's beset drift]] is one of the
better-documented Barrow Strait drift records of the nineteenth century, because
her officers had nothing else to do and kept a table: from 74° 59' N, 105° 38' W
on 10 September 1853, north to 75° 04' N on 13 October, then south and east to
74° 41' N, 101° 22' W by 5 November — 180 miles in eight weeks, then effectively
stationary all winter.

Then the part nobody measured. Left in the pack on 15 May 1854 at 74° 41' N,
101° 22' W, the ship went out through Barrow Strait, down Lancaster Sound, and
south through Baffin Bay and Davis Strait on her own, and was found on
10 September 1855 off the Baffin Island coast — about twelve hundred miles in
sixteen months, working out at something like two and a half miles a day made
good. [[artifact:mcclure-investigator-014|The track drawn here]] has real
endpoints and an invented middle, and is marked as such.

## Cold

- **6 January 1853**, Mercy Bay: mean −61.6 °F over twenty-four hours, with an
  interval at −67 °F. Two caribou were shot that day.
- The Resolute's winter of 1853-54 in the pack off [[place:Cape Cockburn]] is
  covered by a meteorological journal printed in M'Dougall's appendix
  (pp. 512-522), kept at a fixed drifting position of 74° 41' 34" N,
  101° 22' 06" W. That is a full winter's instrumental record from the middle
  of Barrow Strait, and it is in the blue book and the narrative, unexploited
  here: see [[artifact:mcclure-investigator-039]].

## What a scientist might do with this

The historical value of these voyages is not the anecdote. It is that they
produce dated, positioned ice observations from a period two centuries before
the satellite record, in a sector where the multi-year ice budget still governs
whether the northern route is passable. Three of them are unusually clean:

1. The three "stopped here" positions of 1850, 1851 and 1851 in Prince of Wales
   Strait, all within forty miles, from two independent ships.
2. The 1851 open-water anomaly on the west coast of Banks Island — a season
   when the Beaufort pack was off that shore in mid-August.
3. The freeze-up sequence of 7-10 September 1852 at Dealy Island, described
   hour by hour, and the corresponding 1853 sequence a week later in the same
   waters.

Every one of them is pinned on the map in this layer with the source and the
page, and the underlying texts are held in full.
