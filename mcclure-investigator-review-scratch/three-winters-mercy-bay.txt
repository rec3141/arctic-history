# Three winters: Prince of Wales Strait and the Bay of Mercy
[page · mcclure-investigator · draft]

HMS Investigator spent one winter beset in [[place:Prince of Wales Strait]] and
two more in the bay on the north coast of [[place:Banks Island]] that
[[person:Robert McClure]] named the Bay of Mercy. She is still there. What
happened aboard in those three winters is documented twice over, by the captain
in [[source:osborn1856discovery|Osborn's edition of his journals]] and by the
surgeon in [[source:armstrong1857personal|his own book]], and the two accounts
do not agree.

## The first winter, 1850-51

The ship was beset on 30 September 1850 at 73° 50' N, 117° 55' W, beside the
[[place:Princess Royal Islands]], and stayed there for nearly ten months. It was
a good winter by the standards of what followed. Deer and musk-ox were shot,
the sledge journey of October 1850 had already settled the geography (see
[[page:the-sledge-link]]), and in the spring three parties went out — Haswell
south-east to Wollaston Land, [[person:Samuel Gurney Cresswell]] north-west
along the coast of Banks Island, Wynniatt north-east — and came back having
proved Banks Land an island and traced hundreds of miles of coast.

Between 30 May and 2 June 1851 McClure and [[person:Johann August Miertsching]]
visited a camp of five tents near the ship. This was the expedition's only
sustained contact with the Copper Inuit of the strait, and Miertsching's German
diary — translated by L. H. Neatby in 1967 as *Frozen Ships* — is the fuller
record of it. He had been a Moravian missionary in Labrador, and his Inuttitut
was near enough to be understood everywhere from Point Barrow to Victoria
Island. Almost every useful thing the expedition learned from anyone in the
country, it learned through him.

The ship got free on 14 July 1851.

## The Bay of Mercy, 1851-53

Two attempts to force the strait north into Barrow Strait failed. On 16 August
1851 McClure turned back twenty-five miles short and went the other way instead:
south round Nelson Head and then three hundred miles north in three days up the
exposed west coast of the island through open water. On 20 August he was very
nearly crushed against the northern cliffs
([[artifact:mcclure-investigator-004|Cresswell drew it]]). On 23 September the
ship went aground in the night, and on the 24th she was afloat in the bay he
named in thanksgiving: [[artifact:mcclure-investigator-016|"Thus we launch into
this formidable frozen sea. Spes mea in Deo."]]

She never came out of it.

## What short rations do

The arithmetic is the heart of this story, and [[person:Alexander Armstrong]]
set it out plainly.

- **1 October 1851**: two-thirds allowance, because beef had been lost entering
  the ice and a depot left behind.
- **1 February 1852**: at the monthly inspection, for the first time, loss of
  flesh and strength in some men after four months of it.
- **1 July 1852**: Armstrong formally represents to McClure that a more liberal
  scale of diet is needed. Sixteen men show incipient scurvy.
- **6 September 1852**: rations cut *again* — vegetables to two and a half
  ounces a day, meat to eight ounces gross and about six net, flour to ten
  ounces, lime juice halved, and "no extra food was allowed for the sick under
  any circumstances." Men began eating the salt beef and pork raw, "with a
  degree of avidity and relish which must be experienced to be fully
  understood." Armstrong told McClure this would make the scurvy worse; it went
  on regardless.
- **4 October 1852**: the ship's company come aft in a body to ask for more
  food, and are refused.

Armstrong's own theory of the disease, which is wrong in mechanism and right in
practice, is worth reading:
[[artifact:mcclure-investigator-025|"one pound of animal food daily is not
sufficient for men employed on Polar service"]]. He blamed salt meat and
insufficient calories rather than the missing vitamin, but he was correct that
the fresh meat the hunters brought in was the only thing holding the disease
off, and correct that officers, eating better and living in better-ventilated
quarters, were the last to show symptoms.

## The disagreement

In September 1852 McClure told his officers his plan for the spring: divide the
ship's company, send half of them away on foot — one party for Cape Spencer and
Baffin Bay, one up Prince of Wales Strait for the Mackenzie — and keep the rest
aboard to bring the ship out in the summer of 1853.

Armstrong put his objection in writing:
[[artifact:mcclure-investigator-026|the men were "utterly unfit for the
performance of the service"]]. Read one way, McClure was a hard captain
gambling his men's lives on the chance of being the first ship through. Read
another, he was a commander with no supply line, no communications and one
season's food, choosing between two bad options. Both readings are in the
sources. What is not in dispute is that the officers ate better than the men,
and that the men asked for food and did not get it.

## January to June 1853

The third winter was the worst. On 6 January 1853 the mean temperature over
twenty-four hours was −61.6 °F. Through February and March, Armstrong wrote,
"a few hours illness would produce the most alarming degree of prostration I
have ever witnessed... and scurvy of an aggravated character seldom failed to
supervene."

Then, in eight days in April, three men died:

- **John Boyle**, able seaman, on the night of 5 April, of dysentery on a
  scorbutic habit ([[artifact:mcclure-investigator-027]]);
- **John Ames**, able seaman, on 11 April, of dropsy following a heart
  complaint;
- **John Kerr**, gunner's mate, on 12 or 13 April, of general dropsy after long
  scurvy and debility.

(Armstrong dates Kerr's death the 12th; the crew list printed in Osborn's
edition says the 13th.)

[[person:Bedford Pim]] walked into the bay on 6 April, between the first death
and the second, while four men were ashore digging Boyle's grave. Two more of
the Investigator's people died afterwards: Mate H. H. Sainsbury aboard the
Resolute in the drifting pack on 14 November 1853, and able seaman Thomas
Morgan aboard the North Star at [[place:Beechey Island]] on 22 May 1854, buried
there beside Franklin's three men.

[[person:Alexander Armstrong]] and [[person:William Thomas Domville]] of the
Resolute surveyed the whole crew and reported on 23 May 1853 that they were
[[artifact:mcclure-investigator-020|"utterly unfit to undergo the rigour of
another winter in this climate"]]. The ship was abandoned on 3 June. A cairn
with a record of the abandonment was left, stores were landed on the beach, and
a memorial tablet was raised over the three graves.

## What is there now

The graves and McClure's Cache are on the shore, inside
[[place:Aulavik National Park]]; the wreck stands upright on the bottom of
[[place:Mercy Bay]] in about eleven metres of water, just outside the park
boundary. Parks Canada found her by sonar in July 2010 — about fifteen minutes
into the survey — dived her in 2011, and returned in May 2026 with a remotely
operated vehicle lowered through two metres of sea ice, at the request of the
park's Cooperative Management Board.

For a century and a half the stores McClure landed were a resource, not a
relic. Inuvialuit families travelled to Mercy Bay for the iron, copper, wood and
canvas, and material from the Investigator has been documented at sites across
the western Arctic. The ship that came looking for Franklin ended up, in the
end, supplying the people whose country she was mapping.
