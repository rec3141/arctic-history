Between 1898 and 1911 three men worked the same stretch of the map — the
channels and islands west and north of Baffin Bay — for three different
purposes, and between them they settled who the western high Arctic would
belong to.

[[person:Otto Sverdrup]] went to look for the north end of Greenland, was
turned back by ice, and spent four winters instead surveying land no chart had
shown. [[person:Robert Edwin Peary]] used the Ellesmere shore and the labour
of the Inughuit as the launching ramp for an assault on the Pole.
[[person:Joseph-Elzear Bernier]] went north every summer in a government ship
to plant flags, collect fifty dollars a head from Scottish whalers, and leave
documents in cairns. None of the three set out to decide a question of
sovereignty. All three did.

## The Fram's four winters, 1898-1902

The [[artifact:sverdrup-bernier-peary-001|second Fram expedition]] sailed from
Christiania on 24 June 1898 with sixteen men aboard: Sverdrup, his second
[[person:Victor Baumann]], the cartographer
[[person:Gunerius Ingvald Isachsen]], the geologist [[person:Per Schei]], the
botanist [[person:Herman Georg Simmons]], the hunter [[person:Ivar Fosheim]],
the seaman [[person:Sverre Hassel]] who would later stand at the South Pole
with Amundsen, and nine others, among them the cook
[[person:Adolf Henrik Lindstrom]], who had already served in one polar ship and
would serve in two more.

The plan was to sail up the west coast of Greenland and round its northern
end. Ice in Kane Basin ended that in the first season and again in the second.
Sverdrup turned west instead, into [[place:Jones Sound]], and the expedition
became something else: four winters — at [[place:Fram Havn]] in Rice Strait,
at [[place:Harbour Fiord]], and twice at [[place:Goose Fiord]] — and a
programme of sledge surveys that put about 260,000 square kilometres of new
coast on the chart. [[place:Axel Heiberg Island]],
[[place:Amund Ringnes Island]], [[place:Ellef Ringnes Island]] and
[[place:King Christian Island]] all take their names from that survey, and
so does [[place:Eureka Sound]]. Two men died: the doctor
[[person:Johan Svendsen]], who shot himself in the first spring, and
[[person:Ove Braskerud]], of pneumonia in the autumn that followed. Both are
buried on Ellesmere Island. See [[page:sverdrup-four-winters-on-a-new-coast]].

## Bernier's patrols, 1904-1911

Canada had been handed the Arctic islands by an imperial order in council in
1880 and had done nothing with them for twenty years. American whalers were
wintering in Hudson Bay, Scottish whalers were working the Baffin coast on
nobody's licence, Peary was coming and going as he pleased, and a Norwegian
had just spent four years mapping islands no Canadian had seen.

Ottawa's answer was a ship. [[person:Albert Peter Low]] took the Neptune north
in 1903-04 and read a proclamation at Leopold Harbour. Then Bernier took the
C.G.S. Arctic — a Kiel-built sealer bought for the purpose — north almost every
year from 1904 to 1911. He annexed islands one at a time, building cairns and
leaving papers in bottles: [[artifact:sverdrup-bernier-peary-017|Bylot Island
on 21 August 1906]], Baffin Island at [[place:Albert Harbour]] on 9 November
1906, then Griffith, Cornwallis, Bathurst, Byam Martin, Melville, Prince
Patrick, Banks and the rest. He issued whaling licences at fifty dollars each
and entered them in a fisheries return. And on 1 July 1909, at
[[place:Winter Harbour]] on [[place:Melville Island]], where Parry's men had
wintered ninety years before — see [[topic:ross-parry]] — he unveiled a tablet
on Parry's Rock claiming
[[artifact:sverdrup-bernier-peary-018|the whole of the Arctic archipelago]] for
Canada, from 60 degrees west to 141 degrees west and up to the Pole. See
[[page:bernier-and-the-paper-claim]].

## Peary on the Ellesmere shore

Peary belongs in this story sideways. His base was the Inughuit country of
north-west Greenland, but his sledging ground was Ellesmere and the ocean
beyond it. He reoccupied Greely's abandoned station at [[place:Fort Conger]] —
see [[topic:nares-greely]] — broke the big house into three small huts on the
Inughuit model, and lost eight toes there to frostbite in the winter of 1899.
The Norwegians were the first outsiders to hear of it:
[[artifact:sverdrup-bernier-peary-010|"You must take your chances up here, you
know."]]

His expeditions ran on Inughuit labour. Inughuit hunters fed his parties,
Inughuit women sewed the clothing, Inughuit drivers handled the dogs, and four
Inughuit men — [[person:Ootah]], [[person:Egingwah]], [[person:Seegloo]] and
[[person:Ooqueah]] — went the whole distance in 1909 with
[[person:Matthew Henson]]. He also took away three meteorites the Inughuit had
used as their only source of iron for centuries, and in 1897 he took away six
people, four of whom died within the year in New York; one of the survivors was
the boy [[person:Minik Wallace]]. See
[[page:peary-the-inughuit-and-the-ellesmere-shore]] and
[[topic:greenland-inughuit]].

Whether Peary reached the Pole on 6 April 1909, and whether
[[person:Frederick Cook]] reached it a year earlier, has been argued for more
than a century. It is a smaller question than it looks, and it gets a section
rather than a page here: [[page:the-pole-controversy-in-one-page]].

## What it added up to

Sverdrup's survey gave Norway a claim in international law: he had discovered
and mapped the islands, claimed them for the Norwegian king in the last lines
of his own book, and no other state had occupied them. Norway never pressed it
hard, but it sat there. Bernier's cairns and tablets gave Canada the
counter-argument, and the Royal Canadian Mounted Police posts that followed —
Craig Harbour and [[place:Bache Peninsula]] in the 1920s — gave it the
occupation that international law by then increasingly required.

On 11 November 1930 Norway formally recognised Canadian sovereignty over the
Sverdrup Islands, and Canada paid Sverdrup 67,000 dollars for his original
maps, diaries and documents. He was seventy-six and ill. He died fifteen days
later. Forty years after that, drilling found ten trillion cubic feet of gas
under the basin named for him. See
[[page:the-sverdrup-islands-and-the-1930-settlement]].

None of this asked the people who lived there. The Inuit of
[[place:Mittimatalik]] and the Inughuit of [[place:Etah]] were the labour, the
teachers and the audience of these ceremonies, and they were made subjects of
a state most of them had not heard of. Within a generation the same sovereignty
argument would be used to move Inuit families north to
[[place:Ausuittuq]] and [[place:Qausuittuq]] — see [[topic:relocations-qtc]].
That is the through-line from a flag on Bylot Island in 1906 to the High Arctic
relocations of 1953.
