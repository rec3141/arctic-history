The most consequential thing [[person:Joseph-Elzear Bernier]] did on his
voyages was not the tablet. It was a receipt book.

## Fifty dollars a ship

Scottish whalers had worked Davis Strait and Baffin Bay since the 1810s and had
shore stations in Cumberland Sound from the 1850s — see [[topic:whaling]]. They
paid nobody, cleared no customs, and answered to nobody nearer than Dundee.
American whalers wintering in Hudson Bay were in the same position. By 1900 the
bowhead was nearly gone and the trade was living on fox, sealskin, ivory and
Inuit labour, but the pattern of unregulated foreign operation was exactly what
made Canadian officials nervous.

Parliament passed a licensing law, and Bernier went north with the papers. On
1 October 1906 at [[place:Albert Harbour]] he went aboard the Dundee steamer
Eclipse and told [[person:James Mutch]], the firms' representative, that the
new law called for a fifty-dollar licence fee. Mutch, who had spent decades in
Cumberland Sound and at Pond Inlet, "immediately gave me an order for this
payment by the owners of his ship." Bernier then made an inventory of the
dutiable goods on board.

Over the 1906-07 voyage he issued ten licences at fifty dollars — the Diana,
the Balaena, the Eclipse, the Morning and one other, two licences each — five
hundred dollars in all. Two he could not collect. The Scotia and the Snowdrop
were never in his neighbourhood. The Windward, the ship that had carried
[[person:Robert Edwin Peary]] north in 1898, was wrecked on the Carey Islands
on 26 June 1907; Captain Cooney and his crew sailed the boats to Pond Inlet and
were taken aboard another Dundee ship.

He also could not collect at Kekerten, because when he sent an officer ashore
on 28 August 1907 he learned the station agent, W. F. Milne, had died on the
13th "under suspicious circumstances." Bernier landed the ship's doctor and
some officers to hold an inquest. They found Milne had shot himself, and read
the letters he had left on his desk. Blacklead, two days later, had no agent at
all — he had gone home the year before, "as we learned from the few natives at
this station."

Those four sentences in a fisheries return are also a picture of the whaling
economy collapsing: stations without agents, an agent dead of loneliness, a
handful of Inuit left at a post nobody was running any more.

## What a licence actually did

Very little, directly. Five hundred dollars did not pay for a day of the
Arctic's coal. But a licence is a record of a foreign master accepting that a
Canadian officer had authority over him in those waters, and it is the sort of
thing an international tribunal counts. Customs entries, licences, inquests,
mail carried, and a magistrate aboard the annual patrol are the substance of
"effective occupation"; the cairns are the decoration.

The first criminal trial in the eastern Arctic followed the same logic: in 1923
a court was carried to Pond Inlet aboard a government ship to try
[[person:Nuqallaq]] and two others for the killing of the trader Robert Janes.
See [[topic:posts-rcmp-missions]].

## The Inuit side of it

The reports are thin on this and it should be said plainly. Bernier's ships
wintered twice at [[place:Albert Harbour]], within reach of the people of
[[place:Mittimatalik]]. His crews traded with them, hired them, hunted with
them and depended on their dogs and their knowledge. Two Inuit men, recorded
only as Monkeyshaw and Cameo, sailed north with him in 1906, and he left six
months' provisions with their wives. Beyond a handful of such names the
reports do not say who these people were.

What the record does show is that Inuit at Mittimatalik remembered him well:
they called him Kapitaikallak, the stout little captain, and elders interviewed
in the early 2000s spoke of him warmly. It also shows the other side. His
voyages brought diseases against which the people of the north had little
immunity, and his crews hunted hard on ground that Inuit families depended on.
Both things are true at once. See [[source:maceachern2025bernier]] and
[[artifact:sverdrup-bernier-peary-016|the photograph of Bernier among Inuit]].
