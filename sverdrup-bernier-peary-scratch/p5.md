This page exists so that the question does not take over the topic. It is the
most famous thing about [[person:Robert Edwin Peary]] and among the least
important.

## What was claimed

[[person:Frederick Cook]], a doctor who had sailed with Peary in 1891-92 and
with the Belgica in 1897-99, wintered at [[place:Anoatok]] in northern
Greenland in 1907-08 with backing from a New York sportsman. He said he left
land in March 1908 with two young Inughuit, [[person:Ahwelah]] and
[[person:Etukishook]], crossed Ellesmere Island, went out over the sea ice from
Cape Stallworthy on Axel Heiberg Island, and reached the Pole on 21 April 1908.
He then said he was carried west by drift, wintered in a cave on the Devon
Island coast, and got back to Greenland in the spring of 1909. He telegraphed
the news from Shetland on 1 September 1909.

Peary left [[place:Cape Columbia]] on 1 March 1909 with a large party organised
in relays. [[person:Ross Gilmore Marvin]] turned back at 86 degrees 38 minutes
on 26 March; [[person:Robert Bartlett]], the ship's master and the only other
man in the party who could take a navigational observation, was turned back at
87 degrees 47 minutes on 1 April. Peary went on with [[person:Matthew Henson]]
and [[person:Ootah]], [[person:Egingwah]], [[person:Seegloo]] and
[[person:Ooqueah]], said he reached the Pole on 6 April, and was back at Cape
Columbia on 23 April. He telegraphed from Labrador on 6 September 1909, five
days after Cook.

## What is thought now

Cook's claim is not accepted. The University of Copenhagen examined what he
submitted in December 1909 and found it did not prove anything — not that he
had lied, but that there was nothing to judge. Questioned separately afterwards,
Ahwelah and Etukishook each described a journey that never lost sight of land.
Photographs Cook published as "Bradley Land" show no such land.

Peary's claim is disputed and cannot now be settled either way. The
difficulties are these. He sent back the last man who could independently fix a
position, which was contrary to every practice of the time and which he never
adequately explained. The daily distances he reported after Bartlett turned
back are far higher than anything he achieved on the way out, or than Bartlett
managed on the way back over the same ice. He took no longitude observations at
all, navigating by dead reckoning on a compass course, which on drifting sea ice
accumulates error fast. His original diary has features that have been read
both as evidence of fabrication and as ordinary field practice. A 1989 National
Geographic Society study of his photographs and soundings concluded he came
within a few miles; a 1996 study of the same evidence concluded he did not.

The reasonable summary is: Peary probably got closer to the Pole than anyone
before him, probably did not stand on it, and cannot be shown either way from
the record he left.

## The part that is not in doubt

Six men made that journey and five of them were not Peary. Whatever the party
achieved, [[person:Matthew Henson]] and the four Inughuit achieved it with him,
travelled the same ice, and came back. Ootah later gave his own account of the
journey to Knud Rasmussen.

And one man did not come back. Marvin, sent home from 86 degrees 38 minutes,
died on the return. Peary reported that he had drowned at a lead of young ice.
Years later Kudlooktoo, one of the two Inughuit travelling with him, said that
he had shot Marvin. There was no trial, no inquest and no independent
investigation; the killing, if it was one, happened outside any jurisdiction
either government was willing to exercise. That silence is a better illustration
of what sovereignty meant in the high Arctic in 1909 than the tablet at
[[place:Winter Harbour]] is.
