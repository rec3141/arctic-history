[[person:Otto Sverdrup]] had no intention of acquiring territory. He was
looking for the north end of Greenland, could not get there, and mapped what
was in front of him instead. But under the law of the time, discovery and
survey by a private expedition flying a national flag created an inchoate title
that the state could take up. Norway had one, over
[[place:Axel Heiberg Island]], [[place:Amund Ringnes Island]],
[[place:Ellef Ringnes Island]] and [[place:King Christian Island]] — some
150,000 square kilometres.

## Twenty-eight years of not quite arguing

Norway never pressed it. There was no Norwegian interest in the western high
Arctic beyond the whaling and sealing grounds much further east, and the
country had only been fully independent since 1905. But the claim was there, it
was mentioned in Norwegian print, and Canadian officials knew it.

They also knew that Bernier's method — the cairns, the bottles,
[[artifact:sverdrup-bernier-peary-018|the Dominion Day tablet]] — was not
enough on its own. International law in this period was moving towards
"effective occupation": the state that actually administers a territory holds
it. The 1928 arbitration over Palmas and the 1933 Permanent Court judgment on
eastern Greenland — a case between Norway and Denmark — both turned on that
principle. Ceremonies did not count for much; police posts, licences and mail
did.

So Canada built the apparatus. Royal Canadian Mounted Police detachments went
up at Craig Harbour and Pond Inlet in 1922, Dundas Harbour in 1924, and
[[place:Bache Peninsula]] in 1926 — the last of these the most northerly police
post in the world, staffed with Inughuit families brought from Greenland to
make it work, and closed in 1933 because it could not be supplied. The Eastern
Arctic Patrol went north every summer with a magistrate and a doctor aboard,
and [[person:Joseph-Elzear Bernier]] sailed with it into the 1920s.

## Eleven days in November 1930

On 11 November 1930 Norway formally recognised Canadian sovereignty over the
Sverdrup Islands. The note was drafted with care: Norway recognised Canada's
title over these particular islands, and expressly did not thereby recognise
any general sector claim to the Arctic. That distinction still matters, because
the sector theory — Canada owns everything between 60 and 141 degrees west up
to the Pole, which is what Bernier's tablet asserts — has never been accepted
in international law and is not the basis of Canada's position today.

Canada paid Sverdrup 67,000 dollars for his original charts, diaries and
papers. He was seventy-six, ill, and had spent most of his life at sea for very
little money. He died on 26 November 1930, fifteen days after the settlement.

In the same year, at the Department of the Interior in Ottawa, the director of
the North-West Territories and Yukon branch wrote to his deputy minister that
"there is no record showing that Captain Bernier was ever, at any time,
formally commissioned by our Government to claim any areas in the Arctic for
Canada." Bernier had sailed under a royal commission and had printed it in his
reports. The department was building a tidier story in which sovereignty came
from patient administration rather than from a man with a bronze plaque, and
Bernier did not fit it. See [[source:maceachern2025bernier]].

## What it cost, and who paid

The islands changed hands twice — from Britain to Canada in 1880, from a
Norwegian claim to a Canadian one in 1930 — without anyone asking the people
who lived in the region. There were no permanent Inuit settlements on the
Sverdrup Islands themselves; there were, and are, communities all round them,
and the archaeological record on Ellesmere and Axel Heiberg is thick with Thule
and Dorset sites — see [[topic:thule-dorset]].

The logic that made ceremonies insufficient and occupation necessary did not
stop in 1930. In 1953 and 1955 the federal government moved Inuit families from
Inukjuak in northern Quebec and from Mittimatalik to
[[place:Ausuittuq]] (Grise Fiord) on Ellesmere and
[[place:Qausuittuq]] (Resolute) on Cornwallis, told them they could return
after two years, and did not let them. Government documents of the period
discuss the sovereignty value of having Canadian citizens living in the high
Arctic. The Royal Commission on Aboriginal Peoples reported on it in 1994; the
Qikiqtani Truth Commission heard the testimony again in 2008-10; Canada
apologised in 2010. See [[topic:relocations-qtc]].

That is the line from a flag on [[place:Canada Point]] in 1906 to Grise Fiord in
1953: the same argument, made first with cairns, then with police posts, and
finally with people.
