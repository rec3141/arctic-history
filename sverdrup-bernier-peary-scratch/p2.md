[[person:Otto Sverdrup]] was forty-four when the Fram sailed for the second
time, on 24 June 1898. He had crossed the Greenland ice cap with Nansen in
1888 and had commanded the same ship through her three-year drift in the polar
pack. This time the ship was his. The plan, agreed with his backers — the
Christiania brewers Amund and Ellef Ringnes and the consul Axel Heiberg — was
to sail up the west coast of Greenland, round its northern end, and come down
the east side.

He never got past Smith Sound.

## Sixteen men

The crew is listed in the first pages of [[source:sverdrup1904newland]]:
[[person:Victor Baumann]], a naval lieutenant, second in command;
[[person:Oluf Raanes]], mate; [[person:Gunerius Ingvald Isachsen]], a cavalry
lieutenant and the expedition's cartographer;
[[person:Herman Georg Simmons]], a Swedish botanist; [[person:Edvard Bay]], a
Danish zoologist; [[person:Johan Svendsen]], the doctor;
[[person:Per Schei]], geologist; [[person:Peder Leonard Hendriksen]],
harpooner, the only man aboard who had been in the Fram before;
[[person:Karl Olsen]], chief engineer; [[person:Jacob Nodtvedt]], second
engineer; [[person:Ivar Fosheim]], hunter;
[[person:Adolf Henrik Lindstrom]], cook; [[person:Sverre Hassel]], seaman;
[[person:Rudolf Stolz]] and [[person:Ove Braskerud]], stokers and general
hands. See [[artifact:sverdrup-bernier-peary-007|the photograph of them all]].

## The first winter, and two deaths

The ship was frozen in at [[place:Fram Havn]] in Rice Strait in September 1898.
Peary's Windward was beset a few miles away at [[place:Payer Harbour]], and the
two expeditions met on the ice on 6 October:
[[artifact:sverdrup-bernier-peary-009|Peary shook hands and declined the
coffee]]. It set the tone. Peary regarded the Ellesmere shore as his and said
so in print; Sverdrup, who was a working sealing captain rather than a
publicist, mapped it anyway.

Inughuit began arriving in March. [[person:Kolotengva]] came first, four days
out from Karnah in Inglefield Gulf on his way to Peary's ship, and
[[artifact:sverdrup-bernier-peary-011|read the Norwegians' chart, Fosheim
wrote, as easily as a professor of geography]]. He stayed a week, sang and
drummed for them one evening in the fore-cabin, and then guided Baumann and
Hassel north to the Windward, where they found
[[artifact:sverdrup-bernier-peary-010|Peary in his berth with eight toes
newly amputated]]. Fosheim's account of the visits is also, plainly, a record
of Norwegian condescension: within a fortnight the ship's company were
"heartily sick of them all."

Then the deaths. Svendsen, the doctor, shot himself in early June 1899 on a
sledge journey inland from Alexandra Fiord; his body was sewn into sailcloth
and hauled back to the ship, which it reached on 15 June. Braskerud died of
pneumonia that autumn, at the second winter quarters, of an illness nobody left
alive could diagnose. Sverdrup wrote: "Winter was before us, the doctor dead,
Braskerud dead, Peder still ill in his berth, and Nodtvedt ailing — we had
fallen on evil days."

## West, and the new land

Barred again from the north in the summer of 1899, Sverdrup turned the ship
south round Ellesmere and west into [[place:Jones Sound]]. She wintered at
[[place:Harbour Fiord]] in 1899-1900 and then at [[place:Goose Fiord]], where
the ice held her for two winters running. "This Gaasefjord is a very remarkable
place," he wrote at the end. "I shall take good care not to set foot in
Gaasefjord again."

From those anchorages the sledge parties went out every spring. In 1900 they
crossed to the great island they named [[place:Axel Heiberg Island]] for their
patron; in 1901 they came up the channel between it and Ellesmere and named it
[[place:Eureka Sound]] — Sverdrup's chapter heading is simply "Heureka!" And
between 13 April and 31 May 1901 Isachsen and Hassel made
[[artifact:sverdrup-bernier-peary-003|the journey that mattered most]]: seven
weeks, two men and two dog teams, round [[place:Amund Ringnes Island]], west
to sight and name [[place:King Christian Island]], and up the far side of
[[place:Ellef Ringnes Island]] to Cape Isachsen. They also disposed of several
islands that Sir Edward Belcher had charted in the 1850s and that did not
exist: "It is possible that what Belcher saw were icebergs."

The dogs did the work and paid for it. On 21 April a dog called Peary, "worn
out, poor thing," was shot behind a hummock; Hassel gave him half a ration
first, because "so much might be done for an old servant."

## What four winters produced

The Fram reached Stavanger on 19 September 1902, four years and three months
out. The pilot who came aboard did not recognise the ship. Sverdrup's parties
had charted something on the order of 260,000 square kilometres of new coast —
more new land than any expedition of the century — along with Schei's geology,
Simmons's flora and Bay's zoology, published over the following decades as a
multi-volume scientific report.

Norway paid for none of it afterwards. The expedition had been privately
financed, the scientific results took thirty years to appear, and the state
showed no appetite for the Arctic empire its captain had accidentally acquired.
The claim sat unused for twenty-eight years and was then traded away in a week:
see [[page:the-sverdrup-islands-and-the-1930-settlement]].
