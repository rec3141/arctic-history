Between 1886 and 1909 [[person:Robert Edwin Peary]] made eight Arctic
expeditions. Every one of them ran on Inughuit labour, and most of them worked
their sledging from the Ellesmere shore of the Canadian Archipelago. He belongs
in this topic not for the Pole but for what he did on the ground between
[[place:Etah]] and [[place:Cape Sheridan]].

## Fort Conger, and the toes

[[place:Fort Conger]] was the station Adolphus Greely's Lady Franklin Bay
expedition built on Ellesmere in 1881 and abandoned in 1883, leaving nineteen of
twenty-five men dead — see [[topic:nares-greely]]. Peary reoccupied it from
1899. He did not use it as Greely had. He broke the single large timber house
into three small huts on the Inughuit plan, each easy to heat with a blubber
lamp, which is a better piece of practical Arctic engineering than most things
his expeditions are remembered for.

It was on the journey there in February 1899 that he froze his feet. Eight toes
were amputated at Fort Conger. The first outsiders to hear of it were the
Norwegians of the Fram, when [[person:Victor Baumann]] sledged up to the beset
Windward in March 1899 and found Peary in his berth:
[[artifact:sverdrup-bernier-peary-010|"You must take your chances up here, you
know."]] Nine years later, hunting muskox near Cape Sheridan, Peary was still
writing about
[[artifact:sverdrup-bernier-peary-033|"the stumps of both my feet"]].

## Who did the work

The 1908-09 expedition took on Inughuit families at Cape York and Etah with
their dogs, and landed them again a year later. The women sewed every garment
the expedition wore; the men hunted the meat it ate and the dogs it drove, and
built the snow houses it slept in. [[person:Matthew Henson]], an African
American from Maryland who had been with Peary since 1891, spoke Inuktun,
built the sledges, and was the link between the two.

Four Inughuit made the whole of the final journey: [[person:Ootah]],
[[person:Egingwah]], [[person:Seegloo]] and [[person:Ooqueah]]. Henson names
them, which Peary's public accounts often did not:
[[artifact:sverdrup-bernier-peary-032|"We were six: Peary, the commander, the
Esquimos, Ootah, Egingwah, Seegloo and Ooqueah, and myself."]] The
[[artifact:sverdrup-bernier-peary-027|photograph of the five flags]] is a
picture of the four of them and Henson — Peary is behind the camera. In the
American press it became a picture of Peary's achievement.

Henson's own admiration is entangled with the racial language of 1912; he
compares the four men's fidelity to the dogs' in the same sentence in which he
calls them devoted companions. He was himself, in the same book, "a lowly
member of my race." The United States did not formally honour Henson until
1944, twenty-four years after Peary's death.

Payment was in goods — rifles, ammunition, knives, boat timber, wood — and it
was real payment by the standards of the time and place. It was also payment
made by the only buyer, to people who had no way of pricing the transaction and
who bore the risk of the journey.

## What was taken

Two things Peary removed are still argued about.

The first is iron. Three masses of the Cape York meteorite — the Inughuit called
them the Tent, the Woman and the Dog — had been the only source of metal in
north-west Greenland for centuries; harpoon heads and knife blades were made
from flakes hammered off them. Peary shipped all three to New York between 1894
and 1897 and sold them to the American Museum of Natural History for forty
thousand dollars. [[artifact:sverdrup-bernier-peary-031|Ahnighito, the largest,
is still on display there]], on a mount carried down to Manhattan bedrock. The
Inughuit received nothing.

The second is people. In 1897 Peary brought six Inughuit to New York in the
Hope at the request of the anthropologist Franz Boas, who wanted living
informants. They were housed in the museum's basement. Four died of
tuberculosis within a year, including [[person:Qisuk]]. His son, a boy of about
seven called [[person:Minik Wallace]], was given a staged funeral to attend — a
weighted log wrapped in cloth — while the museum kept his father's skeleton in
its collection. Minik found out as a teenager and spent years demanding the
bones. He got nowhere, returned to Greenland in 1909, could no longer speak the
language, came back to the United States, and died in the influenza pandemic of
1918 aged about twenty-eight.
[[artifact:sverdrup-bernier-peary-030|A photograph of him at seven]] survives.

The remains of the four were returned to Qaanaaq and buried there in 1993,
ninety-six years after they were taken. See [[topic:greenland-inughuit]].

## Why it belongs to a page about sovereignty

Peary's presence was one of the reasons Canada started sending ships north. He
used [[place:Fort Conger]], [[place:Cape Sheridan]] and the whole Ellesmere
coast as if they were unowned, because in any practical sense they were. He
named features after his American backers. When [[person:Joseph-Elzear Bernier]]
sailed into Smith Sound in 1908 on a patrol, part of the point was to be seen
doing it.
