On 1 September 1880 an imperial order in council handed Canada everything
Britain claimed north of the mainland: "all the British territory in the
northern waters of the continent of America and Arctic ocean, from 60 degrees
west longitude to 141 degrees west longitude, and as far north as 90 degrees
north latitude." Britain was giving away land it had never occupied and in
places had never seen. Canada, for twenty years, did nothing whatever with it.

By 1900 that was becoming awkward. American whalers were wintering in Hudson
Bay and trading on their own terms. Scottish whalers had worked the Baffin
coast for forty years under nobody's licence. [[person:Robert Edwin Peary]]
came and went as he pleased through Smith Sound. And a Norwegian captain had
just spent four years mapping islands no Canadian official had ever seen.

## The Neptune and the Arctic

Ottawa's response was to buy a ship and send it north with a policeman aboard.
[[person:Albert Peter Low]] took the D.G.S. Neptune in 1903-04, wintered at
Fullerton Harbour, and on 16 August 1904 read a proclamation at Leopold Harbour
taking possession of North Somerset. Two years later
[[person:Joseph-Elzear Bernier]] found the sealed metal box and copied the
document into his own report.

Bernier was a master mariner from L'Islet on the St Lawrence who had commanded
his first ship at seventeen and had spent years lobbying for a Canadian polar
expedition. He never got that. What he got instead was the annual patrol, and
he made it his life's work: voyages in 1904 (as sailing master under Low's
successor), 1906-07, 1908-09 and 1910-11, in the C.G.S. Arctic, a Kiel-built
sealer bought for the purpose.

## How a claim was made

The method was the same every time. Land at a point, build a cairn, hoist a
flag, read a document, put a copy in a bottle and seal it into the cairn. Cut
the ship's name into a stone. Take a photograph.
[[artifact:sverdrup-bernier-peary-014|Bylot Island, 21 August 1906]], reads in
full:

> This island, Bylot Island, was graciously given to the Dominion of Canada,
> by the Imperial Government in the year 1880, and being ordered to take
> possession of it in the name of Canada, know all men that on this day the
> Canadian Government Steamer Arctic, anchored here, and I planted the
> Canadian flag and took possession of Bylot Island in the name of Canada.

They named the spot [[place:Canada Point]], after the C.G.S. Canada. Over two
voyages Bernier repeated the ceremony on Baffin, Griffith, Cornwallis,
Bathurst, Byam Martin, Melville, Prince Patrick, Cobourg, Banks and Victoria
islands, among others — and at [[place:Albert Harbour]] on 9 November 1906,
[[artifact:sverdrup-bernier-peary-013|with a large gathering of Inuit
watching]], on Baffin Island itself.

## Dominion Day, 1909

The Arctic left Quebec on 28 July 1908, at half past one in the afternoon,
steaming out through the British, French and American warships assembled for
the Quebec Tercentenary while the band of H.M.S. Indomitable played Auld Lang
Syne. She patrolled Davis Strait, Baffin Bay, Melville Bay, Smith Sound,
Lancaster Sound, Barrow Strait, Melville Sound and M'Clure Strait, and on 28
August 1908 came into [[place:Winter Harbour]] on [[place:Melville Island]],
where Parry's Hecla and Griper had wintered in 1819-20 — see
[[topic:ross-parry]]. She stayed eleven months.
[[artifact:sverdrup-bernier-peary-022|The chart of the harbour]] made that
winter is one of the first proper surveys of the place.

On 1 July 1909 the ship's company assembled at Parry's Rock, the boulder on
which Parry's men had cut their ships' names in 1820. The chief engineer
[[person:John Van Koenig]] had cut a bronze tablet. Bernier's own account:

> all assembled around Parry's rock to witness the unveiling of a tablet
> placed on the rock, commemorating the annexing of the whole of the Arctic
> archipelago... That we had annexed a number of islands one by one and a
> large area of territory by landing, that we now claimed all islands and
> territory within the degrees 141 and 60 west longitude as Canadian
> territory, and now under Canadian jurisdiction.

Three cheers for the Premier, and the men spent the afternoon picking wild
flowers. The tablet is still on the rock. See
[[artifact:sverdrup-bernier-peary-018|the passage]] and
[[artifact:sverdrup-bernier-peary-015|the photograph]].

It was, legally, a large assertion made on thin evidence — a single ceremony
claiming an archipelago the size of western Europe, most of which the ship had
never approached. Bernier knew it, which is why he kept building cairns island
by island as well. What eventually secured the claim was not the tablet but the
police posts, the licences and the fifty years of administration that followed.

## Afterwards

Bernier made two more voyages, in 1910-11, and then the government stopped
sending him; he spent the rest of his career trading in the north on his own
account and campaigning, without success, for the polar expedition he had
always wanted. He died in 1934. The Musee maritime du Quebec at L'Islet, his
birthplace, keeps his papers.
