# Henry Hudson's Fourth Voyage (1610–11): Extraction from Asher, *Henry Hudson the Navigator* (Hakluyt Society, 1860)

Source file: `db/history/davis-baffin-hudson/files/davis-baffin-hudson-asher1860-hudson.txt` (OCR, ~24,200 lines).

## What is actually in this OCR, and what is not

The volume reprints, from Purchas's *Pilgrims*/*Pilgrimage*, four documents on the fourth voyage:

- **"An Abstract of the Journall of Master Henry Hudson"** — Hudson's own log, in the third person, covering 17 April – 3 August 1610 only (pp. 93–97 of the original pagination, printed as running heads "ABSTRACT OF THE JOURNAL").
- **"A Larger Discourse of the same Voyage... written by Abacuk Pricket"** — the main narrative used below, pp. 98–134 ("WRITTEN BY ABACUK PRICKETT").
- A short letter from Iceland, dated 30 May 1610, headed (confusingly) "Master Samuel Machins Letter" — it is addressed *to* one Macham, not written by him; Asher notes Purchas is too confused to say whether the true author is Hudson, Juet, or Wydhouse (Introduction, p. xv, footnote). Pp. 135–136.
- **"A Note found in the Deske of Thomas Wydowse"** (also spelled Woodhouse/Wydhouse/Widowes), the mathematics student who was among those set adrift — records the shipboard trial of Robert Juet, 10 September 1610. Pp. 136–138.
- Purchas's own chapter **"Of Hudson's Discoveries and Death"** (*Purchas his Pilgrimage*, fol., London, 1626, p. 817), pp. 139–144, which supplies the Digges Island fight and the 6 September 1611 homecoming in Purchas's own third-person voice, drawing on "him who was a speciall setter forth of the voyage" (i.e., Sir Dudley Digges) as well as on Pricket.

Asher's Introduction (pp. xi–xviii and a synoptic table at pp. ccix–ccxi) supplies dating and cross-references used throughout below.

**Not found in this file:** any transcript of "examinations of the survivors before the High Court of Admiralty" and any account of an "1618 Admiralty trial and acquittal." I grepped exhaustively for "examination," "admiralty," "1618," "acquit," "prosecut," "punish," etc. The only trial recorded anywhere in this text is the **shipboard** trial and deposing of Robert Juet on 10 September 1610 (Wydowse's note, pp. 136–138), which is a wholly different event from any later Admiralty proceeding against the survivors. Asher's introduction never mentions 1618 or any post-return legal proceeding; it only says the ship and part of the crew "reached England in safety" (Introduction, p. x). I have not fabricated content for this item — see the Report for how to treat it.

## Verbatim quotations (verified against the OCR text)

1. **The seizing of Hudson.** *"then came John Thomas and Bennet before him, while Wilson bound his amies behind him."* — Abacuk Pricket, p. 121. [Note: the OCR renders "Wilson" as "\yilson" and "armes" as "amies" — clear scanning artifacts on this proper name and word, not variant period spelling; verified at that exact garbled reading in the file.] Dawn, 22 June 1611; ice in James Bay, approx. 53–55°N, 79–81°W.

2. **Pricket's own plea.** *"where, on my knees I besought them, for the love of God, to remember themselves, and to doe as they would be done unto."* — Abacuk Pricket, p. 121. Same time/place as above.

3. **The shallop cast off.** *"they cut her head fast from the sterne of our ship, then out with their top-sayles, and towards the east they stood in a cleere sea."* — Abacuk Pricket, p. 123. 22 June 1611; James Bay ice.

4. **Sharing out the bread.** *"and hee wept when hee gave it unto them."* [Hudson distributing the last bread, a pound a man, with a written "bill of returne" before turning back from the fruitless search south for the Inuit] — Abacuk Pricket, p. 116. c. mid-June 1611, mouth of the wintering bay, James Bay.

5. **Juet's part.** *"for hee sware plainly that he would justifie this deed when he came home."* — Abacuk Pricket describing Robert Juet joining the conspiracy, p. 119. Night of 21 June 1611, James Bay ice.

6. **Greene distancing Pricket from the mutiny (self-serving for Pricket).** *"he knew the worst, which was, to be hanged when hee came home, and therefore of the two he would rather be hanged at home then starved abroad"* — Henry Greene, quoted by Pricket, pp. 117–118. Night of 21 June 1611, James Bay ice.

7. **The Digges Island fight — Pricket kills his attacker.** *"and therewith strooke him into the bodie and the throate."* — Abacuk Pricket, p. 130. 29 July 1611, Digges Island, approx. 62.55°N, 77.5°W.

8. **Juet's death.** *"Then Robert Juet dyed for meere want, and all our men were in despaire."* — Abacuk Pricket, p. 133. c. early September 1611, western approaches, off Ireland.

## Source criticism of Pricket

Abacuk (Habakkuk) Pricket was not a mariner but the personal servant of Sir Dudley Digges, the principal financial backer of the voyage, placed aboard as Digges's man rather than for seamanship. According to Purchas's own gloss (p. 143), the mutineers spared Pricket's life specifically "in hope to procure his master to worke their pardon" — meaning his entire narrative was composed by a man whose freedom, and whose patron's goodwill toward the returned mutineers, depended on how the story was told. Asher's introduction (pp. xiii–xv) is blunt about this: the "Larger Discourse" is "in form a narrative," but "the author's real intention was much more to defend the mutineers than to describe the voyage," an "apologetical essay" that is "extremely clever" at casting a little, but not too much, blame on Hudson himself while throwing the main fault onto men already dead by the time the ship reached home (Greene, Wilson, Thomas, Perse) and presenting the survivors as blameless or meritorious. Purchas himself distrusted the account and says so in a postscript, which is why he printed alongside it the letter from Iceland and Thomas Wydhouse's note — explicitly so that "Pricket's account may in some degree be tested" (Introduction, p. xvi). Wydhouse's note, written by one of the men who was himself set adrift (and so had no motive to flatter the mutineers), places the blame for the "occasions of discord" squarely and specifically on Robert Juet, independently confirming Pricket's portrait of Juet as a chronic troublemaker going back to Iceland. Asher also flags Pricket as the *only* eyewitness account of the mutiny itself — "we can therefore not correct his statements, whether they be true or false" — and separately criticizes the "Larger Discourse" as geographically "most unsatisfactory," clearly written up from memory at home rather than from notes taken during the voyage. Wydhouse's note and Purchas's "Of Hudson's Discoveries and Death" both largely corroborate Pricket's account of the Digges Island fight and the roster of the shallop, but neither offers an independent, non-Pricket-derived account of what happened in the moments the mutiny broke out — for that, Pricket remains the sole source.
