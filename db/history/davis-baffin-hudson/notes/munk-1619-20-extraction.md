# Jens Munk's expedition to Hudson's Bay, 1619-20: extraction from Gosch 1897

Extracted by a research run on 2026-09-08. The English translation of Munk's own journal,
*Navigatio Septentrionalis* (Copenhagen, 1624), together with Gosch's introduction and
commentary, is Book II of Gosch, ed., *Danish Arctic Expeditions 1605 to 1620* (Hakluyt
Society, 1897). The file named in this project as
`db/history/davis-baffin-hudson/files/davis-baffin-hudson-gosch1897-munk.txt` is in fact
**Book I only** (the Greenland expeditions) — it contains no Munk narrative text (no hit
for "Churchill", "Nova Dania", etc.). The actual Book II text — Munk's dedication, the
day-by-day journal, and Gosch's introduction/commentary — is found instead in the staging
scans `db/history/_staging/danisharcticexp01munkgoog.txt` (used as primary source below;
page numbers are the printed page numbers embedded in the OCR, e.g. "24 NAVIGATIO
SEPTENTRIONALIS.") and duplicated in `danisharcticexp03munkgoog.txt`. This should be
flagged so the file on disk under `davis-baffin-hudson/files/` can be re-checked or
re-fetched as the wrong book.

## The expedition

King Christian IV of Denmark commissioned Jens Munk to search for the North-West Passage,
now to be called "Nova Dania," through "Fretum Christian" (Munk's name for Hudson Strait).
Two of the king's ships were used: the frigate **Enhiorningen** ("Unicorn") and the sloop
**Lamprenen** ("Lamprey"). Munk's own title page states the crew as "a Crew numbering in
all **64 Persons**," and gives the embarkation split explicitly: "there were then on the
ship Enhiorningen forty-eight, and, on the sloop Lamprenen, sixteen persons" (48 + 16 = 64).
Gosch's introduction uses the same figure, describing the outcome as sparing "three lives
only out of **sixty-four**" (p. xxxvi) — i.e. Gosch's own tally agrees exactly with Munk's
count and does not differ from it. No alternative figure of 65 appears anywhere in the text
searched.

The ships sailed from Copenhagen into the Sound on 9 May 1619 (Munk waited for wind and did
not actually clear the Sound until 16 May, Whitsunday), under the Danish crown of Christian IV.

## Chronology

- **9 May 1619** — sails from Copenhagen into the Sound.
- **18 May** — a sailor jumps overboard in the North Sea and is lost.
- **25-30 May** — sloop's leak found and repaired at Karmsund, Norway; a cooper dies there,
  three replacement men taken on at Skudenes to keep the complement at 64.
- **2 June** — passes Shetland ("Heth Land"); **4 June** — rounds the Faroes.
- **30 June** — sights Cape Farewell, Greenland, in 60°30'.
- **8-9 July** — sights land on the American side (Frobisher Bay/Resolution Island area) in
  about 62½°, held off by ice.
- **11-15 July** — enters and fights through the ice at "Munckenes" (Resolution Island),
  the north side of the entrance to Fretum Christian (Hudson Strait), in 61°20'; the ships
  are frozen fast together and a knee of the ship's frame is sprung and repaired at sea.
- **17-22 July** — anchors in a harbour Munk names "Rin Sund" ("Reindeer Sound"); meets and
  exchanges gifts with Inuit (see People, below).
- **10 August** — reaches the western side of Fretum Christian.
- **20 August** — in 62°20', working west through the strait past "lisver" and the "Sostrene"
  (Sisters) islands.
- **c. 4 September** — rounds Digges Eyland (Digges Island); from its northern point the
  course to "Jens Munckes Vindterhaffn" in Nova Dania is three days and three nights' sailing
  south-west.
- **7 September 1619** — enters the harbour at the mouth of the Churchill River, which Munk
  names "Jens Munckes Vindterhaffn" (Jens Munk's Winter Harbour), in the country he calls
  Nova Dania. The sloop, separated in the gale, rejoins on 9 September.
- **18-28 September** — the ship is brought up the river and grounded/docked on the western
  shore for the winter, with great difficulty against drift-ice.
- **21 November 1619** — first death of the wintering recorded ("a sailor who had long been
  ill was buried").
- **12 December** — the surgeon on Lamprenen, David Velske, dies; his body cannot be got
  ashore for burial for two days because of the cold.
- **10 January 1620** — the priest, Rasmus Jensen, and the ship's surgeon, Casper Caspersen,
  take to their beds; "violent sickness" (scurvy, compounded by dysentery) now spreads
  through the crew generally. The head cook dies the same day.
- **17 February** — twenty of the crew have now died (the manuscript's marginal tally, per
  Gosch's commentary, reads "21 corpses" under 10 February).
- **20 February** — the priest, Rasmus Jensen, dies.
- **21 March** — the surgeon Casper Caspersen dies; by Gosch's reckoning roughly half the
  crew are dead by the end of March.
- **10 April** — the lieutenant, Maurids (Mauritz) Stygge, dies.
- **12 May** — last date on which a body is buried in the ground; after this the dead can
  only be dragged on deck or thrown overboard.
- **4 June 1620 (Whit-Sunday)** — only Munk and three others remain alive (two ashore, one
  dying in the ship); Munk, believing he is about to die, writes his farewell journal entry
  (quoted in full below). The manuscript notes "61 dead" under this date.
- **8 June** — Munk crawls from the ship to join the two survivors ashore; the sailmaker
  (named "Martin" in the manuscript) dies about this time, leaving three survivors in all.
- **June (after the 8th)** — the three survivors crawl about the shore eating raw greens/roots.
- **18 June** — begin catching fish; slow recovery.
- **26 June** — begin the work of refloating Lamprenen alongside Enhiorningen and clearing
  the decomposed dead from the ship.
- **16 July 1620** — departs the winter harbour in Lamprenen, having named it "Jens Munckes
  Bay" after himself; scuttles Enhiorningen in place by drilling holes in her.
- **20-25 July** — beset in ice on the homeward crossing of Hudson Bay; the rudder is broken.
- **September 5-12** — crosses the North Atlantic, taking daily latitudes (58°59', 59°15',
  60°19').
- **14-15 September** — passes the Orkney Islands.
- **20 September 1620** — sights and makes the coast of Norway.
- **21 September** — comes to harbour south of Alden (Sondfjord) in a gale, nearly wrecked
  for want of an anchor.
- **25 September** — arrives at Bergen.
- **27 September** — writes home to Denmark to report his arrival.

## Death toll and survivors

- **Complement:** 64 (48 on Enhiorningen, 16 on Lamprenen), per Munk's own title page and
  journal entry for 9 May 1619.
- **Deaths:** the manuscript (per Gosch's commentary, p. 123) records a running marginal
  tally — "21 corpses" by 10 February, and **"61 dead"** entered under 4 June 1620, the date
  of Munk's farewell entry. The sailmaker "Martin" dies shortly after and is entered
  separately just below that tally in the manuscript (omitted from the printed text).
- **Survivors: three** — Jens Munk and two other men. Gosch's introduction states this
  plainly: "the whole of the two crews succumbed to the scurvy, excepting Munk himself and
  two others" (p. xxxiv), and his overall summary gives "three lives only out of
  sixty-four" (p. xxxvi). **64 − 61 = 3**, so Munk's own count and Gosch's tally agree
  exactly; there is no discrepancy to reconcile.
- The two surviving companions are **never named** in the printed narrative. Gosch's
  commentary notes this explicitly: the crew "all perished excepting the two who returned
  with Munk, but whose names are not mentioned" (Introduction, p. cxvii).

## Medical detail

Munk's own summary of the symptoms (dated in the narrative to entries around 28 May 1620,
page 47): the illness was "a rare and extraordinary one," with limbs and joints "miserably
drawn together," severe pains in the loins "as if a thousand knives were thrust through
them," the body "blue and brown, as when one gets a black eye," and "the whole body...quite
powerless"; the mouth was "in a very bad and miserable condition, as all the teeth were
loose, so that we could not eat any victuals." Gosch's footnote to this passage states
flatly: "The disease was undoubtedly scurvy" (p. 47, n.).

The two surgeons died in the course of the winter: **David Velske**, surgeon on Lamprenen,
died 12 December 1619; **Casper Caspersen** (styled "M." for Mester), surgeon on
Enhiorningen, took to his bed 10 January 1620 and died 21 March 1620. No formal autopsy of
either surgeon is described in the narrative read; what Munk does record is that, after
Caspersen's decline, he twice asked the dying surgeon for any remedy in his chest, and was
told there was none left to give "if God would not help." After both surgeons were dead,
Munk went through the surgeon's chest himself and complained that the medicines were
labelled only in Latin, with no instructions for use, so that even the surgeon they had
"did not know...for what purpose, and in what way, they were to be employed" — the priest
had had to read the Latin labels aloud for him.

As for treatment: once ashore in June, the three survivors "crawled about everywhere near,
wherever we saw the least green growing out of the ground, which we dug up and sucked the
main root thereof" and found this "benefited us" as they began to recover (p. 49). Earlier
in the winter (25-26 March), while men were still dying, Munk himself went ashore and
gathered "Tydebaer" (red whortleberries/cowberries, i.e. lingonberries) where the snow had
melted, distributing them to the men; he does not use the term "scurvy grass" itself in the
passages read, but the pattern — raw berries and green roots sought out as soon as the
ground was bare — is presented by Munk as the turning point in the survivors' recovery,
and Gosch's introduction concurs that better provision (smoked meat, warmer clothing, more
timely fresh food) "might have averted the terrible mortality" (p. xxxvi).

   > "On the 8th of July, we sighted the land on the American side, but could not reach the

3. **7 September 1619**, arrival at the winter harbour, 58.77N -94.20W:
   > "When I now had come into the harbour aforesaid, though with great difficulty, on
   > account of wind and storm, snow, hail, and fog, I at once ordered my shallop..." (p. 24)

4. **12 December 1619**, Churchill winter harbour, 58.77N -94.20W:
   > "one of my two surgeons, the one on Lamprenen, David Velske by name, died, and his

   > severe" (p. 35)

5. **19 May 1620**, Churchill winter harbour, 58.77N -94.20W — the sick and dead lying
   unburied:
   > "died Erich Hansen Li, who, throughout the voyage, had been very industrious and
   > willing, and had neither offended anyone nor deserved any punishment. He had dug many
   > graves for others, but now there was nobody that could dig his, and his body had to
   > remain unburied." (p. 46)

   > "all the limbs and joints were so miserably drawn together, with great pains in the

   > blue and brown, as when one gets a black eye, and the whole body was quite powerless."
   > (p. 47)

   entry, believing he was dying:
   > "INASMUCH as I have now no more hope of life in this world, I request, for the sake of
   > God, if any Christian men should happen to come here, that they will bury in the earth
   > my poor body, together with the others which are found here, expecting their reward
   > from God in Heaven: and, furthermore, that this my journal maybe forwarded to my most
   > gracious Lord and King" (p. 48)

8. **early-mid June 1620**, ashore at the winter harbour, 58.77N -94.20W — the survivors

   > "we crawled about everywhere near, wherever we saw the least green growing out of the
   > ground, which we dug up and sucked the main root thereof" (p. 49)

9. **16 July 1620**, departure from the winter harbour, 58.77N -94.20W — naming:

## People

- **Jens Munk** — the Danish sea captain in command, author of the journal; one of the
  three survivors.
- **Two unnamed survivors** — "the two who returned with Munk, but whose names are not
  mentioned" (Gosch, Introduction, p. cxvii). One of the two men who spent the last weeks
  ashore may be inferable from context but is not stated; do not invent names for them.
- **Rasmus Jensen**, the priest — takes to his bed 10 January 1620, preaches his last
  sermon 23 January, dies 20 February 1620.
- **David Velske** (surgeon, Lamprenen) — died 12 December 1619.
- **M. Casper Caspersen** (surgeon, Enhiorningen) — took to his bed 10 January 1620, died
  21 March 1620; Gosch's commentary calls him "a particularly ignorant specimen of his
  class" among the period's barber-surgeons.
- **Maurids (Mauritz) Stygge**, "the well-born gentleman," Munk's lieutenant — died 10
  April 1620.
- **Four mates (Styrmaend):** William Gordon (English, chief mate, presumably on
  Enhiorningen) — died 8 April 1620; John Watson (English, presumably chief mate of
  Lamprenen) — died 6 May 1620; Hans Brock (Danish/Norwegian, second mate) — died 23
  January 1620; Jan Pettersen/Johan Petterson (Danish/Norwegian, second mate) — died 31
  March 1620.
- **Jan Ollufsen**, skipper of Enhiorningen — died 25 March 1620.
- **Jens Hendrichsen**, skipper (master) of Lamprenen — died 16 May 1620, body left
  unburied.
- **Erich Munck**, Jens Munk's nephew — died 1 April 1620.
- Gosch's introduction notes that an almost complete roll of the crew's Christian names
  (with patronymics, trades, and home parishes) can be reconstructed from the death
  entries alone, since "Munk mentions the names of nearly all in noticing their deaths,"
  but that this exercise is "of no practical use" for identifying which men served on
  which ship, since none had been in the region before except (perhaps) the two
  Englishmen (Introduction, pp. cxvi-cxvii).

**Native peoples:** Munk records **no meeting with any people at the Churchill River
itself.** He sent 19 men three miles inland on 7 November 1619 "in order to ascertain
whether any inhabitants were to be found" and found none, though he records what he
interprets as native summer dwelling-sites, stone hearths ("idolatrous" altars, in his
reading — actually graves, per Gosch's footnote), and cut chips of wood near the harbour.
The only recorded encounter with indigenous people on the whole voyage is earlier, in
Hudson Strait, at a harbour Munk names "Rin Sund" (17-22 July 1619): a peaceful meeting
and exchange of gifts (knives and iron goods for birds and seal-flesh) with people who are
almost certainly Inuit, given the location and description of their arms, kayaks, and dress.

## The site today

Munk's winter harbour is placed by Gosch (Commentary, pp. 113-114) in what is "now called
Churchill Harbour proper" — the deep pool of the Churchill River just inside Cap Merry,
where the Hudson's Bay Company's wharf and warehouses stood in 1897. The ships were first
moored there, then, because of drift-ice danger, moved about 900 fathoms across a flat to
the western shore, roughly four miles from the river mouth, which Gosch identifies as the
actual wintering spot: one of Munk's cannon was later found there, giving rise to the local
name "Munk's Cove." The Hudson's Bay Company built its first fort on the river in 1688
(rebuilt 1718 or 1721), on the western shore near the same spot, afterwards called the "Old
Fort" once Prince of Wales's Fort was built at the harbour entrance in 1733. The modern
town is Churchill, Manitoba, at the Churchill River mouth on the west coast of Hudson Bay,
about 58.77N -94.20W — the coordinate used throughout this extraction for the winter
harbour and all events there.

## Notes on sourcing

- The file `db/history/davis-baffin-hudson/files/davis-baffin-hudson-gosch1897-munk.txt`
  is mislabelled/misfiled: as delivered it is Book I (the Greenland expeditions), not Book
  II (Munk). This should be corrected or the correct scan attached under that filename.
- All page numbers above are the printed page numbers of the 1897 Hakluyt edition as OCR'd
  in `danisharcticexp01munkgoog.txt` (headers such as "24 NAVIGATIO SEPTENTRIONALIS.");
  they are internally consistent (confirmed against the running "WINTERING IN PORT
  CHURCHILL. NN" headers) but should be spot-checked against the PDF before use in
  `--pages` citations.
- Two textual/OCR points worth flagging: the ship's name is OCR'd inconsistently as both
  "Enhiorningen" and "Enhiomingen" (the latter is simply a scanno, r misread as m); and
  the surgeon on Lamprenen appears as both "David Velske" (journal text) and "David
  Volske" (Gosch's commentary) — treat these as the same man.
