# Sir Thomas Button's Voyage, 1612-13

Source: Miller Christy, ed., *The Voyages of Captain Luke Foxe of Hull and Captain Thomas James of Bristol, in Search of a North-West Passage, in 1631-32* (Hakluyt Society, 1894), Vol. I. All page numbers below are the printed page numbers visible on the OCR running heads of this volume (either Christy's Introduction, Roman numerals liv-ccxxii, or the reprint of Foxe's own *North-West Fox* of 1635, pp. 162-200, which is the section of Christy's edition titled "Button's Voyage, 1612-13"). ## IMPORTANT SOURCE NOTE: Button's own journal is LOST

This is the single most important fact governing this whole account. Christy states it flatly and repeatedly:

- Purchas tried for years to get Button's journal and never received it: "I cannot [he sadly says] communicate that which I could not receive" (Introduction, p. xxxiv, quoting Purchas's *Pilgrimes*).
- Foxe himself, writing in 1635, says: "Concerning this voyage, there cannot be much expected from me, seeing that I have met with none of the Journalls thereof... I can but communicate what I have received from Abacuck Pricket, who was in the same voyage, and [from] others by Relation, and from Sir Thomas Roe" (p. xxxiii, quoting Foxe, original edition p. 162).
- The narrative for the outward voyage and the wintering that survives in Foxe's book (and hence in this volume, pp. 162-176) is a compilation of secondhand reports from three of Button's companions: **Abacuck Pricket** (survivor of Hudson's 1610-11 mutiny voyage, who also sailed with Button), **Captain William Hawkridge**, and **Josias Hubart** (apparently mate or pilot of the Resolution).
- The narrative for the second half of the voyage (from 15 July 1613 onward) is not even secondhand report but a manuscript abstract that Sir Thomas Roe made from Button's own journal and passed to Foxe: "What I received from Sir Thomas Roc was an abstract copy taken out of Sir Thomas Button's owne Iournall, be[ginn]ing at his return to this Hopes Checkt, and not before" (p. 177). Christy's footnote states explicitly: "What follows was supplied in manuscript by Sir Thomas Roe, who had abstracted it (as Foxe says) from Button's own journal, **which is now lost**" (p. 176-177, note).
- Foxe reports that on the captains' return "their journals were taken from them" by the backers, "and therefore who doth desire any further satisfaction from this voyage must seeke it from Sir Thomas Button" (p. 200, quoting Foxe original ed. p. 200). Christy shows (Introduction, pp. xxxiv-xxxv) that Button's journals were still in his own possession as late as a letter of 16 February 1629-30, and speculates they "may even yet be discovered in the possession of his descendants or in some public institution" — i.e., as of 1894 they had never resurfaced, and they have never been found since.

**Conclusion for the database: nothing in this file is Button's own voice except the two inscriptions on the board he left at Port Nelson (found by Foxe in 1631) and a handful of numbers/dates abstracted by Roe. Everything else is Pricket's, Hawkridge's, or Hubart's report of what Button did, filtered through Foxe (1635) and then through Christy's 1894 reconstruction.**

## Backing and object of the voyage

- The voyage was got up by the Adventurers who had sent out Hudson, "with princely assistance," now "in more royall fashion, with greater shipping" (Purchas, quoted p. xviii).
- Patron: **Henry, Prince of Wales**, then aged seventeen; Button is described as "seruant to Prince Henry" (p. xviii). The Prince's Letter of Instructions to Button survives and is printed as Appendix B; in it the Prince writes: "We assure ourself by God's Grace you will not returne, without either the good newes of a passage, or sufficient assurance of an impossibility" (p. xix). Prince Henry died 6 November 1612, before Button returned, aged 18.
- Object: twofold — "the humane desire to learn... what had become of Hudson and his fellow-sufferers," and to follow up Hudson's discoveries toward the passage (p. xviii). Curiously, Hudson is not named in Button's actual written Instructions (Appendix B), though contemporary Dutch accounts (Hessel Gerritsz) state recovering Hudson was an object.
- On 26 July 1612 — about three months after Button sailed — the King granted a Royal Charter incorporating 288 persons as "The Company of the Merchants of London Discoverers of the North-West Passage," with Prince Henry as "Supreme Protector." Foxe states "in the Voyage of Sir Thomas Button were about 160 adventurers" (Introduction, p. xx, quoting Foxe, original ed. p. 268).

## Ships, command, company

- Two ships, victualled for eighteen months: the **Resolution** (Button's flagship, as "Admiral" or "General") and the **Discovery** — Hudson's old vessel — commanded by **Captain John Ingram**.
- Aboard: **Robert Bylot** (or Bileth), who had sailed with Hudson and survived the 1611 mutiny; **Abacuck Pricket**, also a Hudson survivor; **Captain Gibbons**, a relative of Button's, and **Captain (William) Hawkridge**, a friend — both of whom later commanded their own failed North-West Passage voyages (Gibbons 1614, Hawkridge c.1617-19); and **Josias Hubart**, apparently the Resolution's mate/pilot, described by Purchas as, like Foxe, a Hull man, who had sailed with Hall's 1606 and 1607 Greenland voyages and afterwards with Bylot and Baffin in 1615-16.

## Outward voyage and the crossing of Hudson Bay

- Button sailed from London **about the middle of April 1612** (p. xxx — no exact day is recorded).
- He passed through Hudson's Strait and anchored at **Digges Island** as instructed, where he assembled a pinnace and lost **five men, killed by "the salvages"** (natives) — apparently in reprisal for canoes taken from them (pp. xxx, 168-169).
- He then sailed west across the bay — "which was for long more appropriately known as Button's Bay" — naming a cape on its north side **Cary's Swan's Nest**, and fell in with the western shore in about **lat. 60° 40' N**, a place he named **Hopes Checked** (or "the Checks"), because there his hope of sailing straight through into the Western Ocean was disappointed (p. xxx). This made him the first European to cross Hudson Bay east to west and discover its western shore, which he named **New Wales**.

## Landfall at Port Nelson and the wintering, 1612-13

- From Hopes Checked, Button coasted southward and **took harbour on 15 August 1612** (Pricket says the 13th) in **Port Nelson**, naming the river after his sailing master, Nelson, "whom he buried there" (pp. xxx, 166-169). The site: "a small Rile [rill/streamlet] or Creeke on the North side of a River in Lat. 57 [degrees]," identified by Christy with a creek a few leagues north of modern Port Nelson/York Factory on the Nelson River estuary — close to the task's cited 57.06N, -92.5W.
- He hauled up his ships, "putting his small Ship in the foremost and Baracadoe them both (with Piles of Firre and earth) from storme of Snow, Ice, Raine, Floods" (p. 167).
- **Wintering, 1612-13**: "He wintered in his Ship and kept 3 fires all the Winter, but lost many men" (p. 167) — no numeric death toll is given anywhere in the surviving accounts, only "lost many men" (repeated independently by both Pricket, p. 167, and Hawkridge, p. 169). Purchas separately reports Button had "much misery of sickness in his wintering" and that he "was forced to quit the Great Ship" (the Resolution) — a detail Christy notes does not appear in Foxe's printed narrative but is corroborated by the inscribed board Foxe found in 1631 (see below). Christy's footnote dates the mortality to after the New Year, since Edward Glanville's answers to Button's "Demands," apparently written 22 December 1612, still speak of the company "preserved from all dangers whatsoever."
- Details of the winter: 3 deer killed swimming the river; bears and wolves visited the camp; the river did not freeze over until **16 February**, after several thaws; Button, "having beene sick the whole Winter," began to mend on **24 January**; ice did not clear from the river until **21 April**, after which the company caught "abundance of Fish as bigge as Mackrils" daily by net; the company reportedly killed **1,800 dozen white partridges (ptarmigan)** and other fowl over the winter (p. 167).

## Hubart's demands and the naming of Hubbart's Hope

- During the winter Button drew up written "Demands" for his officers on what to do the next season. The replies of **Josias Hubart** survive (pp. 171-176): he advised searching the tide to the northward of "the Checks," reasoning the flood they had experienced from the eastward must come around a headland to the north — advice Button took ("Well guest, Hubart!" — Foxe's marginal note, p. 171).
- It was in following this advice the next season that the party — per Purchas's *Pilgrimes* (cited in Christy's footnote, p. 179) — found "a strong race of a tyde running sometimes East, sometimes Westward, whereupon Josias Hubbart, in his Plot [chart — now lost], called y^ place **Hubbart's Hope**" — identified by Christy as Port Churchill, about 59°N.

## North to Roe's Welcome and Button's furthest, summer 1613

- The surviving narrative resumes, via Sir Thomas Roe's abstract of Button's own (now-lost) journal, on **15 July 1613**, when Button was back off Hopes Checked (p. 177).
- **23 July 1613**: reached and named **Hopes Advance** (identity uncertain — possibly Cape Esquimaux, Baker Foreland, or Cape Fullerton; not to be confused with the modern Cape Hopes Advance on the south shore of Hudson Strait).
- Continuing north into the strait between Southampton Island and the mainland — later named by Foxe (1631) **Sir Thomas Roe's Welcome** — Button reached his **furthest point north, in about lat. 65° N, on 30 July 1613** (given elsewhere in the text as "as far Northerly fully as 65 d."), a point long marked on old charts as "Ne Ultra" (no further). Christy's footnote: "Here, on the 30th of July 1613, Button reached his most northerly point in about 65° N., commonly spoken of as his Ne Ultra" (p. 182 note).
- Believing himself enclosed in a bay rather than a strait, and after violent storms (he "sunk his long Boate" in one), Button turned south-east and followed the west coast of **Southampton Island** downward, effectively giving up on this branch of the search.
- He then spent time at the western end of Hudson's Strait investigating the tide Hudson's mutineer-survivors had reported near Digges Island — by his own account he should have done this at the outset but was following his sailing instructions to search elsewhere first. By the time he finished it was "the end of August," too late in the season to continue.
- He revisited **Digges Island** and set course for home. His outward route through the strait — Foxe notes — was "not through the maine Channell of Fretum Hudson, nor thorow Lumleys Inlet," but through the passage since called **Button's Isles**, near Cape Chidley (p. 200).

## Return to England, 1613

- The exact date of Button's return to England is not recorded in any surviving source. Christy places it about the third week of September 1613, from the manuscript Autobiography of the shipwright **Phineas Pett**: "The 27 day of this month [September 1613]... my noble worthy friend Sir Thomas Button (then Capt Button) alighted at my house, newly being returned from the dangerous Voyage of the North-west Passage, where he had wintered" (p. 200, note).
- Pricket reportedly said "they were at home in 16 dayes" from Hudson's Strait (p. 200) — presumably 16 days' sailing time from the strait to England, not a calendar return date.
- On the captains' return, "their journals were taken from them" and never published in full (p. 200); this is the origin of the entire "journal is lost" problem discussed above.

## Legacy left at Port Nelson

When Foxe wintered at (or rather, stopped over at, without wintering) Port Nelson in August 1631, he found extensive relics of Button's 1612-13 stay, including an inscribed cross and half of an inscribed board (the other half missing) recording, in part:

> "In the right and to [the honour of God] and our dread souera[igne...] this coast of New W[ales was discovered...] Button, Gentleman of [Cardiff, when sailing to] the northwest..." and, on the reverse: "[To this pl]one, I came the 27th of August, 1612, [by foul] weather, where I wintered the [before] written time, and then, by reason o[f wants] and sickness amongst my Company, [I was] forst to leave my owen Ship... Per me Thomas Button, Gentleman." (Introduction, pp. c-ci)

This board — Button's own words, verbatim, though only half-legible on discovery — is the one piece of text in the entire dossier that can fairly be called Button's own voice; it independently confirms Purchas's report that Button had to abandon the Resolution.

## Sources and reliability, in brief

| What survives | Source | Reliability |
|---|---|---|
| Outward voyage, wintering (to July 1613) | Reports of Pricket, Hawkridge, Hubart, gathered by Foxe c.1635 | Secondhand, partial, sometimes contradictory in Foxe's own printed text vs. his MS |
| 15 July - end Aug 1613 (north to 65N and home) | Sir Thomas Roe's manuscript abstract of Button's own journal | Closer to primary, but an abstract, not the original, and undated as to when Roe made it |
| Button's own words | Two inscriptions on a board found by Foxe in 1631 | Genuinely primary, but fragmentary (half the board was missing) |
| Button's full journal | — | **Lost**; last documented in Button's own hands, letter of 16 Feb 1629-30; never recovered |

## Notes on what is unresolved

- No numeric death toll for the 1612-13 wintering survives; only "lost many men" (twice independently stated).
- The exact date Button sailed from London (only "about the middle of April 1612") and the exact date of his return to England (inferred as c. 27 September 1613 from a third party's diary entry) are both approximate.
- The identification of several place names (Hopes Advance, the exact wintering creek) rests on Christy's own late-19th-century conjecture matched against later charts (notably a 1685 John Thornton chart), not on Button's original testimony.
