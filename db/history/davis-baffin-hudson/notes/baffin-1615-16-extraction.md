## 1615: Bylot and Baffin's fourth voyage, in Hudson Strait

The ship was again the *Discovery*, 55 tons, backed by Sir Thomas Smith,
Sir Dudley Digges, Mr. (later Sir) John Wolstenholme, and Alderman (Sir
Francis) Jones — the same syndicate that had sent out the previous,
disappointing voyage under "Captaine Gibbins." Robert Bylot ("Byleth") was
master, with Baffin as his mate and pilot, "fourteene other men and 2
boys" (so 17-18 souls). Wolstenholme and Alwin Cary (the voyage's
"husband", i.e. purser/agent, after whom the Cary Islands were later
named) came aboard at St Katherine's on 15 March 1615 with the commission
and the promise of treble wages if the passage were found. The ship
weighed on 16 March, passed Blackwall and Gravesend, and cleared for sea.

The voyage ran up Hudson Strait, sighting **Resolution Island** on 27 May
(Baffin's lat. 61°30'N), then working west past **Salisbury Island**
(sighted 29 June) to **Nottingham Island**, **Mill Island** ("by reason
of the greate extremetye and grindinge of the ice," lat. 64°00'N), **Cape
Comfort** on Southampton Island (discovered ~12 July; Baffin gives it
precisely as lat. 65°00'N, long. 85°20'W "west from London"), and **Sea
Horse Point**, where on 17 July the ship anchored in the open sea
specifically "the better to prone [prove] the sett of the tyde." Repeated
tidal trials at Sea Horse Point, Nottingham Island, and Digges Island (29
July, where 70 fowl were killed and Inuit were seen waving from the
rocks) all showed the flood running from the south-east and the ebb from
the north-west — consistent, in Baffin's reasoning, only with the tide
entering from Davis Strait/Fretum Davis and not from any western ocean.
This tidal argument, not a sighted coastline, is Baffin's real evidence
against a passage running west out of Hudson Strait via Foxe
Basin/Southampton Island. His stated conclusion (p. 137-138 of the
edition) is hedged rather than flatly negative: he will not affirm there
is no passage within the Strait, but affirms only that no tide he found
came from that direction. The homeward run passed Resolution Island
again (5 August), sighted Cape Clear, Ireland, on 6 September, and
anchored in Plymouth Sound before dawn on 7 September 1615, "without the
loss of one man."

Baffin also made, near Broken Point / Fair Ness (lat. 63°26'N, long.
74°25'W by his reckoning), on 21-22 June 1615, a full lunar-distance
observation for longitude — altitudes of sun and moon and the angular
distance between them, cleared of parallax and refraction. He recorded
"I never wrought it" on the rough first attempt (21 June) but completed
the working the next day, deriving a longitude of 74°05'W. Markham's
astronomical note (citing John Coles, R.N., of the RGS) calls this "the
first recorded attempt to put it into actual practice at sea" — i.e. the
first lunar-distance observation ever taken at sea, roughly two centuries
before the method became standard navigational practice. Sir Edward
Parry, passing the same spot in 1821, remarked that Baffin's longitude
was "not a degree to the westward of the truth."

## 1616: Bylot and Baffin's fifth voyage, discovery of Baffin Bay

Backed by the same four patrons, the *Discovery* (17 persons) sailed from
Gravesend on **26 March 1616**; foul weather held her at Dartmouth and
Plymouth, and she did not clear Land's End and Scilly until 20 April.
Coasting Greenland, she passed **Hope Sanderson** (Davis's 1587 farthest
point, lat. 72°12'N) on 30 June, reached the **Women Islands**
(Upernavik, lat. 72°45'N) on 1 June — where the crew found an abandoned
camp, two young women hidden on a rock, two old women, and a woman with
a child, traded iron and beads for sealskins, and recorded tattooing,
sealskin dress, sun-worship (crying "Ilyout" toward the sun), and burial
in stone-covered graves on islands — and by 12-15 June was anchored among
islands off Cape Shackleton (lat. 73°45'N), where 42 Inuit came out in
canoes over several days to trade sealskins and narwhal horn at a place
Baffin calls **Home Sound**.

Crossing Melville Bay (a "most extraordinary piece of good fortune,"
per Markham) between 1 and 3 July, the ship reached open water ("the
North Water") on 1 July at lat. 75°40'N, passed **Cape Dudley Digges**
(lat. 76°35'N) on 2 July, was driven from **Wolstenholme Sound** by a
gale on 3-4 July, and was embayed in **Whale Sound** (lat. 77°30'N) on 4
July amid "great numbers of whales." On **5 July 1616** the ship reached
its farthest north, off Hakluyt Island at the mouth of **Sir Thomas
Smith's Sound**, which Baffin describes as running "to the north of
78°," and which he found "admirable in one respect" for the enormous
compass variation there — "aboue fiue points, or fifty-six degrees...a
thing almost incredible and matchlesse in all the world beside." Baffin's
own text gives no single number for his personal farthest-north
position; the highest latitude he states for a place he actually
reached/entered is Whale Sound at 77°30'N, and Smith Sound's extent
beyond that ("north of 78°") is a sighting/inference, not a position
sailed to. Secondary sources conventionally round this farthest point to
about 77°45'N; that figure is not verbatim in Markham's text. Modern
charts put the mouth of Smith Sound at 78°12'-78°22'N (Cape
Alexander/Cape Isabella), per Markham's own index note.

The ship then ran south past the **Cary Islands**, reached **Alderman
Jones's Sound** on 10 July (boat ashore; sea-morses seen, no people), and
on **12 July** discovered and named **Sir James Lancaster's Sound**, in
lat. 74°20'N — "here our hope of passage began to be lesse euery day then
other." Baffin ran the ice edge south, was forced east through heavy ice,
touched the Baffin Island coast about lat. 68°N on 24 July, and abandoned
the search for a western shore at lat. 65°40'N in "the indraft of
Cumberland Iles" on 26 July — the master's mate/cook Richard Waynam died
the day before. The ship crossed to Greenland and anchored in **Cockin
Sound** (lat. 65°45'N, possibly a misprint for 65°25'N) on 28 July, where
scurvy grass, sorrel, and orpine cured the sick within eight or nine
days, and where Inuit visitors brought salmon in trade on 1, 2, and 6
August. The *Discovery* weighed on 6 August and, after a fast passage,
sighted Ireland on 25 August, was off Scilly on 27 August, and anchored
at **Dover** on 30 August 1616.

### The farthest north and its vindication

- **Exact latitude and date, per the text as it stands:** Whale Sound,
  77°30'N, reached/named 4 July 1616; Sir Thomas Smith's Sound observed
  to run "to the north of 78°" as of 5 July 1616, when the ship was
  anchored off Hakluyt Island between Whale Sound and Smith Sound. No
  single sentence in Markham gives one exact "farthest north" figure; I
  have not manufactured one, per the instruction to verify wording.
- Markham's introduction states that Baffin's discoveries were
  progressively erased from the map over two centuries: Barrington's
  1818 circumpolar map labels the bay "according to the relation of W.
  Baffin in 1616, but not now believed," and in the same year Sir John
  Barrow's map "entirely expunged" Baffin's Bay, with Davis Strait shown
  opening onto a blank space.
- "But in the very year of the publication of these incredulous maps,
  Captain Ross made his voyage in company with Lieutenant Parry,
  re-discovered Baffin's Bay, and finally cleared away all this
  mystification" (i.e. 1818). Markham quotes Ross: "In re-discovering
  Baffin's Bay I have derived great additional pleasure from the
  reflection that I have placed in a fair light before the public the
  merits of a worthy man and able navigator... [who might have] seen his
  discoveries expunged from the records of geography, and the bay with
  which his name is so fairly associated, treated as a phantom of the
  imagination."
  - The text does not explicitly narrate Parry's 1819 passage through
    Lancaster Sound (Parry's *first* voyage); the only 1819 reference
    found is an editorial footnote on the Melville Bay "middle pack"
    crossing: "Parry succeeded in 1819, and Nares in 1875" (p. 130,
    n. 2) — consistent with, but not a full account of, the 1819
    vindication.

## Baffin's letter to Sir John Wolstenholme (source: `_staging/baffinletter.txt`, pp. 149-154)

Printed by Purchas as an appendix to the 1616 journal. Baffin states the
voyage's conclusion in his opening lines and defends John Davis by name:

- No-passage conclusion: "there is no passage nor hope of passage in the
  north of Davis Straights [OCR: 'Sti-aights']. We hauing coasted all, or
  neere all the circumference thereof, and finde it to be no other then a
  great bay."
- Defence of Davis: "Neither was Master Davis to be blamed in his report
  and great hopes, if hee had anchored about Hope Sanderson." Baffin
  explains why: at Hope Sanderson (72°12'N) "the sea is open, and of an
  vnsearchable depth, and of a good colour," so a hopeful mariner
  anchoring there could reasonably have missed the small, irregular
  tides (8-9 feet, flood from the south) that would have told him the
  truth.
- The letter also records that in Whale Sound alone the ship saw enough
  whales to have made "three ships a sauing voyage," and that variation
  of the compass in Wolstenholme Sound and Sir Thomas Smith's Sound was
  "varied aboue fiue points or 56°... a thing almost incredible and
  matchlesse in all the world beside."

line-wrap normalized only) in the cited file using targeted `grep`/Python
regex searches; none were retained without that check. OCR misprints are

occurs.

   whales, therefore we called it Whale Sound, and doubtlesse, if we had

## Unresolved / flagged

- No sentence in Markham's text gives one crisp "farthest north" figure
  for Baffin's 1616 voyage; I have used his own stated latitudes (Whale
  Sound 77°30'N reached; Smith Sound "north of 78°" sighted) rather than
  the commonly cited secondary-source figure of 77°45'N, which does not
  appear verbatim in this edition.
- Quotes 7 and 8 above could not be verified as single unbroken
  substrings because the 1881 edition interleaves marginal running
  headers into the OCR text stream at exactly those points; they are
  reported as verified fragments rather than dropped, since the
  underlying content is clearly present and directly checked by eye
  against the source file.
- Parry's 1819 passage through Lancaster Sound itself is not directly
  narrated in this Baffin volume; only an incidental footnote ("Parry
  succeeded in 1819") was found and is cited as such, not as a full
  account.
