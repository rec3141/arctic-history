Between 1585 and 1631 a handful of small English and Danish ships worked their
way into the waters this ship now sails, looking for a sea road to Asia. In
forty-six years they mapped, in outline, almost everything: Davis Strait,
Cumberland Sound, Hudson Strait, Hudson Bay, James Bay, Foxe Basin, and the
whole eastern rim of Baffin Bay up to Smith Sound. Then the effort stopped for
nearly two hundred years, and much of what they had found was quietly rubbed
off the charts as too good to be true.

## The four questions they were answering

The premise was old and wrong in its details but not in its outline: that North
America ended somewhere, and that a ship could get round it. [[person:John
Davis]] put it most clearly after his second voyage, writing to his backer
[[person:William Sanderson]] from Exeter in October 1586 —
[[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-028|the passage must be in one of four places,
or else not at all]]. Every voyage on this page is an attempt on one of those
four places.

## Davis, 1585-1587

Davis made three voyages in three summers, in ships of fifty tons and less. In
1585 he crossed to Greenland, coasted the "Land of Desolation", anchored in
Gilbert Sound at [[place:Nuup Kangerlua]] — where his musicians played and his
crew danced for the Kalaallit who came out to them
([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-025]]) — and then struck west to the Baffin
coast, named [[place:Exeter Sound]], and sailed sixty leagues up
[[place:Cumberland Sound]] in water of
[[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-026|the very colour, nature, and qualitie of the
mayne ocean]]. In 1586 he tried again, and the voyage turned ugly: a Kalaallit
man was taken hostage at Gilbert Sound and carried to sea, where the printed
margin records only that he "after dyed"
([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-027]]); the Sunneshine's crew killed three men
in a fight at the same anchorage; two of Davis's own sailors were killed by
arrows on the Labrador coast; and the pinnace Northstarre went down with all
hands. In 1587 he reached 72 degrees 12 minutes on the Greenland side and named
the headland [[place:Sanderson's Hope]], the farthest north anyone had made in
these waters, with the sea open ahead of him. Coming south he ran into the tide
races at the mouth of Hudson Strait —
[[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-029|the water whirling and roring, as it were
the meeting of tides]] — which later mapmakers labelled
[[place:The Furious Overfall]]. His three tracks are
[[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-001]], [[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-002]] and
[[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-003]]; the voyages themselves are set out in
[[page:davis-three-voyages]].

No Kalaaleq or Inuk is named as an individual anywhere in the Davis record.
Forty of their words are, in a vocabulary Davis's people collected in 1586, but
not one person's name. That silence is a property of the source, not of the
history, and it is worth saying so plainly.

## Hudson, 1610-1611

[[person:Henry Hudson]] took the Discovery through the overfall Davis had found
and into the bay that now carries his name. He wintered in James Bay, and in
June 1611, as the ice let go, his crew mutinied and set him, his son and seven
others adrift in the ship's boat. They were never seen again. The survivors
fought the Inuit at Digges Island on the way home, lost more men, starved, and
were acquitted at trial in London. Nearly everything known about it comes from
[[person:Abacuk Pricket]], who was one of the mutineers' party and had every
reason to shape the story. That page is [[page:hudsons-fate]].

The chart that came out of the voyage, [[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-012]],
was engraved in Amsterdam in 1612 from the Discovery's papers. It is why the
bay is Hudson's and not his crew's.

## Button, Bylot, Baffin, Munk, Foxe and James

What followed was a rapid, systematic elimination of the four places.
[[person:Thomas Button]] crossed the bay in 1612-13 and wintered on its west
shore at Port Nelson. [[person:Robert Bylot]] and [[person:William Baffin]] in
the same little Discovery proved in 1615, from the behaviour of the tides, that
no passage led west out of Hudson Strait; then in 1616 they ran up the Greenland
coast past Davis's farthest and round the head of Baffin Bay, naming Smith
Sound, Jones Sound and Lancaster Sound — a farthest north that stood for about
two centuries, and a survey so far ahead of its evidence that the Admiralty
later struck Baffin Bay off its charts as doubtful. [[page:baffins-farthest-north]]
follows that.

[[person:Jens Munk]] sailed for Christian IV of Denmark in 1619 with two ships
and sixty-odd men, wintered at the mouth of the Churchill River, and came home
in 1620 with two companions; the rest died of scurvy where they lay. See
[[page:munk-at-the-churchill]] and [[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-016]].

In 1631 [[person:Luke Foxe]] and [[person:Thomas James]] sailed in the same
season on the same errand and met, absurdly, in the middle of Hudson Bay. Foxe
went north into what is now [[place:Foxe Basin]] and came home with every man
alive; James sank his ship deliberately at [[place:Charlton Island]] to save her
through a terrible winter and came home a year later. After them the search
through Hudson Bay was over for a century:
[[page:foxe-and-james-1631]].

The ten tracks on the map are Davis's three ([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-001]],
[[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-002]], [[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-003]]),
Hudson's ([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-004]]), Button's
([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-005]]), Bylot and Baffin's two
([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-006]], [[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-007]]),
Munk's ([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-008]]), Foxe's
([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-009]]) and James's
([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-010]]). Where a leg is inferred rather than
observed, the track's description says so.

## Why it still matters here

The names on the ship's chart plotter are theirs: Davis Strait, Hudson Strait,
Baffin Bay, Lancaster Sound, Foxe Basin. So are the first written observations
of sea ice, tides and weather in this region, which are now read as data — a
seventeenth-century date for an ice edge is a real point in a four-century
series. And these were not empty waters. Every one of these voyages met people
who already lived here, and the meetings are recorded in the visitors' words
only: with music and dancing, with trade, with kidnapping, and with killing on
both sides. Those encounters belong to the communities of Qikiqtaaluk (Baffin
Island) and Kalaallit Nunaat as much as to English maritime history, and they
have their own page: [[page:the-people-they-met]]. See [[topic:frobisher]] for
the generation before Davis, [[topic:ross-parry]] for the ships that finally
vindicated Baffin in 1818 and 1819, and [[topic:hudson-strait-south-baffin]] and
[[topic:inuit-oral-history]] for what happened in these places afterwards.
