In two summers, in a 55-ton bark that had already been to the bottom of Hudson
Bay and back, [[person:Robert Bylot]] and [[person:William Baffin]] did the two
most useful things anyone did in this whole search: they proved where the
passage was not, and they drew the outline of Baffin Bay so accurately that
nobody believed it for two hundred years.

The ship was the Discovery — the same ship Hudson had been set adrift from
([[page:hudsons-fate]]), with the same Bylot who had brought her home in 1611
and had never been punished for it. Bylot was master both times. Baffin was
mate and pilot, and he kept the journals, which is why the bay is his and not
Bylot's. The backers were [[person:Thomas Smith]], [[person:Dudley Digges]],
[[person:John Wolstenholme]] and Alderman Jones, and they promised treble wages
if the passage were found. The record is [[source:markham1881baffin]].

## 1615: proving it by the tides

The 1615 voyage went up [[place:Hudson Strait]] and along the south-east side of
Southampton Island: Resolution Island on 27 May, Salisbury Island, Mill Island
(so named "by reason of the greate extremetye and grindinge of the ice"), Cape
Comfort, Sea Horse Point. The track is [[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-006]] and
Baffin's working chart of the strait is
[[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-014]].

What makes this voyage different is how it reached its conclusion. On 17 July
Baffin anchored the ship in open water on purpose, to time the tide:

> Heare we found, most apparently to all our companies sight, that in this
> place the tyde of floud doth come from the south east, and the ebb from the
> north west, being the certaynest sett of tyde I have yet made pruofe of.

([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-042]].) He repeated it at Nottingham Island and
at the [[place:Digges Islands]]. The reasoning is simple and it is right: if the
flood comes from the south-east, the tide is entering from the Atlantic, and
there is no great ocean pressing in from the west. Every earlier captain had
argued from the look of the water and the run of the coast — that is exactly
what misled [[person:John Davis]] in Cumberland Sound thirty years before
([[page:davis-three-voyages]]). Baffin argued from a measurement, and he was
careful about what it did and did not prove: he would not affirm there was no
passage anywhere within the strait, only that no tide he could find came out of
one.

On the way there, on 22 June, he did something else nobody had done. Near Broken
Point he took the altitudes of the sun and the moon and the angular distance
between them, cleared them for parallax and refraction, and worked out his
longitude: 74° 05′ W. Markham calls it the first recorded attempt to put the
lunar-distance method into actual practice at sea. It was two hundred years
early. Parry, passing the same spot in 1821 with a chronometer, said Baffin's
longitude was not a degree from the truth.

They came into Plymouth Sound on 7 September 1615 without the loss of one man.

## 1616: round the top of the bay

They sailed again on 26 March 1616, seventeen people aboard, and went up the
Greenland coast: past Sukkertoppen, past the Disko coast, past
[[place:Sanderson's Hope]] — Davis's farthest, at 72° 12′ — on 30 May, and on
into water no European had recorded. At the Women Islands off Upernavik the
people fled at their approach, leaving behind two young women hidden on a rock,
two old women and a woman with a child. Off Cape Shackleton in mid-June
forty-two people came out in kayaks over several days and traded sealskins and
narwhal ivory for iron and beads. Baffin writes down the dress, the women's
facial tattooing, a cry of "Ilyout" made toward the sun, the graves covered over
with stones. He writes down no names, and there are none to recover.

Then they got the one thing every ship on this coast needs and few get: an open
year in Melville Bay. Between 1 and 3 July they were through the middle pack and
into the North Water at 75° 40′. On 2 July they passed Cape Dudley Digges; on
the 3rd they were blown out of Wolstenholme Sound; on the 4th they were in
[[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-043|Whale Sound, in the latitude 77 deg 30 min,
with more whales in it than they had any means of killing]]; and on 5 July they
lay off Hakluyt Island at the mouth of [[place:Smith Sound]], which Baffin
reported running "to the north of 78°". There the compass varied above five
points — 56 degrees — which he called a thing almost incredible and matchless in
all the world beside. It is, and it is real: they were close to the magnetic
pole's own hemisphere of strangeness.

That was the farthest north, and it stood for about two centuries. It is worth
saying exactly what the text supports, because the books repeat a number that is
not in it: Baffin gives no single farthest-north figure. The highest latitude he
states for a place he actually entered is Whale Sound, 77° 30′. Smith Sound
running "north of 78°" is a sighting, not a position sailed to. The often-quoted
77° 45′ appears nowhere in Markham's edition.

Coming south they passed [[place:Jones Sound]] on 10 July and, on 12 July, "were
open of another great Sound, lying in the latitude of 74° 20′, and we called it
Sir James Lancaster's Sound"
([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-046]]) — [[place:Tallurutiup Imanga]], the actual
Northwest Passage, whose door they looked at and did not enter. In the same
breath the hope of a passage "began to be lesse euery day then other". The cook
Richard Waynam died of scurvy on 26 July; two days later they anchored in Cockin
Sound, ate scurvy grass, sorrel and orpine, and the rest of the sick were well
in nine days. They were at Dover on 30 August. The track is
[[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-007]].

## The letter, and the erasure

Baffin wrote to [[person:John Wolstenholme]] to say what the money had bought:

> there is no passage nor hope of passage in the north of Davis Straights. We
> hauing coasted all, or neere all the circumference thereof, and finde it to
> be no other then a great bay, as the voyage doth truely shew.

([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-044]].) He was right about the bay and wrong
about the passage, and he was wrong for a reason that is written on his own
track: the ice pushed him past the mouth of Lancaster Sound. In the same letter
he defended Davis by name
([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-045|Neither was Master Davis to be blamed in his
report and great hopes]]), on the technical ground that the tides at Hope
Sanderson are small and irregular and would not have told Davis anything.

Then the strangest part. Because Baffin said there was no passage, nobody had a
reason to go back, and because nobody went back, nothing he had drawn could be
confirmed. Over two centuries his names came off the maps. Barrington's map of
1818 marks the bay "according to the relation of W. Baffin in 1616, but not now
believed"; Sir John Barrow's map of the same year removed it altogether and left
Davis Strait opening into blank paper. Foxe had printed all of it in 1635 —
Smith Sound, Alderman Jones, Lancaster Sound, lettered in at the top of his card
([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-018]]) — and it was rubbed out anyway.

In that same year, 1818, John Ross sailed north with Parry, went round the bay,
and found every one of Baffin's names where Baffin had put them, his latitudes
good and his coastline right. Ross wrote that he took particular pleasure in
having placed before the public the merits of a worthy man and able navigator
whose discoveries had been treated as a phantom of the imagination. The next
year Parry sailed into Lancaster Sound and kept going — see
[[topic:ross-parry]].

Baffin himself never knew any of it. He went east with the East India Company
and was killed at the siege of Qeshm in the Persian Gulf in 1622, aged about
thirty-eight.

## Why it matters on this ship

The Amundsen works in the water Baffin drew. Tallurutiup Imanga is now a
national marine conservation area co-managed with the Inuit of Mittimatalik
(Pond Inlet), Ikpiarjuk (Arctic Bay), Qausuittuq (Resolute), Ausuittuq (Grise
Fiord) and Clyde River. The North Water polynya that let Baffin through in 1616
— the Pikialasorsuaq — is the most productive water in the Arctic and is the
subject of an Inuit-led management commission today. And the method matters as
much as the map: Baffin settled a geographical question by measuring the tide,
and reported his numbers so that somebody else could check them. That is the
same argument this ship makes every day.
