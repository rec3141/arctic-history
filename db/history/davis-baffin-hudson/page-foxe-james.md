In the spring of 1631 two English ships sailed for Hudson Bay on the same
errand, two days apart, from ports that could not stand each other.
[[person:Luke Foxe]] left Deptford on 5 May in the Charles, a king's pinnace
lent by Charles I, twenty men and two boys, backed by London and by
[[person:John Wolstenholme]] and [[person:Thomas Roe]]. [[person:Thomas James]]
had left Bristol on 3 May in the Henrietta Maria, twenty-two men, backed by the
Society of Merchant Venturers of Bristol. Both carried the King's instructions.
Both carried a letter to the Emperor of Japan.

They were the last serious attempt on the passage through Hudson Bay for a
hundred years, and between them they finished the job the previous four
expeditions had started: after 1631 nobody could think the bay led anywhere.

## Twenty years of narrowing it down

[[person:Thomas Button]] had gone first, in 1612-13, sent by the Adventurers
under the patronage of Henry, Prince of Wales, both to press on with the search
and to look for [[person:Henry Hudson]] ([[page:hudsons-fate]]). He crossed the
bay from east to west — the first European to do it — struck the western shore
at about 60° 40′ and named the place Hopes Checked, because that is what
happened to them there, and wintered at Port Nelson, where he buried his sailing
master Nelson and named the river for him. He lost five men killed at the
[[place:Digges Islands]] on the way in, and through the winter he "lost many
men": no source gives a number. He was sick himself the whole winter, and in the
spring he had to abandon the Resolution and come home in the Discovery — Hudson's
old ship, with [[person:Robert Bylot]] aboard again. In July 1613 he reached
about 65° N in the strait later called Roe's Welcome, a point marked Ne Ultra on
the old charts. His track is [[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-005]].

Almost none of that is Button's own testimony. His journals were taken from him
when he returned, were still in his hands in 1630, and have never been found.
What survives is what Foxe collected in 1635 from [[person:Abacuk Pricket]],
William Hawkridge and [[person:Josias Hubart]], plus an abstract Sir Thomas Roe
had made of the journal before it vanished. The one exception is
[[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-051|half a board he left standing at Port
Nelson]], which Foxe found nineteen years later and copied down: "To this place
I came the 27th of August, 1612... and then, by reason of wants and sickness
amongst my Company, I was forst to leave my owen Ship." That is all of Button's
voice there is.

Then came Gibbons in 1614, who achieved nothing; [[person:Robert Bylot]] and
[[person:William Baffin]] in 1615, who proved by the tides that no passage ran
west out of [[place:Hudson Strait]] ([[page:baffins-farthest-north]]); and
[[person:Jens Munk]] for Denmark in 1619, who lost sixty-one of sixty-four men at
the Churchill ([[page:munk-at-the-churchill]]). Yet the maps still showed the
bay open to the west ([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-015]]), and hope is
expensive but not as expensive as certainty.

## The meeting, 29-30 August 1631

Foxe went in through the strait in June, north-west to the island he named Sir
Thomas Roe's Welcome, then turned and worked the entire west shore of the bay
southward — Churchill, which he called Hubart's Hope, and Port Nelson, where he
overhauled the ship and found Button's board. On 27 August he wrote that all
hope of a passage on that shore was at an end. Two days later he sighted a sail.

James had come across the bay from the north, run onto rocks near Nelson Shoal,
and passed Port Nelson on 17 August while Foxe was ashore there refitting a
pinnace, neither knowing the other was within a day's sail. Now they met in open
water in the middle of Hudson Bay, and Foxe wrote the encounter up for print.

James kept his flag up as a king's ship would, and said he was carrying His
Majesty's letters to the Emperor of Japan:

> "Keepe it vp then", quoth I, "but you are out of the way to Iapon, for this
> is not it."

([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-047]].) James pressed him to take harbour and
winter in the bay, citing Button's example; Foxe answered that Button was no
precedent for him, and that he would rather parallel his poverty with poor
Hudson's, who took no harbour before the first of November. Then he went to
dinner aboard the Henrietta Maria, and reported the meal:

> wee dined betwixt decks, for the great cabin was not bigg enough to receiue
> our selues and followers; during which time the ship... threw in so much
> water as wee could not haue wanted sause if wee had had roast Mutton.

([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-048]].) He went on, in print, to wonder whether
James's people were better off impounded amongst ice, where the piercing air
would keep them from putrefaction, or in the open sea, being thus daily pickled.
They parted on 31 August.

On 2 September both ships reached the same cape at the bottom of the bay from
opposite directions and did not meet. Foxe named it Wolstenholme's Ultimum Vale,
a dig at his own backer, being quite certain no more money would be spent here.
James named it Cape Henrietta Maria, for the queen. Her name is the one on the
chart.

## Foxe: north, and home with everybody

Foxe turned north instead of wintering. He went up past Nottingham Island and
Mill Island into the channel and basin that carry his name now, past a coast
still called Foxe Land, further than [[person:William Baffin]] had gone that way
in 1615. On 7 September he wrote
[[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-050|Now the Master and three men more are downe.
God better it!]] On 22 September he reached 66° 47′ N and called it Foxe his
Farthest, and turned for home because his people were sick. On 31 October:

> I came into the Downes with all my men recovered and sound, not having lost
> one man nor boy, nor any manner of tackling, having been forth neere six
> months. All glory be to God!

([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-049]].) He was criticised for coming back the
same year and wrote a defence of it. Read against the rest of this topic — a
shallop cut adrift in James Bay, sixty-one graves at the Churchill, four on
Charlton Island — bringing everyone home was the rarest achievement in it. His
book, North-West Fox (1635), is vain, funny, unfair to almost everybody, and the
map folded into it ([[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-017]] and
[[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-018]]) is the first printed chart to carry
Baffin's names at the top of the world. He died at Whitby in 1635, the year it
appeared. The track is [[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-009]].

## James: the winter at Charlton Island

James spent September among the islands of [[place:James Bay]], grounding more
than once, and in early October found himself off [[place:Charlton Island]] with
winter on him. He stripped the ship, landed the provisions and built three
houses ashore. On 17 October John Barton, the gunner's mate, drowned. On
29 October, unable to secure the Henrietta Maria any other way, he had her sunk
in shoal water so that the ice would hold her instead of crushing her — one of
the strangest decisions in Arctic seamanship, and it worked. On 22 November the
gunner Richard Edwards died of an injury from a capstan accident in August. The
chief mate John Warden died on 6 May, the carpenter William Cole on 18 May.

They dug the ship out and raised her in April, refitted her in June, nearly
burned themselves alive on 25 June when a fire set to clear the woods got away
from them, and sailed on 2 July 1632. On the way home James went north up Foxe
Channel to about 65° 30′, not knowing Foxe had been in that water the year
before. He was at Bristol on 22 October 1632. The track is
[[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-010]] and his chart, the Platt of Sayling, is
[[artifact:davis-baffin-hudson-019]].

His book, The Strange and Dangerous Voyage of Captaine Thomas James (1633), is
the bleakest thing written in this whole search, and it sold. Christy's 1894
edition devotes ten pages to the claim — made first by J. F. Nicholls of the
Bristol City Library in 1870 and examined at length by Ivor James of Cardiff in
1890 — that Coleridge, a daily user of that library, drew on it for the ice and
the horror of The Rime of the Ancient Mariner. Christy's own verdict is careful:
an important source, perhaps the chief one, but certainly only one among several.
It is a claim about influence, not a documented fact, and it should be read that
way.

## After 1631

Foxe's news that there was no passage through the bay, Christy writes, "was not
sufficiently promising to induce anyone to send out any other expedition until
nearly a century later". The next serious attempts were the Hudson's Bay
Company's, under Christopher Middleton in 1741-42 and Moor and Smith in 1746-47,
and by then the Company had been trading out of the bay for seventy years and the
question had changed from where the passage was to who owned the fur.

What the six voyages in this topic actually produced was not a route but a
coastline: the strait, the bay, James Bay, Foxe Basin, the west shore, and the
whole eastern rim of Baffin Bay, drawn well enough that the next people to come
this way — the whalers, the Company, and eventually [[topic:ross-parry]] — knew
where they were. See [[topic:whaling]] and [[topic:posts-rcmp-missions]] for what
came after.
