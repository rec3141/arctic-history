The Northwest Passage was made on 26 August 1905. The *Gjøa* reached the Pacific
on 31 August 1906. In between lies the third winter, which cost the expedition
its only Norwegian life.

## Stopped thirty hours short

The ship worked west from [[place:Nelson Head]] and on 3 September 1905 was
stopped by ice at [[place:King Point]] on the Yukon coast — by
[[person:Roald Amundsen]]'s reckoning about thirty hours' sailing short of open
water. There was nothing to be done about it, so they settled in.

They were not alone. The San Francisco schooner *Bonanza* had been wrecked on
the point, and her second mate, the Norwegian [[person:Christian Sten]], rowed
out to meet them. House-building began on 11 September and the roof was on by
the 15th. Sten's own house, fifty yards west, was built of planks and boards off
the wreck and looked, Amundsen said, "for all the world like a villa residence":
two rooms, the inner one for Sten, his wife [[person:Kataksina]] and their small
daughter Anni, the outer one a shared kitchen where the harpooner
[[person:Jimmy]] and his wife [[person:Kappa]] had a bed by the stove and
[[person:Neiu]] and his wife were berthed on a trapdoor directly above it, in
112 degrees on baking days.

So the third winter was spent as a mixed Norwegian, Inuvialuit and American
settlement of about a dozen adults, and the pattern of the first two held.
Kataksina sewed Amundsen's boots and travelling clothes. Neiu guided
[[person:Godfred Hansen]] and [[person:Peder Ristvedt]] on a two-month hunting
expedition to lay in fresh meat. The magnetic and meteorological series went on
uninterrupted.

## The ride to Eagle

Amundsen could not wait a year to tell the world. On 21 October 1905 he set out
for the nearest telegraph — Fort Egbert, at [[place:Eagle]] on the Yukon River
in Alaska, roughly 800 km away over the Richardson Mountains and up the river —
and the [[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-002|route he took]] is on the map. He travelled
with Captain Mogg of the wrecked *Bonanza*, with Jimmy and Kappa as far as Fort
Yukon, and with the winter mail carriers, on ski, snowshoe and dog sledge, at
temperatures that eventually brought the telegraph wires down.

He came in off the river ice in early December, drove into the town, and sent
his message from the telegraph office inside the fort, held by two companies of
the US 3rd Infantry. "I despatched my precious telegram, which was only just in
time, as shortly afterwards the wires broke down, owing to the intense cold."

Then he waited two months for the answer, as the guest of Frank N. Smith of the
Northern Commercial Company, whose household he remembered with unqualified
warmth. He started north again on 3 February 1906 and was back aboard on
12 March. It is 1,600 km of winter travel to send one message — and the point of
the *Gjøa* expedition's whole method is that by 1905 Amundsen could do it as a
matter of routine.

## Wiik

[[person:Gustav Juel Wiik]] was twenty-eight, from Horten, trained at the
magnetic observatory at Potsdam. He and Amundsen had run the nineteen-month
series at [[page:two-winters-at-uqsuqtuuq|Gjøahavn]] between them, and he was
still taking observations at King Point.

He fell ill on 26 March 1906 with acute pain in the right side. Amundsen took it
for pleurisy and treated him out of Uckermann's *Medical Guide* — there was no
doctor, and the nearest was at [[place:Qikiqtaruk]] (Herschel Island). On
30 March Amundsen wrote in the log that Wiik was making good progress. That
night he had a rigor, and in the morning his pulse was irregular. Amundsen went
to Jimmy to send him for the doctor; a gale was blowing from the north-west and
Jimmy would not start into it. They settled that he would go the next morning.

> [[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-031|"At about 5 p.m. … the bell rang very loudly, all
> of a sudden. There was something in the ring that spelt disaster, and I ran
> off at full speed. But I was only just in time to see our dear friend breathe
> his last. It was an inexpressibly sad moment. I closed the eyes of our late
> comrade, and we remained sitting there for a while in silence and sorrow."]]

[[person:Anton Lund]] finished the coffin on 3 April, painted black. It was laid
on two stools in the outer room of the house under a flag, and stayed there five
weeks while the crew moved out and nailed the room up, because the ground was
frozen too hard to dig.

On 9 May 1906, at half past ten in the morning, with the flags at half mast, the
coffin was drawn on a sledge to the magnetic observatory Wiik had built with his
own hands, on open ground facing the Arctic Sea. Amundsen read the Lord's
Prayer. The room was filled with driftwood and walled up. Later that summer they
raised a high cross over it and turfed the grave, and the American whalers
promised to keep it.

On 6 May, three days before the burial, Amundsen had handed a party bound for
Fort McPherson an open telegram announcing the death, with a covering letter to
the manager there asking him to send it off at once and to advance the money.
His reason was precise and awful: he did not want Wiik's mother to hear from
some other source that all was well with the expedition before she heard the
truth from him. The mail from the fort came into Herschel Island in August with
nothing in it from Fort McPherson. The telegram never arrived.

## Getting away

The *Gjøa* left King Point in the second week of July 1906 — the narrative gives
no day; she was at Herschel Island by the 13th — waiting only on
[[person:Adolf Henrik Lindstrom]]'s last ovenful of bread. Kataksina came aboard
the wreck of the *Bonanza* with Anni on her back, and Kunak and Neiu with their
families, to see the ship off. Passing the grave, the crew lowered the flag to
half mast.

At Herschel Island the whaling skippers advised them to lie a fortnight before
going on. Amundsen ignored the advice, met ice, and had to come back and take
it. [[person:Manni]] — the young Netsilingmiut who had joined the ship at
[[place:Uqsuqtuuq]] the summer before, whom Amundsen and Lindstrom had taught to
read and write, who had once left to live among Inuit again and found it
unbearable and returned — drowned there, shooting ducks, in the summer of 1906.

## Nome

They rounded Point Barrow some time between 9 and 24 August 1906; Amundsen dates
neither. On the evening of 31 August a broken gaff, not a plan, brought the
*Gjøa* in to [[place:Nome]]. A launch came out in the dark flying the Norwegian
flag beside the Stars and Stripes, and as Amundsen and Godfred Hansen were
brought ashore about a thousand people sang *Ja, vi elsker dette landet*.

Three years and two and a half months out of [[place:Christiania]]. Two men had
died: Wiik at King Point, and Manni at Herschel Island. See
