On 12 September 1903 the *Gjøa* came into a small, almost landlocked harbour on
the south-east coast of King William Island. Simpson Strait lay open to the west
and the Northwest Passage was, that afternoon, there for the taking.
[[person:Roald Amundsen]] anchored at half past eight in the evening and stayed
for one month short of two years.

The place is [[place:Uqsuqtuuq]] — "lots of fat", for the seal oil — and the
Netsilingmiut had been coming to it long before the *Gjøa*. Amundsen named the
anchorage Gjøahavn after his ship, and the hamlet is Gjoa Haven in English to
this day.

## Why he stopped

The expedition's stated object was not the passage. It was to re-observe the
North Magnetic Pole, which [[topic:ross-victory|James Clark Ross]] had fixed at
Cape Adelaide on the west coast of [[place:Boothia Peninsula]] on 1 June 1831,
and to find out how far and how fast it had shifted in seventy years. That
question needed a fixed station running for months, not a ship dashing past. The
pole was some ninety miles off; the harbour was sheltered, had fresh water, and
sat on ground where reindeer and fish could be got. Amundsen chose it and let
the passage wait.

## The station

The buildings went up fast. Amundsen and [[person:Godfred Hansen]] dug the
trenches for the magnetic variation house themselves — "more than once we
stretched our aching backs, unaccustomed to this kind of work, and wished
trench-digging to the deuce" — and the observatory was finished on 26 September
1903. On the 29th they began a hut for [[person:Peder Ristvedt]] and
[[person:Gustav Juel Wiik]] to live in, 250 feet away on the same ridge; it took
sixty packing cases. Last came *Uranienborg*, the astronomical observatory,
built as a rotunda because Hansen wanted one, with an empty barrel for an
instrument pier.

Not a scrap of iron went into the magnetic house. The
[[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-003|self-registering variation instruments]] began
recording on 2 November 1903 and ran, with the ship as base, for nineteen
months — the longest continuous magnetic series taken anywhere in the Canadian
Arctic to that date. Wiik, who had trained at the magnetic observatory in
Potsdam, ran the absolute observations; Amundsen worked alongside him and, when
spring came and the load outgrew two men, Hansen was taught the instruments and
brought in. The results were not reduced in the field. They went home in the
ship and were worked up in Norway afterwards, which is exactly how a modern
research cruise handles its data.

By the second winter Amundsen was running two magnetic observatories, and the
second one is the best single illustration of what these two winters were
really about. One was Wiik's, built of old sails and tarpaulins in summer and
banked over with snow when the cold came. The other, a hundred yards north,
Amundsen had built as a large igloo, with thick ice windows let into all the
walls for daylight, and the instrument standing on a snow socket. It weighed
66 lbs, and Amundsen recorded that not the slightest movement of it was observed
during the whole of the spring. A European geophysicist had asked Netsilik
builders for a laboratory and got a better instrument pier than a barrel. The
two huts were within calling distance of one another so that simultaneous
readings could be taken. See
[[page:what-the-netsilingmiut-taught-them]].

## Towards the pole, April–May 1904

On 6 April 1904 Amundsen and Ristvedt set out by sledge up the east coast of
Boothia, travelling in company with [[person:Teraiu]] and
[[person:Kachkochnelli]] and their families, who were going to Abva (Mount
Matheson) to catch seals. It was 22° below zero and the first day of the year
they felt any warmth in the sun.

They got as far as a station a little south of the
[[place:Tasmania Islands]] and turned back on 7 May. Amundsen believed they had
"probably passed over" both magnetic poles, the 1831 position and the current
one, without being able to fix either. He meant to go on to Victoria Harbour and
take a comparison series where the two Rosses had wintered with the *Victory* in
the 1830s — "possibly even more interesting than at the Pole itself" — and did
not, because his left foot gave out and laid him up from 12 to 18 May.

Then, on 21 May, they reached their depot on the Boothia coast and found it
stripped: eleven pounds of pemmican scattered on the ground, and a sledge gone.
Amundsen names Kaumallo and Kalakchie. Ten cakes of pemmican, two packets of
chocolate and a little bread had to carry two men and their dogs back to the
ship, which they reached on the evening of 27 May. "So our trip was not a
brilliant success," he wrote. A year later the sledge came back — left out on
the ice near the Nechilli camp rather than handed over, which Amundsen read as
fear of what he might do. [[person:Anton Lund]] repaired it and pronounced it
better than new.

He is honest, on the whole, about theft going both ways and about his own
severity: when two men took a tin each from the ship he forbade them to come
aboard ever again, and they "slunk away very sheepishly".

## The rest of the two years

The routine that made this possible was food. [[person:Adolf Henrik Lindstrom]]
cooked fresh reindeer, seal and fish and baked bread all winter, and the *Gjøa*
came through three Arctic winters without a case of scurvy —
[[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-011|the cook with a catch]] is not an incidental
photograph. Lund and Helmer Hanssen were the hunters; twenty reindeer carcasses
came aboard from one trip to Booth Point in September 1903 alone. Hansen and
[[person:Helmer Hanssen]] charted the Neumayer Peninsula and built cairns on
every knoll, and found, to Amundsen's surprise, an island south of the station
that M'Clintock had missed — presumably, he thought, passed in a fog.

There was [[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-010|Christmas aboard in 1903]] and a long
winter of visits: Amundsen and Hansen took as many as thirteen Netsilingmiut
guests to sleep on the cabin floor on cold nights, the crew of the fore-cabin
declining on grounds of smell.

In April 1905 [[person:Godfred Hansen]] and Ristvedt left on the expedition's
largest piece of original survey: the unmapped east coast of Victoria Island,
north to Cape Nansen, where they left a document on 26 May 1905, 800 miles out
and back between 2 April and 25 June. Amundsen printed Hansen's own account as
the supplement to volume II of [[source:amundsen1908northwest]].

The ice did not let the ship out in the summer of 1904. It did in 1905: the
windlass turned at three in the morning on 13 August. What happened next is
[[page:the-passage-west-august-1905]].
