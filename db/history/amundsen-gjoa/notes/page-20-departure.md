The first leg of the *Gjøa*'s voyage, from [[place:Christiania]] to
[[place:Beechey Island]], took ten weeks and very nearly ended the expedition
twice inside forty-eight hours. It is told in chapters II and III of
[[source:amundsen1908northwest]], volume I.

## A departure at night, in the rain

The *Gjøa* cast off on the night of 16 June 1903, in torrential rain, with only
the crew's nearest relations on the quay. There were seven men aboard —
[[person:Roald Amundsen]], [[person:Godfred Hansen]], [[person:Anton Lund]],
[[person:Helmer Hanssen]], [[person:Peder Ristvedt]],
[[person:Gustav Juel Wiik]] and [[person:Adolf Henrik Lindstrom]] — in a 47-ton
sloop with a 13-horsepower paraffin motor.

The night sailing is usually explained by Amundsen's debts: he had mortgaged
himself to fit the ship out, and a creditor was threatening to have her stopped.
Amundsen himself says only that the last week of enforced idleness had worn
everyone down and they were glad to go. Both can be true. What is certain is
that the expedition left with no fanfare at all, and that the contrast with the
send-off Franklin's ships got in 1845 is the whole story of the *Gjøa* in
miniature: a small ship, a small crew, no navy behind it.

## Greenland: dogs, skins and Knud Rasmussen

The *Gjøa* worked up the Greenland coast. At
[[place:Qeqertarsuaq]] (Godhavn) on Disko Island she took on dogs, sledges and
sealskin clothing — Amundsen served out Iceland jerseys and "Nansen" clothing to
everyone, and most of the crew bartered for sealskins on top. She sailed on
31 July with a salute from the battery on the hill. The dogs came from two
sources and did not agree about it: Amundsen writes of "the old *Fram* dogs and
the new ones from Godhavn" settling their quarrels in a battle royal on the ice
whenever the ship stopped.

North of Upernavik they crossed Melville Bay — the stretch Amundsen called the
most difficult in the whole Northwest Passage for a ship as small as his — and
got through without mishap in the first half of August. Then, on 15 August, off
Wolstenholme Island, the lookout called two kayaks ahead. The two Inughuit men
who came alongside could not make themselves understood until one of them
grinned and said a single word: "Mylius!" A rattle of gunfire went up from
behind an ice mass and six more kayaks shot out, one flying a Norwegian flag and
one a Danish one. It was the Danish Literary Expedition, and the men who climbed
aboard were Ludvig Mylius-Erichsen and [[person:Knud Rasmussen]] — the same
Rasmussen who, twenty-eight years later, would publish
[[source:rasmussen1931netsilik|the fullest ethnography of the Netsilingmiut]]
that exists. Mylius-Erichsen gave Amundsen four dogs; two of the puppies were
named *Mylius* and *Gjoa*, and *Gjoa* was, Amundsen says, the best dog they had.
See [[topic:greenland-inughuit]].

## The depot on Dalrymple Rock

At [[place:Dalrymple Rock]], a bare cone of rock rising straight out of the sea,
the Scottish whaling captains Milne and Adams had landed a depot for the
expedition and walled it round with barbed wire. Mylius-Erichsen handed over
their letter wishing the *Gjøa* luck. There was no harbour, so the sloop lay
unprotected while a day and a half of loading went on.

She left on 17 August carrying 4,245 gallons of petroleum, with the deck down to
the waterline and cases piled almost to the main boom, the dogs perched on top
of them. This is worth holding on to: everything that follows in the next
fortnight — a fire and a grounding — is about a very small, very overloaded ship
full of fuel.

## Beechey Island, 22–24 August 1903

The *Gjøa* anchored in Erebus Bay at nine in the evening on 22 August. Amundsen
and Wiik began magnetic observations the next morning; the ship left at one
o'clock on the 24th. In between, Amundsen walked up to
[[topic:franklin-1845|Franklin's]] three graves, and to the memorials to Bellot
and Belcher's people, on the beach where Franklin's ships had spent the winter
of 1845–46.

> [[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-027|"I was sitting on one of the chain lockers
> looking towards the land with a deep, solemn feeling that I was on holy
> ground, Franklin's last safe winter harbour. My thought wandered back — far
> back."]]

He was about to sail, in a herring boat, past the place where those ships were
lost. See [[topic:franklin-search]] for what the searchers found here in 1850.

## The fire

On the night of 31 August a shriek went up on deck and flame and smoke came out
of the engine-room skylight — in a compartment holding 2,200 gallons of
petroleum. Wiik stayed at his post below. Two extinguishers and hand pumping put
it out.

The margin was one order given hours earlier. Amundsen had told Ristvedt to
drain a tank that was leaking, and Ristvedt had emptied it. In the fight with
the fire the tap of that now-empty tank was wrenched clean off. Had it still
been full, over 100 gallons of petroleum would have poured into the flames.

## The reef off Matty Island

At six the next morning, 1 September, the *Gjøa* went hard aground on a
submerged reef near [[place:Matty Island]] in James Ross Strait — the third
shoal she had touched that day, and this one had a fathom of water over its
shallowest part against a loaded draught of ten feet. A gale was rising.
Amundsen ordered the boats cleared and provisioned; the expedition was, for some
minutes, over.

It was Lund, the first mate, who proposed throwing the deck cargo overboard
instead of abandoning ship. Four-hundredweight cases went over the rail, the
gale lifted her, and she was carried bodily across the bare rock.

> [[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-028|"She was lifted up high and flung bodily on to
> the bare rocks, bump, bump — with terrific force …. In my distress I sent up
> (I honestly confess it) an ardent prayer to the Almighty. Yet another thump,
> worse than ever, then one more, and we slid off."]]

Eleven days later she anchored in the harbour at
[[place:Uqsuqtuuq]] and stayed nearly two years:
[[page:two-winters-at-uqsuqtuuq]].
