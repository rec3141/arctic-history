The *Gjøa* never sailed home. What happened to her afterwards, and what happened
to the name, is the reason this page exists on a Canadian icebreaker's dashboard
in 2026.

## The sloop

She was built by Knut Johannesson Skaale at Rosendal in 1872 — 70 feet by 20, a
square-sterned sloop of 47 register tons — and named for her first owner's wife.
For twenty-eight years she fished herring. [[person:Roald Amundsen]] bought her
on 28 March 1901 from Asbjørn Sexe of Ullensvang, largely with his inheritance,
and chose her for reasons he set out plainly: she was shallow enough to use the
shoals of the Arctic straits, and small enough that a crew of seven could live
off the country. He had watched what a large crew in large ships had cost
[[topic:franklin-1845|Franklin]].

He took her sealing in the Barents Sea for five months in 1901 as a training
cruise, then spent the winter strengthening her ice sheathing and fitting the
13-horsepower paraffin motor. She left [[place:Christiania]] on
[[page:the-night-departure-and-beechey-island|16 June 1903]] and reached
[[place:Nome]] on 31 August 1906.

## San Francisco, 1906–1972

From Nome she went on to San Francisco, where the Norwegian-American community
persuaded Amundsen to leave her rather than sail her home round Cape Horn. He
had his own reason for agreeing: the fame the *Gjøa* had won him was the lever
that would get him the *Fram*, and the *Fram* was the ship for what he intended
next. He and his crew went home by liner.

The sloop was given to the City of San Francisco in 1909 and dragged up the
beach to the north-west corner of Golden Gate Park, where she sat behind a low
fence for sixty-three years. A caretaker was hired and lived aboard — he is
enumerated on the ship in the 1910 US census — and a seal tank was installed
behind her stern, the fur seal serving as a night watchman. The vessel that had
survived the ice of James Ross Strait was steadily worn down by souvenir
hunters, salt, sand and campfires. A restoration begun in 1939 was interrupted
by the war and finished in 1949; by the 1960s she was deteriorating again.

In 1972 she was shipped back to Norway, largely through the efforts of Erik
Krag, a San Francisco shipping-company owner who was knighted for it. She went
to the Norwegian Maritime Museum at [[place:Bygdoy]] in Oslo, and in 2009 the
Fram Museum took over her exhibition; she now stands in her own building beside
[[person:Fridtjof Nansen|Nansen]]'s *Fram*. Her original engine was reinstalled
in 2017: [[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-023|the ship as she is now]]. A memorial
*bauta* stands where she lay in Golden Gate Park.

## What Amundsen did with what he learned

The line from [[place:Uqsuqtuuq]] to the South Pole is not a metaphor. Amundsen
reached the pole on 14 December 1911 with four companions and dog sledges, in
caribou clothing on the Netsilik pattern, having wintered in snow shelters and
eaten fresh meat — and came back with every man alive. Robert Falcon Scott's
party, hauling their own sledges in wool and gabardine, died on the return.
[[person:Helmer Hanssen]], who had learned to drive dogs at Uqsuqtuuq, drove the
lead sledge. [[person:Adolf Henrik Lindstrom]], the *Gjøa*'s cook, was the cook
at Framheim. The techniques came from
[[page:what-the-netsilingmiut-taught-them|the Netsilingmiut]]; the credit went
to Norway.

Amundsen disappeared in June 1928 in a flying boat over the Barents Sea, looking
for Umberto Nobile's crashed airship. He was fifty-five. No trace of the aircraft
has been found.

## The community

[[place:Uqsuqtuuq]] took the ship's name in English: Gjoa Haven, the only
settlement on King William Island, a hamlet of about 1,350 people at the 2021
census. It is Netsilik country and was long before 1903. The
[[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-025|Nattilik Heritage Centre]], opened in 2013, holds
the community's own account of this history, together with material from the
Franklin searches.

That is not incidental to the *Gjøa* story. Netsilik and Inuinnait oral
testimony — collected by Rae in 1854, by Hall and Schwatka, by Amundsen from
[[person:Uchyuneiu]] in 1904, and over a lifetime by Louie Kamookak of Gjoa
Haven — is what put the searchers in the right water. *Erebus* was found in 2014
in Wilmot and Crampton Bay, where Inuit testimony had always said a ship went
down; *Terror* was found in 2016 in Terror Bay after Sammy Kogvik of Gjoa Haven
told the search team about a mast he had seen sticking out of the ice.
See [[topic:wrecks-disasters]] and [[topic:inuit-oral-history]].

## CCGS *Amundsen*

The ship this dashboard runs on was laid down at Burrard Dry Dock in North
Vancouver on 4 January 1977, launched on 10 March 1978, and entered Canadian
Coast Guard service in March 1979 as CCGS *Franklin* — renamed *Sir John
Franklin* in 1980 at the crew's request. A *Pierre Radisson*-class icebreaker,
98 metres, Arctic Class 3. She spent the 1980s and 1990s on winter icebreaking
in the Gulf of St Lawrence and the Arctic sealift, was declared surplus in 1996,
used as an accommodation vessel at Voisey's Bay, and laid up in reserve in 2000.

In 2001 the Coast Guard could not supply an icebreaker for Arctic research at
all. A consortium of Canadian universities and federal departments proposed
converting the laid-up ship into a dedicated Arctic research vessel; the refit
at Les Méchins, Quebec, cost CAD $30.7 million, $27.7 million of it from the
Canada Foundation for Innovation. Holds became laboratories. A moon pool was cut
so that instruments can go down through the hull without cutting the ice.

She recommissioned as CCGS *Amundsen* on 26 August 2003 — ninety-eight years to
the day after the *Gjøa* met the *Charles Hanson* off
[[place:Nelson Head]] and completed the passage. Whether that was intended is
not recorded here; the coincidence is exact.

Since then she has been the platform for ArcticNet and Amundsen Science, spent
398 days in the Arctic in her first two missions, and in 2004 became the first
Canadian vessel to bring hospital services to remote northern communities since
the CCGS *C. D. Howe* was withdrawn in 1970. She took the passengers off the
grounded cruise ship *Clipper Adventurer* in Coronation Gulf in August 2010 and
assisted the grounded *Akademik Ioffe* in the Gulf of Boothia in August 2018.
Her image is on the back of the Canadian fifty-dollar note. Her helicopter
crashed in the Arctic on 9 September 2013 with the loss of three lives,
including a University of Manitoba scientist. Her home port is Quebec City.

## Why the name was changed

A ship named for Sir John Franklin became a ship named for Roald Amundsen. The
two men attempted the same water sixty years apart. Franklin took two
bomb vessels, 129 officers and men, three years of tinned provisions and no
intention of learning anything from the people who lived there, and lost every
man. Amundsen took a herring sloop, six companions, and two winters of
instruction from the [[page:what-the-netsilingmiut-taught-them|Netsilingmiut]],
and lost one man to illness and one, [[person:Manni]], to a shooting accident on
the way home.

A Canadian Arctic research vessel that works with northern communities, carries
Inuit knowledge holders and scientists in the same wardroom, and takes its
instrument packages down through a moon pool cut for the purpose, is on the
second side of that comparison. Whatever the Coast Guard's reasons in 2003, the
name fits the work.
