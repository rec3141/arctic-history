The *Gjøa* spent nearly two years at [[place:Uqsuqtuuq]] and thirteen days
completing the Northwest Passage. The thirteen days were the worst of the
voyage. Amundsen could not eat through most of them.

## Leaving, 13 August 1905

"Precisely at 3 a.m. on August 13th, 1905, the windlass turned." Amundsen had
taken a last farewell of [[person:Ugpi]] — the Owl — and his other friends among
the Netsilingmiut.

Two Netsilingmiut sailed with them. [[person:Talurnakto]] had been offered the
eighth place and could not, in the end, face leaving his country, so
[[person:Tonnich]] applied for it and got it; on coming aboard he was scrubbed
on deck under [[person:Gustav Juel Wiik]]'s supervision, clipped, dusted with
insect powder and dressed in a new frieze suit made in a Norwegian prison, and
Amundsen says no one was ever prouder of their clothes. A few days out, near
Douglas Bay, Tonnich changed his mind and went ashore.
[[person:Manni]] took his place. His foster-father
[[person:Umiktuallu]] came aboard and wanted paying for him, and was satisfied
with a file and an old knife — a transaction Amundsen records with amusement and
which reads very differently now.

## Navigating without a compass

The problem in these waters is the thing the expedition had come to study. This
close to the North Magnetic Pole a magnetic compass is useless, and the charts
were either blank or wrong. So the *Gjøa* worked west the way a small ship works
an uncharted coast: two lookouts aloft, a man on the lead without a pause, and
the captain or [[person:Godfred Hansen]] at the helm where they could see
furthest.

> "The man attending to the soundings had his full share of work; the lead flew
> up and down so rapidly that it was almost a wonder it did not melt. Ten
> fathoms and a clay bottom, was the report; then again, eight fathoms, stone;
> ten fathoms and clay." (vol. II, p. 107)

[[person:Anton Lund]] and [[person:Helmer Hanssen]] took the crow's nest, being
the best qualified; [[person:Peder Ristvedt]] and Wiik had the engine. Watches
ran eighteen hours a day.

## Eta Strait

[[place:Simpson Strait]] divides around Eta Island. Hansen and Helmer Hanssen
had established by sledge, the previous spring, that the northern channel was
impassable for the ship, so everything depended on the southern one: three
quarters of a mile wide, known to be foul, and, Amundsen wrote, a passage "we
had long talked about and shuddered at".

They took it under a flat calm on a broiling summer day. The lead never stopped;
the helm went from board to board "just as if we were in thick ice". The
shallowest water they found was three fathoms, and the *Gjøa* drew ten feet
loaded. "I can vouch for it, as it was my turn at the helm, that the passage did
not, by any means, resemble a bee-line." The narrative gives no date for this
day — it falls between the departure on the 13th and daybreak on the 15th.

## The Royal Geographical Society Islands, 15 August

At daybreak on 15 August a group of islands stood across the whole horizon north
to south — a group nobody had charted, which Amundsen discovered and named the
[[place:Royal Geographical Society Islands]]. Ice surrounded them. There was no
way round to north or south, so they picked the weakest point in the belt, went
full speed ahead with everybody holding on, and took the thumps.

West of the islands the bottom turned ragged and stony and the soundings jumped
from seventeen fathoms to five and back. "We bungled through zigzag, as if we
were drunk," with the lookout in the crow's nest "jumping about like a maniac,
throwing his arms about for starboard and port". Amundsen anchored on 17 August
at five in the morning, and afterwards wrote that his hours of rest in this
stretch were spent brooding, and that at every meal he felt a devouring hunger
and could not swallow.

Then the water opened, the watches went back to six hours, and they made fair
westing.

## Nelson Head, 26 August 1905

At four in the afternoon on 26 August a high land came up to windward through
mist. By dead reckoning they should have been off Cape Parry on the mainland, so
Amundsen took it for Cape Parry. When the air cleared in the early morning it
was [[place:Nelson Head]], the southern cape of Banks Island, and he had been
wrong by a long way. He is candid about it, and adds the explanation American
whalers later gave him: there is a great deal of iron in these mountains, the
compass is "utterly distracted", and strong currents finish the job.

His watch ended at eight and he turned in. Some time later he half woke to
running feet overhead and was annoyed, assuming a bear or a seal.

> [[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-029|"Lieutenant Hansen came rushing down into the
> cabin and called out the ever memorable words: 'Vessel in sight, sir!' … The
> North West Passage had been accomplished — my dream from childhood. This very
> moment it was fulfilled. I had a peculiar sensation in my throat … I could
> feel tears coming to my eyes."]]

The ship was the San Francisco whaling schooner *Charles Hanson*, Captain James
McKenna, working east out of the Pacific. A vessel coming from the west was the
proof: there was continuous navigable water from the Atlantic to the Pacific,
and the *Gjøa* had sailed it.

> [[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-030|"'Are you Captain Amundsen?' was his first
> remark. … 'Is this the first vessel you have met?' the old man asked. And when
> I admitted it was so, his countenance brightened up. We shook hands long and
> heartily. 'I am exceedingly pleased to be the first one to welcome you on
> getting through the North West Passage.'"]]

**A note on the date.** The events are entered here on 26 August 1905, the date
Amundsen and his crew kept and the one the literature uses. Read closely, his
own narrative puts the sighting of Nelson Head at 4 p.m. on the 26th and the
meeting after 8 a.m. the *following* morning — which would make it the 27th.
This database follows his date and flags the discrepancy rather than silently
correcting a man's account of the most important morning of his life.

## What "first" means

Others had established that the passage existed. [[topic:franklin-1845|Franklin]]
proved the last link at the cost of his ships and 129 men;
[[topic:mcclure-investigator|McClure]] crossed from west to east in 1850–54 but
abandoned the *Investigator* and finished by sledge and in another ship;
[[topic:franklin-search|the searchers]] filled in the coasts. What the *Gjøa*
did that nobody had done was carry one hull the whole way. She could do it
because she was small enough to use three fathoms of water and shallow channels
no naval ship could enter — and because her crew had spent two winters learning
how to live there.

The passage was made on 26 August 1905. The *Gjøa* did not reach the Pacific for
another year: [[page:the-third-winter-at-king-point]].
