# SCICEX and Arctic Submarine Ice Research: Extract from Workshop Report (1999) and GRL Papers

## 1. CRUISES

- Commanding Officer: CDR Brian Wegner
- Chief Scientist: T. DeLaca
- Duration: 21 days in Data Release Area
- Historic first mission: pilot/proof-of-concept cruise; able to penetrate interior regions of Makarov and Canada basins that icebreakers had not reached (SCICEX 2000, p. 647)

**USS Cavalla (1995)** — 26 March – 8 May 1995
- Commanding Officer: CDR Joe Leidig
- Chief Scientist: T. DeLaca
- Duration: ~6 weeks

**USS Pogy (1996)** — 13 September – 28 October 1996
- Commanding Officer: CDR Jim Reilly
- Chief Scientist: R. Sambrotto
- Duration: ~6 weeks
- Collected patch ice survey data (initial survey September 1996, follow-up 40 days later in October 1996, SCICEX 2000, p. 5368); SCAMP geophysical mapping data (SCICEX 2000, p. 4768)

**USS Archerfish (1997)** — 3 September – 2 October 1997
- Commanding Officer: CDR Steve Kremer
- Chief Scientist: T. Whitledge
- Duration: ~30 days
- Autumn cruise; surfacing in sea ice was not permitted by Navy (SCICEX 2000, p. 583)

**USS Hawkbill (1998)** — 1 August – 2 September 1998
- Commanding Officer: CDR R. Perry
- Chief Scientist: R. Muench
- Duration: ~32 days
- August cruise; collected SCAMP data to complement 1996 narrow-beam bathymetry data (SCICEX 2000, p. 5487)

**USS Hawkbill (1999)** — April – May 1999
- Commanding Officer: CDR R. Perry
- Chief Scientist: M. Edwards
- Spring cruise (scheduled in report; "scheduled in the spring of 1999", SCICEX 2000, p. 416)

### Historical Cruise (Pre-SCICEX)

**USS Pargo (1993)** — Also referenced as the 1993 Pargo cruise in Rothrock et al. (1999), this is the same cruise listed above; data are publicly available through NSIDC (Rothrock 1999, p. 3469).

### Program Structure & Declassification

- **Memorandum of Agreement (MOA)**: Signed August 1994; Navy committed to five cruises through 1999 (SCICEX 2000, p. 4230-4231)
- **Program Start**: Initiated 1993 with USS Pargo proof-of-concept cruise; expanded into SCICEX program (SCICEX 2000, p. 407-417)
- **Data Release Area (DRA)**: Navy approved declassification and release of data within the "SCICEX box," an irregular polygon excluding foreign exclusive economic zones (SCICEX 2000, p. 421-424)
- **Arctic Submarine Laboratory (ASL)**: Located at SUBDEVRON FIVE DET ASL, San Diego CA 92106; responsible for mission planning and support to civilian scientists (SCICEX 2000, p. 492-493)
- No specific Gore-McCain reference found in examined sections; declassification governed by Navy guidelines (SCICEX 2000, p. 1549)

## 2. FINDINGS

### Ice Draft / Thickness Thinning (Rothrock et al. 1999)

**Overall Arctic change (1958–1976 to 1993–1997):**
- Mean ice draft decreased from **3.1 m to 1.8 m** = **1.3 m decrease (42%)** (Rothrock 1999, p. 3469, Table 1)
- Mean ice thickness in the 1990s: **between 1 and 3 m**, "considerably thinner than previous estimates" (Rothrock 1999, p. 3470)

**Regional breakdown (1958–1976 baseline vs. 1993–1997):**
- Chukchi Cap: 2.1 m → 1.2 m (−0.9 m, −43%)
- Beaufort Sea: 2.1 m → 1.2 m (−0.9 m, −43%)
- Canada Basin: 3.5 m → 2.2 m (−1.3 m, −37%)
- North Pole: 3.8 m → 2.4 m (−1.4 m, −37%)
- Nansen Basin: 3.9 m → 2.2 m (−1.7 m, −43%)
- Eastern Arctic: 3.3 m → 1.5 m (−1.8 m, −55%)
(Rothrock 1999, Table 1, p. 3471)

**Interannual trends (1993–1997):** Modest evidence of continued thinning at rate ~0.1 m yr⁻¹ overall; trends negative in three of six regions; significant at 80–90% level in four regions (Rothrock 1999, p. 3471)

**1993–1997 regional trends (m yr⁻¹):**
- Chukchi Cap: +0.01
- Beaufort Sea: −0.14
- Canada Basin: −0.17
- North Pole: +0.01
- Nansen Basin: +0.17
- Eastern Arctic: −0.28
- All regions: −0.10
(Rothrock 1999, Table 2, p. 3471)

### Atlantic Layer Warming (SCICEX 2000)

- Temperature of Atlantic water layer (ca. 200–800 m depth): **warmed by as much as 1°C in some locations** (SCICEX 2000, p. 640)
- Evidence from 1993 USS Pargo cruise most dramatic (SCICEX 2000, p. 645–646)
- Water overlying Atlantic layer in Eurasian Basin became "more salty (and less stably stratified)" (SCICEX 2000, p. 642)
- Atlantic layer circulates counterclockwise around Arctic at several hundred meters depth; indicates "strengthened inflow of warm water into the Arctic Ocean that could affect the ice cover" (SCICEX 2000, p. 506–510)

### Boundary Shift (Upper Ocean Structure)

- Prior to 1990: Atlantic/Pacific boundary along Lomonosov Ridge
- After 1990s: Shifted to Alpha and Mendeleyev ridges (SCICEX 2000, p. 636–637)

### Halocline & Chemical Tracers

- Boundary between Pacific and Atlantic regimes in halocline "particularly clear" in distribution of isotope ¹²⁹I, which originates from European nuclear reprocessing plants and enters Arctic from Atlantic side (SCICEX 2000, p. 666–668)

### Bathymetry & Geophysics (SCICEX 2000)

- **Gravity and bathymetry data suggest Earth's crust is thin or nonexistent at slow-spreading Gakkel Ridge** (SCICEX 2000, p. 526–528)
- Peak-to-trough Free Air gravity anomalies ranged **80–120 mGals** at Gakkel Ridge (SCICEX 2000, p. 4748)
- Morphology of Gakkel Ridge "distinctively blocky" with axis deeper than typical slow-spreading ridges (SCICEX 2000, p. 4742)
- Combined gravity anomaly and bathymetry data require "thin, normal density crust" at Gakkel Ridge (SCICEX 2000, p. 4752)
- Results suggest magmatic production at Gakkel Ridge may be suppressed (SCICEX 2000, p. 4756)

### Biology & Chemistry (SCICEX 2000)

- Canada Basin is "a large repository for carbon from rivers, the atmosphere, and other areas of the Arctic Ocean" (SCICEX 2000, p. 524–525)
- Biological and chemical data show "regions where organic matter and carbon dioxide are entering the Arctic Ocean" (SCICEX 2000, p. 520–523)

## 3. ROTHROCK 1999 & KWOK 2009: KEY NUMBERS & CRUISES USED

### Rothrock et al. 1999 (GRL)

**Cruises used for comparison:**
- Historical period (1958–1976): USS Nautilus (August 1958), USS Seadragon (August 1960 and July 1962), USS Skate (July 1962), USS Queenfish (August 1970), HMS Sovereign (October 1976)
- 1990s period (SCICEX): USS Pargo (September 1993), USS Pogy (September–October 1996), USS Archerfish (September 1997)
(Rothrock 1999, p. 3470)

**Mean drafts (all regions):**
- 1958–1976: **3.1 m**
- 1993–1997: **1.8 m**
- Change: **−1.3 m (−42%)**
(Rothrock 1999, p. 3471, Table 1)

### Kwok & Rothrock 2009 (GRL)

**Data sources:**
- Historical cruises: nine submarine cruises 1993–1997 (SCICEX); nine submarines 1958–1976; 29 crossing locations identified (Kwok 2009, p. 91–97)
- ICESat data: ten campaigns spanning 2003–2008, fall campaigns (mid-Oct to mid-Nov) and winter campaigns (late Feb to late Mar) (Kwok 2009, p. 57–66)
- Submarine data from 34 cruises over 1975–2000 in DRA providing 2,203 samples of 50-km mean draft (Kwok 2009, p. 227–235)

**Three periods compared (end of melt season, seasonally adjusted to September 15):**

**Period 1 (1958–1976) vs. Period 2 (1993–1997) vs. Period 3 (2003–2008):**
- Chukchi Cap: 1.95 m → 0.98 m → 0.70 m [Change (1)→(2): −0.97 m, −50%; (1)→(3): −1.25 m, −64%]
- Beaufort Sea: 1.95 m → 0.98 m → 0.97 m [−0.97 m, −50%; −0.97 m, −50%]
- Canada Basin: 3.45 m → 2.05 m → 1.70 m [−1.40 m, −40%; −1.75 m, −51%]
- North Pole: 3.77 m → 2.27 m → 1.89 m [−1.51 m, −40%; −1.89 m, −50%]
- Nansen Basin: 3.88 m → 2.05 m → 2.11 m [−1.83 m, −47%; −1.77 m, −46%]
- Eastern Arctic: 3.24 m → 1.30 m → 1.24 m [−1.94 m, −60%; −2.00 m, −62%]
- **All Regions: 3.02 m → 1.62 m → 1.43 m** [−1.40 m, −46%; −1.59 m, −53%]
(Kwok 2009, Table 1, p. 151–162)

**Trends (m yr⁻¹):**
- 1993–1997 (SCICEX): −0.11 m yr⁻¹ overall
- 2003–2007 (ICESat): −0.18 m yr⁻¹ overall
(Kwok 2009, Table 2, p. 171–185)

**Long-term thickness change (1980–2008):**
- Peak winter thickness (1980 submarine data): **3.64 m**
- 1.89 m (winter 2008 ICESat record)
- **Net decrease: 1.75 m (48%)**
- Summer thickness: 2.80 m (1980) → 1.15 m (2007)
- **Decrease: 1.65 m**
(Kwok 2009, p. 202–204)

**Steepest rate of decrease in submarine era:** 0.08 m yr⁻¹ in 1990; ICESat period (2003–2008) shows winter/summer decline of **0.10/0.20 m yr⁻¹** (Kwok 2009, p. 265)

## 5. QUOTES (SCICEX 2000 Report)

**Quote 1** (p. 506–510):
"Hydrographic data show a warming of the Atlantic water that circulates counterclockwise around the Arctic Ocean at several hundred meters depth; this indicates a strengthened inflow of warm water into the Arctic Ocean that could affect the ice cover."

**Quote 2** (p. 515–519):
"Sea ice draft data show how thin the ice cover has become in the 1990s, especially in the eastern longitudes of the Arctic Ocean; this may prove to be one sign of the Arctic's response to global warming."

**Quote 3** (p. 545–553):
"Alone among the ships operating in the Arctic, the submarine can operate freely beneath the shifting pack ice. It is also stable and quiet. These attributes make it an ideal platform for underway observations. The great strength of the submarine in the Arctic Ocean is in making repeated basin-wide surveys as well as more localized and detailed surveys and in geophysical mapping."

**Quote 4** (p. 4287–4291):
"SCICEX has been a shining example of 'dual use' of military assets. In tandem with national security objectives, it has given us access to the Arctic Ocean for research and education. This region's scientific secrets have remained locked away in ice long after our other oceans were being opened to exploration."

**Quote 5** (p. 4316–4318):
"SCICEX has found dramatic changes in temperature, ocean currents, and how water coming in from the Pacific and Atlantic moves around the Arctic Ocean. And we have indicators that the sea ice covering the Arctic Ocean is thinning."

**Quote 6** (p. 938–941):
"The first things we want to know about sea ice are its extent and its thickness. Such knowledge has applications in understanding physical, chemical, and biological systems in the Arctic, in a broad range of engineering problems related to operations in the Arctic, and in studies of environmental change."
