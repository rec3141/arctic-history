# Extract from Lyon 1959: The Polar Submarine and Navigation of the Arctic Ocean

## 1. CRUISES

### USS NAUTILUS (Wilkins 1931)
- **Date:** 1931
- **Area:** North of Spitsbergen
- **Description:** First attempt to dive under ice; discussion given by Sir Hubert Wilkins and associates and Naval Examining Board

### USS ATULE (SS403)
- **Date:** 28 July 1946
- **Area:** Kane Basin
- **Action:** Penetrated 1,000 yards under ice field but suffered collision between ice and periscope shears

### USS SENNET (SS408)
- **Commanding Officer:** Comdr. J. B. Icenhower, USN
- **Date:** January 1947
- **Area:** Ross Sea, Antarctica (Task Force 68, Central Task Group)
- **Assignment:** Establishment of air-base on Ross Ice Barrier
- **Task Group Ships:** USS MT. OLYMPUS (AGC8), USS MERRICK (AKA97), USS YANCEY (AKA93), USCGC NORTHWIND (WAG282)
- **Passage Dates:** Ice pack transit began 31 December 1946
- **Description:** Demonstrated punishment submarine can take in heavy ice; bow ramming operations, towed by NORTHWIND
- **Positions:** Vicinity of Scott Island; Ross Sea ice pack extending from Barrier to Scott Island
- **Results:** Sonar and radar observations near Scott Island at northern edge of ice pack

### USS BOARFISH (SS327)
- **Commanding Officer:** Comdr. J. H. Turner, USN
- **Date:** August 1947 (initial dive 1 August)
- **Area:** Chukchi Sea
- **Submarine Force:** Included USS NEREUS (AS17), USS CAIMAN (SS323), USS CABAZON (SS334), USS CHUBB (SS329)
- **Commander:** Rear Admiral A. R. McCann (overall command)
- **Positions:** Observers aboard on initial dive (1 August 1947): Rear Admiral A. R. McCann, USN; Captain L. C. Chappel; Captain D. C. White
- **Under-Ice Operations:** Three extended dives totaling approximately 10 hours, distance of about 30 miles
  - **1 August 1947 (0900):** Test heading into ice at slow speed; obtained QLA ranges up to 600 yards on flat ice 15 feet long
  - **1 August 1947 (1439):** Initial dive to 110 feet with Admiral McCann aboard; keel depth 120 feet; ran under ice; ice average 8-10 feet deep (pieces ~75 feet long); one piece reached 40 feet deep; circled and headed out
  - **4 August 1947:** Morning dive to 110 feet; loose brash and slush at ice field edge; under ice: QLA showed good indications on 6-foot ice, strong echoes from 40-foot ice
  - **5 August 1947:** Longest run, five miles; keel depth 120 feet; average QLA contact ranges 400 yards with echoes at 800 yards; ice averaged 10-15 feet thick, 1,500 yards horizontal extent; passed under 50-foot-deep ice piece
- **Oceanographic Survey:** USS NEREUS conducted surveys across Bering Sea and near ice in Chukchi Sea, plus inshore work along Alaskan coast

### USS CARP (SS338)
- **Commanding Officer:** Comdr. J. M. Palmer, USN
- **Date:** 3-11 September 1948
- **Area:** Chukchi Sea, vicinity 167°W, 72°N
- **Description:** Successfully conducted vertical dives and ascents in ice lakes; penetrated pack ice approximately 54 miles to edge of consolidated arctic pack
- **Operations:** About fourteen vertical dives and ascents in ice lakes
- **Representative Dive (5 September 1948):** Ascent from one lake to adjoining lake separated by floe ~400 yards across; QLA indicated considerable glacons with clear area of ~350 yards; surfacing order at 125 yards after topside fathometer indicated passage from under floe; floe lay ~100 yards astern, glacon ~200 yards forward
- **Track Chart:** Figure 13 shows position of brash ice and arctic pack
- **Results:** Successful vertical dive and ascent procedure; some ice caught on afterdeck during vertical ascent

## 2. WILKINS'S NAUTILUS 1931

The report mentions Wilkins's 1931 NAUTILUS expedition north of Spitsbergen as the first attempt to dive under ice. A complete discussion of this experiment was given by Sir Hubert Wilkins and his associates and by the Naval Examining Board. The expedition is referenced as demonstrating early interest in under-ice submarine capability but is not detailed in this 1948 report.

## 5. KEY CONCLUSIONS (1948 Report Requirements for Arctic Submarine)

- **Under-Ice Sonar Navigation:** Scanning sonar (QLA) with Plan Position Indicator (PPI) display is essential for under-ice navigation. QLA provides range and bearing to ice features out to 600+ yards, with most critical information at 300-400 yards range. Sonar distinguishes between large, medium, and small ice pieces by visual display and aural indications (smooth pieces give bell-like echo; rough jagged pieces give polychromatic echo).

- **Upward Fathometer for Vertical Operations:** Type NK portable fathometer (Submarine Signal Co. 808B/808J) mounted on topside is mandatory for detecting ice overhead during vertical ascents into ice lakes. Fathometers must be installed both forward and aft to prevent accidents during ascents, with forward-looking fathometer critical for detecting ice in the direction of ascent.

- **Buoyancy Control for Vertical Maneuvering:** Improved buoyancy control systems required. Safety tank venting causes sonar blanking at critical times; solution requires either large inboard vents on safety tank or small amidship tank at center of buoyancy with variable water displacement capability for flexible depth control without acoustic interference.

- **Pressure Hull Modifications (Turtleback Design):** Submarine must be made symmetrical with topside keels (turtleback) to rest underneath ice with positive buoyancy, analogous to "bottoming." Turtleback provides flush deck approximately 1,400 square feet at height of present bridge, allowing resting under ice floes at low pressure (estimated 0.5 psi at 50 tons positive buoyancy).

- **Ice-Boring Capability:** Thermal ice drills required for engine supply and exhaust to allow operations under continuous ice. Four ice drills proposed as heat-pressure type devices with electric resistance heaters, driven by hydraulic pressure from ship's systems. Drills must bore through ice to create breathing holes for snorkel air intake and hot exhaust exit; minimum 8-inch inside diameter recommended for 30-foot drill length to deliver 12,000 cubic feet per minute of air.

- **Supporting Modifications:** Installation of 32-foot periscopes in control room, retractable sonar heads, modifications to internal equipment for sonar navigation and thermal drill operation. Simple modification of fleet-type submarine can supply approximately 75 percent of answers needed for design of strictly under-ice operational vessel.

## 6. QUOTES

1. **On Arctic Ocean Navigation Capability (p. 6):**
   > "The Arctic Ocean is potentially navigable. The realization of an under-ice submarine is within reach, and is far closer than had been envisioned. Simple modification of a fleet-type submarine may supply 75 per cent of the research answers."

2. **On Diving and Buoyancy Control (p. 29-30):**
   > "Normal diving and surface procedure required revision while operating in the arctic ice-field as vertical ascents and descents were almost always mandatory. The chief disadvantage in using safety tank was the necessary venting which blanked out the sonar gear."

3. **On Sonar for Under-Ice Navigation (p. 48):**
   > "The choice of safe cruising depth is determined from oceanographic data of the area (bottom contour, density gradients, expected ice thickness for particular season), observed physical properties of ice surface, and experience. The QLA provides a continual check, or index, on the bottom contour and the character of the ice canopy."

4. **On Ice Drill Requirements (p. 73-74):**
   > "The topside keel provides a flush deck, or 'turtleback' for ascents in leads, and for resting under ice floes. It is a free-flooding superstructure with the deck at about the height of the present bridge. Four ice drills are proposed which can be interconnected in any intake-exhaust sequence in order to provide flexibility and safety."

5. **On BOARFISH Ice Thickness Observations (p. 48):**
   > "The longest run under the ice, five miles, was made on this day. The ice averaged 10 to 15 feet in thickness and 1500 yards in horizontal extent. We passed under one piece of ice that was fifty feet deep."

6. **On Submarine Capability After Operations (p. 6):**
   > "Such modification is urgent, as an interim measure, because of the long delay between the design and completion of a strictly under-ice submarine."
