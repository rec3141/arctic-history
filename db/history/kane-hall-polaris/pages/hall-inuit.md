[[person:Charles Francis Hall]] was a Cincinnati engraver and small-time newspaper printer with no money, no ship, no crew and no Arctic experience. What he had was a conviction that survivors of [[topic:franklin-1845|Franklin's expedition]] might still be living among the Inuit, and that the way to find out was to go and live among the Inuit himself. He is the only nineteenth-century Arctic traveller of any nation who did that as a method rather than as an emergency.

He sailed from New London on 29 May 1860 as a passenger on the whaling barque *George Henry*, thirty-one souls aboard, with one interpreter, [[person:Kudlago]]. Kudlago died at sea on 1 July "in lat. 63 deg N. when near the coast of Greenland, and about 300 miles from his native place", asking "Do you see ice, ice?" Hall had lost his interpreter before the voyage had properly begun. The ship anchored in Cornelius Grinnell Bay on 8 August.

## Two accidents that made him

The first was the gale of 27 September 1860 in Field Bay:

> "On the 27th day of September (1860) there broke upon us that fearful gale which caused the loss of my expedition boat and the far-famed *Rescue*, drove the *Georgiana* on shore, and came near proving the destruction of the *George Henry* and all on board."

Of his own boat there was "nothing remaining but the stern-post fast to a three-inch cable". The plan of sailing to King William Island was finished. What was left was to travel as Inuit travelled — by sledge, on foot, in igloos, eating what was hunted — and that is what made him.

The second was 2 November 1860.

> "While intently occupied in my cabin, writing, I heard a soft, sweet voice say, Good morning, sir. The tone in which it was spoken — musical, lively, and varied — instantly told me that a lady of refinement was there greeting me. I was astonished. Could I be dreaming?" ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-022]])

## Tookoolito and Ebierbing

[[person:Tookoolito]] — Taqulittuq, called Hannah by the whalers — was a young Inuk woman from Qikiqtaaluk (Baffin Island) who had been taken to England in 1853 with her husband [[person:Ebierbing]] (Ipiirvik, "Joe") and another Inuk, and had spent two years there. She spoke, read and wrote English. She had dined with Queen Victoria and the Prince Consort. She poured tea from a tin caddy in her own tupik and kept the manners of an English drawing room in a sealskin tent, and she was entirely and unsentimentally Inuk.

She also assessed the qallunaat, which almost nobody in this literature was in a position to do:

> "I feel very sorry to say that many of the whaling people are very bad, making the Innuits bad too; they swear very much, and make our people swear. I wish they would not do so. Americans swear a great deal — more and worse than the English. I wish no one would swear." ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-023]])

From that November until Hall's death eleven years later, Tookoolito and Ebierbing worked with him on every expedition. She interpreted; he hunted, drove, guided and built. Neither is a servant in the record and neither is a curiosity: they are the two people without whom nothing Hall achieved would have happened.

The apprenticeship was literal. On 10 January 1861 Hall left the ship by sledge with Ebierbing, Tookoolito and Koodloo at thirty below and did not sleep aboard again for six weeks: "My first night (February 21st, 1861) on board the *George Henry*, after forty-three days away in an igloo." Two days before he left, he had sat all night beside a dying woman, Nukertou, whom custom required be left alone — "At last I could count nineteen between her inspirations... At length Nukertou ceased to live." Tookoolito made him withdraw before the death itself, so that his presence would not spoil the skin clothing. He recorded the custom and obeyed it, which is more than most of his contemporaries did.

## Nine generations: the Frobisher tradition

Hall never found a Franklin survivor. What he found instead, in Field Bay in the spring of 1861, was one of the best-attested pieces of long-transmitted oral history anywhere in the Arctic record.

On 11 May 1861, through Tookoolito, an elder named [[person:Ookijoxy Ninoo]] — Hall thought her about a hundred years old — described ships that had come long ago: first two, then three, then many. She described bricks, coal and timber left on an island called Niountelik. She described five white men taken by her people, who wintered ashore, built a boat with a mast and sails, and went away in it.

Martin Frobisher came into this bay in 1576 with two vessels, in 1577 with three, and in 1578 with fifteen. On the first voyage he lost five men. See [[topic:frobisher]].

> "Nine generations had passed away since the visit of Frobisher, yet now, on the 11th of May, 1861, I received from an old woman, probably a hundred years old, statements which I could not otherwise than believe to be facts concerning him and his co-adventurers!" ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-024]])

(Note for anyone working from earlier summaries: Hall wrote **nine** generations, not five.)

He then found the physical corroboration. On 28 May, sorting through Inuit oddments at Twerpukjua, "my eye accidentally caught sight of a piece of brick". Its owners said their mother had given it to them long ago and pointed to Niountelik; Ebierbing said, "That is the same as I have seen on that island." Hall: "This relic, then, was more precious to me than the gold which Frobisher sought there under the direct patronage of Queen Elizabeth." In September 1861 he landed on [[place:Kodlunarn Island]] — *qallunaat*, the white man's island, as the Inuit had called it from time immemorial — and found stone house remains, coal, iron and glass "all covered with the moss of ages", a trench some eighty-eight feet long, and the slip on the shore where a boat had been built.

He also settled a three-hundred-year-old cartographic error. Frobisher had taken this inlet for a strait leading through to Asia. Working from Inuit knowledge and from a chart drawn for him by [[person:Koojesse]] on 24 August 1860, Hall showed that [[place:Frobisher Bay]] is a bay ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-019]]).

The account of the Kodlunarn landing itself is in *Life with the Esquimaux* volume II, which is **not** among the staged texts — the staged copy is volume I only and stops in June 1861 — so the September 1861 landing is currently cited at second hand from [[source:nourse1879narrative]]. A request for volume II is on the record and was answered "need more info".

## Coming home, and what it cost

Hall returned to New London on 13 September 1862 with three cases and a cask of Frobisher relics and a twenty-pound piece of iron. Tookoolito and Ebierbing came with him, and stayed. Their first child, Tukeliketa — "the Butterfly", born about August 1861 — was exhibited with his parents on Hall's American lecture tour and died in New York on 28 February 1863, aged eighteen months.

He was the first of the three children they buried, and the only one of the three buried at Groton before Panik. Their second, called Little King William, died on the trail on 13 May 1866 during the second expedition. Their adopted daughter [[person:Panik]], who survived six months on the ice floe as a small child, died at [[place:Groton]] on 18 March 1875. Tookoolito died there of consumption on 31 December 1876, aged thirty-eight — "Among her last words was the petition, Come, Lord Jesus, and take thy poor creature home!" ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-020]]).

Ebierbing outlived them all. Before going north again with Schwatka in 1878 he was seen at the Starr Burying Ground weeding the grass from his wife's grave:

> "Hannah gone! Punna gone! Me go now again to King William's Land; if have to fight, me no care." ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-027]])

He went back to the Arctic and is thought to have died there about 1881.
