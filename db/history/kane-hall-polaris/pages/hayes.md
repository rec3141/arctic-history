[[person:Isaac Israel Hayes]] had been Kane's surgeon. He was one of the nine who walked away from the *Advance* in August 1854 and one of the nine carried back to her on Inughuit sledges in December ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-006]]); he lost toes doing it. Five years later he went back as commander, in a schooner of his own, to prove the thing Kane's man Morton thought he had seen from [[place:Cape Constitution]]: an ice-free ocean at the top of the world.

The schooner *United States* was hauled into the stream at Boston late on 6 July 1860 with fourteen persons aboard. Hayes gives no date for finally standing out to sea — one of many gaps in [[source:hayes1867open]], which is a literary book rather than a log.

## Going north

He reached [[place:Upernavik]] on the evening of 12 August. That night his boatswain and carpenter [[person:Gibson Caruthers]] "had retired the night before in perfect health, and was found dead in his berth next morning"; he was buried in the Upernavik ground the following day. Hayes was held four days and engaged [[person:Jensen]] as interpreter and hunter.

He crossed Melville Bay in fifty-five hours, and at Cape York on 25 August found [[person:Hans Hendrik]] living in a sealskin tent two hundred feet above the sea, watching for a ship. Six years after Kane's party had left him behind, Hans Hendrik came aboard with his wife [[person:Merkut]] and their ten-month-old daughter Pingasuk. Hayes never once writes the name Hans Hendrik or Suersaq in the whole book: he is only "Hans".

The schooner was frozen in at [[place:Port Foulke]], on the Greenland side just south of Etah, at about 78 deg 17 min N. Hayes gives no date for entering the harbour, naming it, or being locked in — the span in the timeline is worked back from his own remark of 12 July 1861, "It is just ten months to a day since we were locked up", so it is entered as approximate. The pages of the staged text that would have given the sunset directly (pp. 100-101) are missing from this copy; counting his own "one hundred and twenty-six days" back from the sun's return on 18 February 1861 gives about 15 October 1860.

Of that winter he wrote the sentence the book is remembered for:

> "I have seen no expression on the face of Nature so filled with terror as THE SILENCE OF THE ARCTIC NIGHT." ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-014]])

## Sonntag's death

In the first fortnight of December 1860 a distemper killed eighteen of Hayes's thirty-six dogs. "Seven died during four days, and I saw with consternation my fine teams melting away and my hopes endangered." Without dogs there would be no sledge journey and no farthest north, so on 21 December — midwinter, in the dark — he sent his astronomer and second, [[person:August Sonntag]], and Hans Hendrik south to buy dogs from the Inughuit: one sledge, nine dogs, twelve days' provisions, no tent.

Sonntag never came back. By Hans Hendrik's account, given to Hayes on 1 February 1861, Sonntag ran ahead of the dogs to warm himself, went through thin ice at a recently opened tide-crack, refused to stop and change into the dry clothes on the sledge, and was speechless by the time Hans Hendrik got him into an abandoned snow hut at [[place:Sorfalik]]. He died there after nearly a day unconscious. Hayes gives no day of death; the only date anywhere is the one chiselled on the gravestone, "Died December, 1860".

Hayes cross-questioned him for an hour and put three things he could not reconcile on the page — that Sonntag travelled five miles in wet clothing with an experienced hunter beside him, that the sledge apron and sleeping bag and a full change of dry clothes went unused, and that no message was sent:

> "Hans sticks to the story of yesterday; and, after questioning and cross-questioning him for an hour, I get nothing new." ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-016]])

He then formally declined the conclusion: "it would be unreasonable as well as unjust to suspect him of desertion." Note carefully what is and is not being said. The charge Hayes rejects is desertion, not killing; and he had already suspected Hans over the disappearance of the hunter [[person:Peter]] a month earlier and sent him out anyway, because "Hans was the only available driver, for he alone knew where to find the native villages". A white commander's suspicion of an Inuk employee, printed and never withdrawn, is part of this record; [[source:hendrik1878memoirs|Hans Hendrik's own memoirs]] are the other part, and neither should be read without the other. See [[page:the-inuit-of-these-expeditions]].

The teams were only rebuilt when [[person:Kalutunah]], [[person:Tattarat]] and [[person:Myouk]] came in with dogs at the end of February.

## The farthest north that is not accepted

On the evening of 3 April 1861 Hayes left Port Foulke with twelve men, two dog sledges and a twenty-foot metallic lifeboat on a man-hauled sledge, to cross Smith Sound and go north up the Ellesmere shore. The crossing alone took thirty-one days: eighty miles as the crow flies, some five hundred actually travelled over broken ice. He made the western shore near Cape Hawks on 11 May — "In camp at last, close under the land; and as happy as men can be who have achieved success and await supper."

Pushing on with [[person:George F. Knorr]] alone after Jensen broke down, he stopped on 18-19 May at what he called [[place:Cape Lieber]] and left a record in a glass vial under a cairn:

> "This point, the most northern land that has ever been reached, was visited by the undersigned, May 18th, 19th, 1861... My observations place us in latitude 81 deg 35 min, longitude 70 deg 30 min W." ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-012]])

From a cliff he took to be eight hundred feet high he saw water again — Morton's sea, from the other side:

> "all the evidences showed that I stood upon the shores of the Polar Basin, and that the broad ocean lay at my feet." ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-013]])

**The claim is not accepted and never has been.** Hayes's own preface concedes that his positions were computed in the field without reducing the magnetic variation or fixing absolute time. Later surveys of the coast he described cannot be reconciled with 81 deg 35 min, and he was probably some degrees short. The coordinates in this database are entered as *his figures*, flagged in both the place note and the event detail, and must not be read as a position. He named a headland he could only see, at an estimated 82 deg 30 min, Cape Union.

He was back aboard on 3 June, having travelled by his own count 1,300 miles since 3 April.

## Leaving

The schooner floated again on 11 July 1861 — "the schooner gave a lurch to port... and we were really afloat" — ten months to a day since she was locked in. Hayes said goodbye to the Inughuit at [[place:Etah]] on the 13th. Kalutunah wept.

> "'Alas!' said he, 'we will soon be all gone.' I told him that I would come back... 'Come back soon,' said he, 'or there will be none here to welcome you!'" ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-015]])

Hayes counted the whole group at about a hundred people, "a very considerable diminution since Dr. Kane left them, in 1855". He sailed on 14 July, was dismasted in an equinoctial gale, put into Halifax, and reached Boston in October — no day given; the only fix in the book is the first newspaper he bought, carrying the Ball's Bluff battle of 21 October 1861. He was refitting for a second attempt when the Civil War took his ship and his men. He never went back.

The open polar sea did not exist. But the water Morton and Hayes both saw does: the North Water polynya and the recurring open leads of Kennedy Channel, which is why both men, standing on opposite shores seven years apart, honestly reported an ocean.
