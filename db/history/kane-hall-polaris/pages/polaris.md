[[person:Charles Francis Hall]] finally got a ship. The *Polaris* was a naval vessel, refitted, with a Congressional appropriation behind her and a mandate to reach the North Pole. She left New York on 29 June 1871 and sailed from New London on 3 July. Aboard were [[person:Tookoolito]], [[person:Ebierbing]] and their adopted daughter [[person:Panik]]; [[person:Hans Hendrik]] joined in Greenland with [[person:Merkut]] and their children; [[person:George Emory Tyson]] came as assistant navigator, [[person:Sidney O. Buddington]] as sailing master and [[person:Emil Bessels]] as chief of the scientific corps. Hall's last dispatches went ashore at Tessiusaq, north of [[place:Upernavik]], on 24 August.

## The farthest north, and Thank God Harbour

At the end of August 1871 the *Polaris* pushed into Robeson Channel and reached what was then the highest latitude any ship had attained. **How high is genuinely unsettled, in the official record itself.** Four figures are in print: 82 deg 29 min by dead reckoning; 82 deg 26 min as Hall assigned it; 82 deg 16 min from the meteorologist Meyers's computation, which Blake's edition calls the "real latitude"; and 82 deg 11 min from a review at the Hydrographic Office, which is the figure usually cited today. All four are recorded in the timeline entry and none is preferred here.

She was driven back south and secured for the winter on the north Greenland shore in Polaris Bay, at a place Hall named [[place:Thank God Harbour]]. He dates a written order from there "latitude 81 deg 38 min north; longitude 61 deg 44 min west", 10 October 1871.

## Hall's death

On 10 October Hall left on a two-week sledge journey north with Chester, Ebierbing and Hans Hendrik. He came back on 24 October, having been unwell for three days on the trail, and was aboard at two in the afternoon.

> "He had not been in the cabin more than a half-hour when John Herron, the steward, brought him a cup of coffee. He drank it, and was immediately taken very sick. He vomited a good deal, retching violently... At 8 p.m., [Dr Bessels] announced that Captain Hall's left side was paralyzed, and that he had had an apoplectic attack."

He rallied on the 25th and 26th, was worse on the 27th, and over the next fortnight went in and out of delirium. Tyson's journal for 3 November: Hall "talks wildly — seems to think some one means to poison him," and he names two people (the names are blanked out in the printed book). Davis records that in his delirium he suspected even Morton, and "refused to take any nutriment from the hands of any one except Hannah" — that is, only from Tookoolito. He died about half past three in the morning of **8 November 1871**, aged fifty, and was buried ashore on the 11th with a copper cylinder holding the history of the expedition put into the grave. The head-board read: "TO THE MEMORY OF C. F. HALL, Late Commander of the U. S. North Polar Expedition, Died Nov. 8th, 1871: Aged 50 years."

A board convened by the Secretary of the Navy examined Dr Bessels on 26 December 1873 and concluded that Hall "died from natural causes — viz., apoplexy — and that the treatment of the case by Dr. Bessels was the best practicable under the circumstances." Nobody was charged.

The question did not stay closed. In 1968 Chauncey Loomis exhumed the body at Thank God Harbour, where the permafrost had preserved it, and tissue analysis showed a large intake of arsenic in the last weeks of Hall's life. That is twentieth-century work and is not in any of the sources used on this page; it is noted here because a reader will ask. It does not establish murder — arsenic was in the medicine chest — but it does mean the dying man's own accusation cannot simply be dismissed as delirium.

## 15 October 1872: the separation

The ship broke out in the summer of 1872 and drifted south down Smith Sound, beset. On the evening of 15 October, in a gale near [[place:Littleton Island]], she was nipped so hard that Buddington ordered the boats, the provisions and the people onto the ice against her being crushed. About ten at night the floe split.

> "Very shortly after, the ice exploded under our feet, and broke in many places, and the ship broke away in the darkness, and we lost sight of her in a moment." ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-036]])

Nineteen people were left on the ice, and fourteen went with the ship. Each party believed the other lost.

**On the floe** were Tyson; Meyers the meteorologist; Herron the steward; Jackson the cook; six seamen — Kruger, Jamka, Lindermann, Anthing, Lindquist and Johnson; and the two Inuit families: Ebierbing, Tookoolito and Panik (Puney); Hans Hendrik, Merkut and their four children [[person:Augustina Hendrik]], [[person:Tobias Hendrik]] (seven), [[person:Sophie Elisabeth Hendrik]] (four) and [[person:Charlie Polaris]], born aboard the ship on 11 August 1872 and two months old.

Hans Hendrik's own account of that night is [[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-032]]: "Here we were left, ten men, our wives and children, and the Tuluks, making nineteen in all, and having two boats, no boat remaining with the ship."

## The drift

They drifted for **198 days** and about **1,500 miles**, from Smith Sound down the whole length of Baffin Bay and Davis Strait and along the Labrador coast: [[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-035]]. Tyson's journal is the record, and it is an honest one — he says plainly on 13 March, "I have no sextant, and no means of accurately ascertaining our position," and he disputes Meyers's figures repeatedly. He gives a latitude every week or two; he gives a **longitude only twice in the entire journal**, so the drift line reconstructed here has his latitudes and mostly interpolated longitudes, marked as such. The day-by-day table is in the topic's notes.

What kept them alive was hunting. Tyson says so himself, over and over:

> "In our situation he is the 'best man,' for without him we should get little enough game, I fear." (21 November 1872, [[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-037]])

> "They do not stop for fog, cold, or wind. They understand the situation they are in, and consequently they are the only ones here I can in any measure rely on. Were it not for 'little Joe,' Esquimau though he be, many, if not all, of this party must have perished before now... We survive through God's mercy and Joe's ability as a hunter." (29 January 1873, [[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-038]])

Ebierbing built the snow huts, hunted through the dark months and shot the seals whose blubber was the party's only fuel: without oil they could not melt ice for water. Hans Hendrik hunted beside him and nearly died doing it ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-041]]). Tookoolito kept the clothing and the boots repaired, without which nobody could have gone out at all.

It was not a comfortable nineteen. The ten white men were armed; Tyson, their commander, owned neither gun nor pistol and had to borrow from Ebierbing. In December, Ebierbing brought Tyson his pistol unasked:

> "He says 'he don't like the look out of the men's eyes.' I know what he fears; he thinks they will first kill and eat Hans and family, and then he knows Hannah's, Puney's, and his turn would be next... it will go hard with any one who harms even the smallest child on this God-made raft. Hannah seems much alarmed." ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-039]])

Nothing of the kind happened. But it is worth naming who feared it and who was feeding everybody, and it is worth setting beside Henry Rink's question in 1878, when he had read all the published accounts and could not find the answer: whether the two Inuit men were the only ones on the floe who caught seals, "and, if not, how many were killed by the other ten" ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-034]]).

On the night of 11-12 March 1873 a gale broke the floe "into hundreds of pieces". By 1 April what was left would not hold them and nineteen people took to a single boat — the other had been broken up for fuel — making about twenty miles west before climbing onto another pan. On 9 April the sea washed the tent away and the women and children lived in the boat from then on. They shot a seal on 18 April that was the difference between living and starving.

> "At 5 A.M... the watch on the look-out espied a steamer coming through the fog, and the first I heard was a loud cry, 'There's a steamer! there's a steamer!'... I also started Hans off with his kyack, which he had himself proposed to do, to intercept her." ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-040]])

The sealer *Tigress*, Captain Bartlett of Conception Bay, took them off at about five in the morning of **30 April 1873**, "in latitude 53 deg 35 min N., off [[place:Grady Harbour]], Labrador". From the evening of 2 May to noon on the 4th a gale blew that would have finished them on the ice. They were landed at Bay Roberts on 8 May, at St John's on the 12th, and the USS *Frolic* brought them to Washington on 5 June. **Not one of the nineteen died**, including a two-month-old baby, a four-year-old and a seven-year-old, through an Arctic winter on drifting sea ice.

## The other fourteen

The ship, leaking and nearly out of coal, was warped in and run ashore at [[place:Life Boat Cove]] on 16 October 1872, five miles from the coast opposite Littleton Island. The fourteen aboard built a house from her timbers and wintered there, helped by the Inughuit of the district. In April they built two boats about twenty feet long out of what was left of the *Polaris* and gave a third to the Inuit. They left at one in the morning of 3 June 1873, worked south along the coast for three weeks, and on **23 June** were picked up by the Dundee-trading whaler *Ravenscraig* of Kirkcaldy, Captain William Allen, lying at 75 deg 38 min N, 65 deg 35 min W off Cape York. (Blake's narrative misprints this as 23 July; his own chronology and the official narrative both give June, and June is right.) They came home by way of Dundee.

In August 1873 the *Tigress*, sent north to look for them, found the empty winter camp at 78 deg 23 min N, 73 deg 46 min W and the *Polaris* herself sunk a mile and a half offshore. That find also settled an argument: Tyson had said the separation happened near Littleton Island and Meyers had said Northumberland Island, and Tyson was right.

## Afterwards

Tookoolito and Ebierbing went home to [[place:Groton]], Connecticut. Panik, who had crossed Baffin Bay on an ice floe as a small child, died there on 18 March 1875. Tookoolito died on 31 December 1876, aged thirty-eight. Ebierbing went back to the Arctic with Schwatka in 1878 and is thought to have died there about 1881 ([[artifact:kane-hall-polaris-027]]).

Hans Hendrik took his family home to Greenland, went north again with George Nares in 1875-76, and published his memoirs in 1878 — the only book any Inuk of these four expeditions wrote. See [[page:the-inuit-of-these-expeditions]].

Hall is still at Thank God Harbour, at about 81 deg 38 min N — among the most northerly graves in the world.
