# How this history was made

The History tab of the CCGS *Amundsen*'s dashboard is a research database
about the human history of the Canadian Arctic Archipelago and Baffin Bay,
from the first Paleo-Inuit arrivals some 4,500 years ago to the ship this
dashboard runs on: a wiki, a map layer, a timeline and a bibliography. It was
researched and written by artificial intelligence and then checked. This page
says how, so that a reader knows how far to trust a given line.

## The short version

AI agents, instances of Anthropic's Claude, did the reading and the writing.
They worked from a brief written by a person, and cited a source for every
fact they entered. A second pass of AI reviewers then checked every narrative
page, line by line, against the primary sources, and only a page a reviewer
could stand behind lost its draft mark. A person, R. Eric Collins of the
University of Manitoba, set the brief, answered every question the agents
could not settle themselves, made the judgement calls, and owns the result.

Nothing here replaces the primary sources. Every artifact carries its
citation, every quotation was checked against the text it came from, and
where the sources disagree the page says so rather than choosing silently.

## Who did what

**A person** wrote the brief, chose the topics, answered every question
referred up, made the judgement calls on permissions, sensitive framing and
what to buy or borrow, and is the named author and owner of the layer.

**The research agents** read the sources for one topic each: voyage
narratives, journals and logs, archival scans, Inuit testimony as recorded,
and modern scholarship. They entered what they found as sources, artifacts,
people, places, dated events and voyage tracks, and wrote the narrative pages
that tie them together. What they could not do alone, a download behind a
library gate, a rights holder's permission, a book held only on a shelf, a
question of how to frame something, they referred to the person and carried
on.

**The review agents** re-read every narrative page against the primary texts
held on disk: every quotation word for word, against the page image itself
where the scanned text was unreliable; every date and coordinate; every link;
the licence on every image. They corrected what was wrong before a page lost
its draft mark. A page a reviewer could not stand behind stayed a draft, with
the reason recorded.

## What the review caught

The review was not a formality. Among the errors it found and corrected
against the sources:

- **Two fabricated quotations**, passages attributed to real authors that
  appear nowhere in their work, were removed and replaced with what the source
  actually says.
- **Misattributed testimony**, including a passage of Inuit survivor testimony
  credited to the wrong person, and several expedition quotations given to the
  wrong speaker or the wrong year, was corrected against the record.
- **A claim the source never made**, a stated cause of death where the
  narrative pointedly does not say, was withdrawn.
- **Coordinates** wrong by tens to hundreds of kilometres, and **plate
  captions** misread when the images were gathered, were fixed against each
  book's own list of illustrations and against georeferenced page images.
- **A death missing from the modern record**, a stoker who died in 1853 and
  is lettered on a surveyed harbour plan, and **Inuit personal names preserved
  in oral history and in no European record**, were recovered and entered.

These are listed not to alarm but to be honest: an unreviewed AI draft
contains mistakes of exactly this kind, which is why the review exists and
why a page's draft mark is worth noticing.

## The standards the work was held to

This is a history of a region whose communities are living and whose past
includes violence, relocation and epidemics. The brief set these standards:

- **Indigenous knowledge and presence are the narrative, not an appendix.**
  Every named Inuit, Inughuit and other Indigenous participant the record
  preserves is entered as a person. Oral history is treated as evidence, cited
  and weighed like any other source.
- **Humility about "discovery."** Most of what these expeditions "discovered"
  was inhabited or long known. The pages say what happened, sighted, charted,
  reached, mapped, and keep the word "discovery" only inside a direct
  quotation, a proper name, or an explorer's own reported claim.
- **Living communities are named as they are now**, the Inuktitut name first
  and the historic English name in parentheses, and hard history is reported
  factually, in the terms the communities and the Qikiqtani Truth Commission
  use. Their testimony is quoted exactly as published, never softened or
  sharpened.
- **Disagreement is shown, not hidden.** Where sources conflict on a date, a
  position or a fact, the page presents the conflict and says which it
  follows.

## Reading the layer honestly

- **A page without a draft mark** has been checked against its sources by a
  reviewer. **A page marked draft** has been researched but not yet fully
  verified: treat its specifics with more caution.
- **Every artifact carries a credit and a source**; follow them to the
  original. Quotations are verbatim from the cited text and page.
- **A track is a reconstruction** of a route from a published narrative, log
  or the explorer's own sketch map, accurate to about ten kilometres, with
  inferred segments labelled as such. It is not a survey or a GPS record.
- **Coordinates carry the coarseness of their sources.** A wreck position may
  be the centre of a protected area rather than a surveyed point, and the
  artifact says so where that is true.

## Tooling

Everything lives in one database, written only through a tool that refuses an
artifact without a credit and a source, checks coordinates against the region,
and stores dates in a form the timeline can check. Texts and images were
fetched through the university library; scanned books without usable text
were run through optical character recognition. The bibliography can be
exported from the tab in BibTeX, RIS and CSL forms.
