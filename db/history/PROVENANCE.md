# How this history was made

The History tab is a research database about the Canadian Arctic
Archipelago and Baffin Bay in two halves: the human history, from the first
Paleo-Inuit arrivals some 4,500 years ago to the ship this dashboard runs
on, and the natural history, what the archipelago is and does: its rock and
fossils, its ice on land and at sea, the water under the ship, the sky, the
weather, the magnetic field, and the living things, together with what
every one of those people wrote down about them. Both halves were
researched and written by artificial intelligence, then checked. This page
says how, so a reader knows how far to trust a line.

## The short version

AI agents, instances of Anthropic's Claude, did the reading and the writing,
one topic each, from a brief written by a person, citing a source for every
fact. A second pass of AI reviewers checked every narrative page against the
primary sources: every quotation word for word, every date, coordinate, link
and licence. A page a reviewer could not stand behind stays marked as a
draft. A person, R. Eric Collins of the University of Manitoba, wrote the
brief, answered the fifty questions the agents referred up, and owns the
result.

## What the review caught

Two fabricated quotations, found nowhere in the work they were attributed
to. Inuit survivor testimony credited to the wrong person. A cause of death
the source never gave. Coordinates wrong by tens of kilometres. All corrected
against the sources. An unreviewed AI draft makes mistakes of exactly this
kind, which is why a draft mark is worth noticing.

## The standards

Indigenous people are the narrative, not an appendix: every named Inuit,
Inughuit and other Indigenous participant is a person in the record, and
oral history is evidence. "Discovery" appears only inside a quotation.
Communities are named as they are now, and hard history is told in the
terms the Qikiqtani Truth Commission uses. Where sources disagree, the page
says so.

## The natural history

The natural half was built the same way, one topic to an agent, twenty-one
topics from the rock to the microbes, with one hub for Inuit knowledge of
nature. Its atom is the observation: one subject, one date, one place, one
source, and the number in the unit it was written in, so Greely's Fahrenheit
stays Fahrenheit and the page does the arithmetic. The agents read the
expeditions' own appendices and registers first, then the open catalogues
and series: the museums' specimen records, the wildlife surveys, the
Geological Survey, the ice and weather services, the observatories. A
subject carries its scientific name and its names in Inuktitut, Inuinnaqtun,
Inuktun and Kalaallisut, each cited to the vocabulary or the elder it came
from; what Inuit and Inughuit knew and know of an animal, an ice, a wind or
a star is entered as testimony with the knowledge-holder named as the
observer. The modern community knowledge studies belong to the communities
that made them: the layer summarises them, cites and links them, and credits
the elders, but quotes nothing from them verbatim. The figures on these
pages were drawn by the agents from the rows they entered and say so in
their captions; a photograph of a species from a citizen-science record is
both a picture and an observation, with its photographer credited.

## Reading it honestly

Nothing here replaces the primary sources; every artifact carries a credit
and a source to follow. A track is a reconstruction from a narrative,
accurate to about ten kilometres, and coordinates are as coarse as their
sources.

## The record, in numbers

One bibliography serves both halves: about 700 works cited, of which some
50 are datasets. Some 270 narrative pages and 3,300 artifacts on the human
side; on the natural side about 155 pages, 950 subjects, 6,500
observations, of them some 760 from named Inuit and Inughuit
knowledge-holders, and 7,000 pictures and plates. The tab shows the current
figures.
