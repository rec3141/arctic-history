Five stations went into the Canadian Arctic Archipelago between 1947 and 1950.
Nobody lived at any of the five sites when they were chosen. Two of them are
still working; two are wrecks with the books still on the shelves; the fifth grew
into a military station and a village of two hundred.

## How it was decided

The idea was American. In 1945 the United States Weather Bureau argued for a
line of weather stations from Banks Island to northern Greenland: two main
stations reachable by ship, and up to eight satellites within about 800 km of
them, put in by air. Enabling legislation became American law on 12 February
1946. Talks opened in Ottawa on 18 May 1946, and Canada, which had every reason
to be careful about American installations on its own ground, took eight months
over it. Cabinet approved a Joint Arctic Programme on 28 January 1947, and on
4 March C.D. Howe announced nine Arctic weather stations within three years.

The arrangement was deliberately even: equal numbers of Canadians and Americans
at every station, a Canadian officer-in-charge and an American executive officer
at each. [[person:Charles J. Hubbard]] of the US Weather Bureau ran the American
side and picked the sites; D.C. Archibald and, above him,
[[person:Andrew Thomson]] ran the Canadian side.

## Eureka, 7 April 1947

The first was [[artifact:science-stations-004|Eureka]]. A reconnaissance flight
down the length of Eureka Sound on 25 March 1947 settled on
[[place:Eureka|Slidre Fiord]]: bold headlands at the mouth, perfectly smooth ice
in the reaches, low country and a flat valley for an airstrip. On Easter Sunday,
7 April, a C-47 flew the 360 miles from [[place:Pituffik]] (Thule) and put down
on the sea ice at 11:02. Four men got out: the air task force commander,
Hubbard, the Canadian officer-in-charge [[person:Justin Courtney]] and the
American executive officer Per Stoen. Courtney tasted the ice to make sure it was
fresh — fresh means multi-year, and multi-year means it will take a loaded
aircraft. They marked the site with a snow cairn and were back aboard in forty
minutes.

Eureka has been occupied every day since. It is the longest continuous High
Arctic record Canada has, and everything that has been built there since —
including [[artifact:science-stations-013|PEARL]] — sits on top of it. See
[[page:the-eureka-record]].

## Resolute, 30 August 1947: the station that was somewhere else

The main station was meant for Winter Harbour on [[place:Melville Island]], where
Parry wintered in 1819-20 ([[topic:ross-parry]]). It never got there.
[[artifact:science-stations-001|Task Force 68]] tried four times in August 1947:
stopped by ice near Byam Martin Island; a propeller blade broken off in Melville
Sound, halving the *Edisto*'s icebreaking power; fog; and finally a ten-mile band
of pack ice on 28 August. On 29 August Hubbard accepted Resolute Bay on
[[place:Cornwallis Island]] instead — "except that the country appeared
uncomfortably barren", as the Canadian observer put it — and unloading began the
next morning.

The accident of that choice shaped the next eighty years. Resolute became the
main station, then an RCAF base in 1949, then the airfield that supplies Alert,
Isachsen and Mould Bay, then the base of the
[[artifact:science-stations-014|Polar Continental Shelf Project]], and then, in
1953, the reason a community of Inuit from [[place:Inukjuak]] and
[[place:Mittimatalik]] was placed eight kilometres down the beach. That part of
the story is [[page:the-station-and-the-settlement]].

## Isachsen and Mould Bay, April 1948

From Resolute in the spring of 1948 reconnaissance flights picked two more sites.
[[artifact:science-stations-005|Isachsen]], at 78°47'N on
[[place:Isachsen|Ellef Ringnes Island]], was set up from 2 April by
[[person:Alan Innes-Taylor]], a Yukoner with real polar experience who wrote the
place up for the first volume of the journal *Arctic* and privately despaired of
the men he had been given. [[artifact:science-stations-006|Mould Bay]] on
[[place:Prince Patrick Island]] followed on 11 April; thirty-two flights carried
170 tons of stores in by the 25th, and seven men — three Canadians, three
Americans and a mechanic — put up a Jamesway hut to keep it out of the weather.

Both were dreadful postings and both lasted decades. Isachsen's upper-air
observations stopped in June 1978 and the station closed on 19 September that
year; nothing was flown out that could not pay for its seat, so the trucks are
still in the garage. Mould Bay got a new operations building in 1985, then sank
into the permafrost, and the Atmospheric Environment Service closed it in 1997
because shutting one Mould Bay saved the cost of twenty stations in the south.

## What they were for

Weather, first: surface observations three times a day, then balloon soundings
twice a day, radioed south to feed the forecasts that made transpolar aviation
possible. The stations soon carried more — seismographs, tide gauges, solar
radiation, ozone, and eventually the background greenhouse-gas measurements at
Alert that are still quoted in every assessment of the Arctic climate.

They were also, without anybody pretending otherwise, a way of being present.
Canada wanted Canadians on the ground wherever Americans were, and a post office
counted: [[artifact:science-stations-010|Eureka's opened on 10 August 1947]] with
a Mountie swearing in the postmaster. Yet when
[[artifact:science-stations-016|the joint programme ended on 26 August 1972]] and
the last American flag came down at Resolute, the people who have gone through
the station files and interviewed the staff found that nationality had made
almost no difference to what was measured or how. The met techs volunteered for
the same reasons in 1975 as in 1950: money, and a year away from distractions.
And from 1974, when Heather Blain and Cheryl Leyten were posted to Eureka,
[[artifact:science-stations-015|the stations stopped being all-male]].
