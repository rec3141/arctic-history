# HMS Investigator (Capt. Robert McClure), 1850–1854 — dated positions and events

Source: Sherard Osborn (ed.), *The Discovery of the North-West Passage by H.M.S.
"Investigator," Capt. R. M'Clure, 1850, 1851, 1852, 1853, 1854* (1856), from the
Google Books OCR text at
`/data/dev/arctic-history/db/history/mcclure-investigator/files/mcclure-investigator-osborn1856-mcclure.txt`.

Page numbers below are the **printed book page numbers** (as OCR'd in running
heads/footers), not file line numbers, unless marked "front matter" (roman
numerals). The OCR is a double-spaced Google scan; some numerals and letters
are garbled (e.g. "0"/"O", "3"/"8", "y"/"0"). Where a figure is uncertain I
flag it explicitly rather than silently "fixing" it.

Coordinate convention: decimal degrees, **west longitude is negative**.

## 2. Key dated events

1. **1850-07-20** — McClure writes his now-famous despatch to the Admiralty at sea (Lat. 51°26'N., Long. 172°35'W.), setting out his intended plan of action if separated from Collinson's Enterprise. (p. 42–47)
2. **1850-08-02** — First ice seen; immense herds of walrus observed near the pack edge. (p. 58–59)
3. **1850-08-06** — Point Barrow doubled; Investigator "free from all anxiety upon the score of being able to enter the Arctic Ocean from Behring's Strait." (p. 60–61)
4. **1850-08-08** — Master Mr. Court lands near Point Pitt to erect a cairn; first meeting with three Iñupiat. (p. 63)
5. **1850-08-11** — Reaches Jones' Island; visited by 24 natives in two baidars. (p. 73)
6. **1850-08-14** — Runs aground among shoals near the Mackenzie delta approach; refloated after five hours, losing eleven casks of salt meat overboard. (p. 79–80)
7. **1850-08-24** — Landing near Point Warren: initially hostile reception from Inuvialuit (bow and arrow drawn), resolved through Moravian missionary/interpreter Johann Miertsching. (p. 86–88)
8. **1850-08-31/09-07** — Cape Bathurst reached, then Cape Parry; new land sighted, and McClure lands to take possession "in the Queen's Name," naming it Baring Island and its headland Nelson Head. (p. 92–104)
9. **1850-09-11** — First unmistakable signs of the approaching winter; ship beset by ice in Prince of Wales Strait — the first wintering begins. (p. 109)
10. **1850-09-17** — Investigator reaches her first season's most advanced position, lat. 73°10'N., long. 117°10'W. — about 30 miles from Barrow's Strait. (p. 114)
11. **1850-09-27/28** — Ship nearly wrecked, swept by the pack toward the cliffs of Princess Royal Island; narrowly escapes destruction within 500 yards of the rocks — the first failed attempt to break through toward Barrow's Strait ends here. (p. 117–119)
12. **1850-09-30** — Ship becomes stationary for the winter at lat. 73°50'N., long. 117°55'W. (p. 120)
13. **1850-10-21** — Sledge party under McClure (with Mr. Court, Wynniatt, and Dr. Armstrong assisting) sets out from the wintering ship to reach Barrow's Strait and confirm the Passage. (p. 133–134)
14. **1850-10-24** — From a point of land, McClure observes the strait's shores diverging, encouraging hope of reaching Barrow's Strait; latitude fixed by the star Capella at 73°25'N. (p. 138–139)
15. **1850-10-26** — From "Mount Observation" (600 ft above the strait), McClure and his party see Barrow's/Melville Strait and, beyond it, the loom of Melville Island — the North-West Passage is visually confirmed. Camp that night at Cape Lord John Russell. (p. 140–142)
16. **1850-10-30/31** — McClure, separated from his sledge party in a snowstorm six miles from the ship, spends the night alone on the ice before finding his way back. (p. 143–146)
17. **1851-04-18** — Three sledge parties (Haswell to the S.E. toward Wollaston Land, Cresswell N.W. along Banks Land's coast, Wynniatt N.E. toward Cape Walker) leave the ship for spring search duty. (p. 166)
18. **1851-05-20** — Cresswell's party returns after 32 days, having traced 170 miles of Banks Land's coast and established that Banks Land is an island. (p. 183–184)
19. **1851-05-29** — Haswell's party returns after 47 days, having reached lat. 70°45'N., long. 114°W. on Wollaston Land — coincidentally within 40 miles of a point reached by Dr. Rae ten days later. (p. 187–188)
20. **1851-05-30 to 06-02** — McClure and Miertsching visit an Inuit encampment of five tents near the ship — first direct meeting with the local Copper Inuit/Inuvialuit population in this strait. (p. 188–191)
21. **1851-06-07** — Wynniatt's party returns, ending the spring 1851 search season. (p. 192)
22. **1851-07-10/14** — First wintering ends: ship breaks free after being fixed in one spot for nearly ten months. (p. 199–200)
23. **1851-08-16** — Ship reaches her second season's most advanced position (73°43'43"N., 115°32'30"W.), 25 miles from Barrow's Strait; ice found impassable — the second failed attempt to break through Prince of Wales Strait. McClure decides to bear up and go round the south end of Banks Land instead. (p. 202–203)
24. **1851-08-17/19** — Rapid 300-mile run up Banks Land's west coast in clear water, rounding Nelson Head and Cape Kellett. (p. 205–206)
25. **1851-09-01** — Ballast Beach named (lat. 74°25'N., long. 122°W.), where ballast was gathered in anticipation of a second wintering. (p. 216)
26. **1851-09-23** — Ship runs ashore during the night; the sheltering bay is named the Bay of Mercy, where the ship will remain for good — the second wintering begins. (p. 221–223)
27. **1852-04-11/28** — McClure's sledge journey to Winter Harbour, Melville Island, seeking a depot or relief ship; finds neither, only McClintock's 1851 notice. (p. 250–251)
28. **1852-05-15** — Sick list rises to 13 — first clear sign scurvy is taking hold despite fresh venison; rations had already been cut to two-thirds on entering the second winter. (p. 223, 253)
29. **1852-07-01** — Surgeon reports "evident symptoms of debility, with incipient scurvy, in sixteen of the men." (p. 253)
30. **1852-08-24** — Lead of water closes after a brief hope of escape; second wintering ends and the third (1852–53) begins in earnest. (p. 257–258)
31. **1852-09-01/05** — Kellett's Resolute and McClintock's Intrepid reach Melville Island and Winter Harbour, unaware the Investigator lies only ~180 miles away; they winter at Dealy Island. (p. 285)
32. **1852-09-23** — Lieut. Mecham, on an autumn depot-laying trip, discovers McClure's record on Parry's "sandstone rock" at Winter Harbour, giving the first news of the Investigator's whereabouts and the Passage's completion. (p. 286–287)
33. **1853-03-10** — Lieut. Bedford Pim and Dr. Domville leave Melville Island for Banks Land by sledge, aiming to intercept the Investigator. (p. 288)
34. **1853-04-05/06** — First death aboard ship (self-inflicted, from drinking medicine-bottle dregs); McClure addresses the crew to lift morale. **1853-04-06** — Lieut. Pim reaches the ship on foot, the first outside contact in three years — the rescue. (p. 288–291; see caveat below on the exact death date)
35. **1853-05-23** — Medical survey of the crew finds only four men fit to face another winter; the rest are ordered home — the third wintering ends with the decision to abandon ship. (p. 331–334)
36. **1853-06-03** — H.M.S. Investigator formally abandoned in the Bay of Mercy after a depot is landed and graves marked. (p. 335)
37. **1853-06-17** — Investigator's crew reach the Resolute and Intrepid at Dealy Island, walking overland from Mercy Bay. (p. 336)
38. **1853-09-09** — Resolute and Intrepid, having briefly broken free on 18 August, are permanently beset for a fourth Arctic winter, carrying the Investigators with them. (p. 338–339)
39. **1853-10-07** — Lieut. Cresswell, carried home via the "Phoenix," reports to the Admiralty the achievement of the North-West Passage and the safety of McClure's crew. (p. 342)
40. **1854-04-14** — McClure and his crew quit the Resolute and Intrepid and march on foot to Beechey Island, ending their fourth Arctic winter. (p. 342)

Caveat on the April 1853 deaths (crew-list appendix, front matter pp. xxiv–xxv):
The appendix "List of the Officers and Crew" has marginal remarks that appear
misaligned by one row in the OCR. As best I can reconstruct it: **Robert
Tiffeny, Captain of the Maintop**, is annotated "Died 6th April, 1853, Bay of
Mercy; the first death. Lieut. Pim arrived next day" — but the main narrative
(p. 289) has McClure discussing, on 6 April, the burial of "the man who died
**yesterday**" (i.e., 5 April), with Pim arriving that same day, 6 April. I
cannot fully reconcile the appendix table with the narrative and flag this
rather than pick one silently. A second entry, **John Kerr, Gunners' Mate**,
is annotated "Died 13th April, 1853, at Bay of Mercy ... on board H.M.S.
Investigator" — this one is less ambiguous in the OCR but carries the same
general risk of table misalignment.

## 3. Verbatim quotable passages

All transcribed as printed, with only obvious OCR spacing artefacts silently
closed up (the source is "double-spaced" word-by-word OCR); no wording has
been changed. Where the OCR renders a word ambiguously I say so.

1. **The moment the Passage was seen (26 Oct. 1850), Mount Observation, p. 141:**
   > "The North-west Passage was discovered! All doubt as to the water-communication between the two great oceans was removed; and it now alone remained for Captain M'Clure, his officers and men, to perfect the work by traversing the few thousand miles of known ground between them and their homes."

2. **Thunderstorm at 70°N, 15 Aug. 1850, p. 81–82 (McClure's journal, quoted):**
   > "The west wind was ushered in with rain, and thunder and lightning. The two latter I never before witnessed in such a latitude (70° N.). The thermometer rose to 45°+ Fahrenheit, and the air was quite sultry, with dark heavy clouds rising overhead, resembling those seen in a thunder-storm in our own country."

3. **Near-wreck at Princess Royal Island, 27/28 Sept. 1850, p. 119 — seamen's dialogue:**
   > "'It looks a bad job this time!' inquiringly remarked one of the sailors... 'Yes!' was the rejoinder... 'the old craft will double up like an old basket when she gets alongside of them rocks!'"

4. **Drifting into the pack, 10 Sept. 1851, p. 218–219 (McClure's diary):**
   > "Thus we launch into this formidable frozen sea. Spes mea in Deo" [My hope is in God].

5. **Naming of the Bay of Mercy, 24 Sept. 1851, p. 222–223:**
   > "the bay was appropriately called the Bay of Mercy. It was no empty expression; for every heart in that ship was filled with emotion, and many prayed that... the recollection of the bounty and goodness of Him who had upheld them... might never be effaced from their memories."

6. **Mecham finds McClure's record at Parry's Rock, Winter Harbour, 23 Sept. 1852, p. 286–287:**
   > "Lieut. Mecham could hardly credit his senses when he discovered a document upon its summit, detailing the accomplishment of the north-west passage, and the position of H.M.S. 'Investigator' in Banks Land!"

7. **State of the crew, third winter — Report of Survey, 23 May 1853, p. 332–333:**
   > "There exists in all of them at present, with one or two exceptions, well marked evidence of scurvy and debility in various stages of development, with great loss of flesh and strength... their present state of health is such as renders them utterly unfit to undergo the rigour of another winter."

8. **The moment Pim was seen walking across the ice, 6 April 1853, p. 289–290:**
   > "we perceived a figure walking rapidly towards us from the rough ice at the entrance of the bay... When within about two hundred yards of us, this strange figure threw up his arms, and made gesticulations resembling those used by Esquimaux... we saw that his face was as black as ebony."

9. **McClure's own description of meeting Pim, 6 April 1853, p. 289 (his journal, "almost verbatim"):**
   > "While walking near the ship, in conversation with the first lieutenant upon the subject of digging a grave for the man who died yesterday... we perceived a figure walking rapidly towards us from the rough ice at the entrance of the bay."
   (Note: this and passage 8 overlap in the source — McClure's journal *is* the narrative of the sighting; I've split the "approach" sentence from the "black-faced stranger" sentence to keep each under 80 words.)

10. **McClure's letter to the Admiralty, 20 July 1850, p. 47–48:**
    > "I consider that we have said adieu to the world for the next two years. May that arm which has conducted us so far in safety, still continue its protection upon a service where all else is weakness indeed!"

## What I could not confidently establish

- Several positions (Cape Bathurst, Cape Parry, Nelson Head, Mercy Bay,
  Winter Harbour, Dealy Island, Beechey Island, Point Barrow) are **not**
  given as coordinates in this text — the OCR only names the place. I have
  supplied modern real-world coordinates for these and marked them
  "inferred," per instructions, rather than inventing period figures.
- The 1851-08-19 fix ("123° 62' 3?'W") is almost certainly an OCR
  misreading; I have guessed 123°52'30"W but do not have high confidence in
  the seconds value.
- The identity and exact date of the *first* scurvy/illness death in the
  Bay of Mercy (5 vs 6 April 1853; Robert Tiffeny vs. possible row
  misalignment) cannot be fully resolved from the OCR — see caveat in
  Section 2, event 45.
- I did not find an explicit narrated passage describing McClure
  *depositing* his record at Parry's Rock (only Mecham's later discovery of
  it is narrated in detail) — quote 6 above documents the discovery, not the
  deposit, since the deposit itself is not dramatized in the source text.
