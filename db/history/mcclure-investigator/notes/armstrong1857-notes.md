## 2. Scurvy and diet

- **Full polar ration scale** (as described before reduction): "one pound of meat daily — salt beef, pork and preserved meat on alternate days, with a fair proportion of flour and preserved vegetables." Armstrong argues this flat scale (same at "the Equator or the Poles") is inadequate for a second Arctic year onward. (p. 468–469)
- Armstrong's own diagnosis of cause: as stores of body fat/reserves built up in the first season are depleted, "a larger instead of smaller quantity of the original allowance of food is necessary to generate heat... If the supply is not equal to the demand, the body wastes, and debility and disease of a scorbutic character ultimately ensue." He states flatly: "I am...of opinion that one pound of animal food daily is not sufficient for men employed on Polar service." (p. 469)
- He also blames **salt meat** specifically: "Salt meat should be proscribed from an Arctic dietary...calculated to promote...impairment of the vital energy...and the consequent supervention of scurvy and debility." (p. 469)
- Officers, he notes, stayed healthier than the men on the same nominal ration because of a "larger and more varied quantity of food" from their private mess stock, and better ventilated quarters — "they were the last to manifest any symptoms of scurvy." (p. 469)
- **1851-10-01**: two-thirds allowance of provisions ordered at the start of the second winter, owing to beef losses on first entering the ice and a depot left behind. (p. 467–468)
- **1852-09-06**: further reduction — vegetables to 2½ oz/day, meat to 8 oz gross (~6 oz net after bone/jelly), flour 10 oz, tea/cocoa/sugar in "fractional parts of an ounce," lime juice cut to half; "no extra food was allowed for the sick under any circumstances — the same scale of diet being ordered for all." (p. 545)
- Consequence of the small, quickly-shrinking cooked portions: officers and men alike took to **eating salt beef and pork raw**, and preserved meat cold or half-frozen — "eaten and enjoyed with a degree of avidity and relish which must be experienced to be fully understood." Armstrong reported this to McClure as likely to worsen scurvy, "but it went on uninterruptedly." (p. 546)
- Officers' mess ate whatever "had life": seals, foxes, and lemmings/field mice were "always a most welcome addition to our fare" once rations ran short. (p. 548)
- Hunting successes directly relieved the sick list: after a bad stretch with "no Deer from this date until the last day in October," a run of luck in November let them issue **venison three times a fortnight** in lieu of ship's rations; hares and ptarmigan were specifically set aside for the sick. (p. 546)
- **1852-02-01** monthly inspection: first time Armstrong records visible loss of flesh/strength after four months of reduced diet, though general health "otherwise good." By **1852-07-01** the sick list is "daily increasing" and the "scorbutic taint" becoming general enough that he formally pushes for "a more liberal scale of diet, and...a larger proportion of vegetable food." (p. 500, 531)
- **1852, mid-July**: discovery of wild sorrel ("Scurvy Grass being scarce") let each man have a small daily antiscorbutic allowance; by 1 August Armstrong could "clearly discern an improvement in the general appearance of the men." (p. 539)
- **1852-12-31 / 1853 Jan–Mar**: sick-bay "full of occupants," much of the lower deck given over to hammocks to keep the sick warm; dysentery and other exhausting diseases general; "scurvy of an aggravated character seldom failed to supervene" after even a short illness. (p. 553, 556)
- **Disagreement with McClure over the 1853 evacuation plan** (closest the text comes to "staying a third winter," since by the time the plan was announced a third winter was already unavoidable — the argument was over whether to march out half-starved men rather than wait): Armstrong writes that on hearing McClure's plan to send half the debilitated crew overland to Cape Spencer/Baffin's Bay and Mackenzie River, "I could only view this contemplated hazardous undertaking in the most serious light, from my knowledge of the debilitated condition of the men. I could arrive at no other conclusion than that they were utterly unfit for the performance of the service." He says he had "on many former occasions" already told McClure the state of the men, raised it again, and "it had no result." (p. 543–544)
- He is explicit that the deaths of April 1853 were preventable given resources: "our losses by death were entirely owing to the want of them [lime juice and extra food]." (p. 566)

## 3. Verbatim quotable passages

All transcribed as printed, spacing normalized (OCR double-spaces collapsed to single, line-end hyphens rejoined); letter-level scanner errors left as-is.

1. **First meeting with the Esquimaux, Point Drew, 8 Aug 1850** (p. 96):
   > "We at once landed, and having called out to them words of peace in their own language, we approached; they timidly met us, and in a few minutes we were rubbing noses — the customary mode of friendly salutation with them."

2. **Ration reduction, 6 Sept 1852** (p. 545):
   > "the average weight did not exceed six ounces, which with ten ounces of flour constituted the allowance on which we had lived for the previous twelve months."

3. **Armstrong's disagreement with McClure over the evacuation plan, 8 Sept 1852** (p. 544):
   > "I could only view this contemplated hazardous undertaking in the most serious light...I could arrive at no other conclusion than that they were utterly unfit for the performance of the service."

4. **Condition of the crew, early 1853** (p. 556):
   > "Dysentery, and other exhausting diseases were very general amongst the men...A few hours illness would produce the most alarming degree of prostration I have ever witnessed...and scurvy of an aggravated character seldom failed to supervene."

5. **The sighting of Pim, 6 April 1853** (p. 560):
   > "Two officers were returning across the ice from superintending the work, when a figure was observed approaching from the rough ice to seaward. They at first supposed him to be one of our men, but as he came towards them, he proved to be a stranger."

6. **Death of John Boyle, night of 5 April 1853** (p. 560):
   > "Death for the first time made its appearance amongst us, and removed from this world, John Boyle, (A.B.), after a short illness, from the exhausting effects of dysentery, acting on a scorbutic habit...He had spoken cheerfully a few minutes before death, but on making a slight exertion in his bed, sudden syncope ensued, and he died without a struggle."

7. **Deaths of Ames and Kerr, 11–12 April 1853** (p. 566):
   > "The second death occurred on the 11th — that of John Ames, from dropsy supervening on an affection of the heart of only nine days duration...and the third, and last, on the following day, was that of John Kerr, gunner's mate."

8. **Armstrong's diet philosophy** (p. 469):
   > "If the supply is not equal to the demand, the body wastes, and debility and disease of a scorbutic character ultimately ensue. I am, therefore, of opinion that one pound of animal food daily is not sufficient for men employed on Polar service."

9. **Sledge-hauling to Dealy Island, June 1853** (p. 578):
   > "It is impossible to convey a truthful idea of the labour of dragging a sledge over rough ice; at times it can only be moved a few feet, until it be again arrested by a ragged or almost precipitous wall of rough, broken up floe ice."

10. **Homecoming, 6 October 1854** (p. 595):
    > "We landed off Ramsgate on the 6th of October, 1854, after an absence of four years and ten months."

## 4. Named people

- **Robert M'Clure** — Captain, commander of HMS *Investigator*. Announced the ration reductions and the 1852 evacuation plan; overruled Armstrong's objections; led sledge party to Melville Island 1853; commanded the ship until abandonment 3 June 1853.
- **Alexander Armstrong** — the author; Surgeon and Naturalist, R.N. Ran monthly medical inspections, argued repeatedly for larger rations, conducted the joint Survey of 22 May 1853, commanded the 4th sledge on the march to Dealy Island.
- **Mr. Piers** — Assistant Surgeon. Co-signed Armstrong's written objection to the 1853 march (p. 443); helped rescue and treat a man found near-dead of exposure (probably Wynniatt) on a sledge journey; part of the Cape Spencer party told off in 1853.
- **Lieutenant Cresswell** — senior Lieutenant. Led sledge journeys (reached "turning point" 14 May 1851); dispatched with a boat party in May 1851; given command of the Cape Spencer/Baffin's Bay relief party in 1853.
- **Lieutenant Haswell** — second Lieutenant. Ordered to survey along the south-eastern coast; commanded the 2nd sledge (with Mr. Paine) on the walk to Dealy Island.
- **Mr. Court** — Second Master. Frequent boat/shore party leader (e.g. accompanied Armstrong to Point Drew); commanded the 3rd sledge to Dealy Island; shot a wolf/had wolf encounters in spring 1853.
- **Mr. Wynniatt** — Mate. Led detachments and sledge work; nearly died of exposure and frostbite on a journey (found with barely perceptible pulse, revived by his party, escorted back under Piers's care); one of the officers sent with Cresswell's party in 1853.
- **Mr. Sainsbury** — Mate. Fell ill; sent back with the *Resolute*'s surgeon's party on 24 May 1853 as he had "rallied considerably."
- **Mr. Paine** (Joseph C. Paine) — Paymaster. Divided/issued the reduced daily rations in the Gun-room by lot; commanded the 2nd sledge jointly with Haswell.
- **Mr. Ford** — Carpenter. Accompanied Piers with a rescue sledge for the exposure casualty.
- **William Newton** — crew member, mentioned once with a creditable act noted by Armstrong (detail not fully legible).
- **Sergeant John Woon, R.M.** — Royal Marine. Rescued Charles Anderson (9 Feb 1852) by dragging him back to the ship over several miles after Anderson collapsed with hemorrhage from exposure; also shot a wolf in March 1853. Armstrong's footnote calls him "the patriarch and Mentor of our crew."
- **Charles Anderson** — Able Seaman (per Armstrong's footnote, "a man of colour," said he was Canadian, "inured to a hunter's life in North America"); the man Woon rescued.
- **Henry May** — Quartermaster of the watch. Armstrong's footnote: "a fine old fellow," "the patriarch and Mentor of our crew," a veteran of former Arctic expeditions; later served again with Armstrong on HMS *Cornwallis*.
- **John Boyle**, Able Seaman — first death, night of 5 April 1853, dysentery on a scorbutic habit.
- **John Ames**, Able Seaman — second death, 11 April 1853, dropsy/heart affection on a scorbutic habit.
- **John Kerr** — gunner's mate; third death, 12 April 1853, general dropsy after long scurvy and debility.
- **Thomas Morgan**, Able Seaman — fifth and last death, 22 May 1854, aboard HMS *North Star* at Beechey Island, "from the effects of his previous sufferings" (per ship's memorial roll: scurvy and scrofula). Armstrong: "a most excellent man, and his loss was much and universally deplored."
- (A fourth death is recorded in the ship's memorial list at the front of the book — a crew member who died of consumption aboard HMS *Resolute* off Cape Cockburn, 14 Nov 1853 — but the OCR of that table is too garbled to reliably attach a name to the entry; not otherwise mentioned in the body text.)
- **Lieutenant Bedford Pim**, HMS *Resolute* — arrived 6 April 1853 after a 28-day sledge journey from Melville Island, "the earliest Polar travelling on record"; brought news of rescue; later returned to *Investigator* and accompanied the crew's final march.
- **Captain Kellett**, HMS *Resolute* — senior officer at Dealy Island/Melville Island; ordered the medical Survey of 22 May 1853 and the eventual abandonment of *Investigator*; dispatched Pim on 10 March 1853 on finding McClure's 1852 record at Winter Harbour.
- **"The Surgeon of the *Resolute*"** — sent by Kellett to co-conduct the Survey with Armstrong; not named in the OCR text.
- **Lieutenant M'Clintock** — of Captain Austin's expedition; his 6 June 1851 record, found in a tin case at "Parry's Rock" (Winter Harbour), told McClure's men that Austin's expedition had wintered between Cornwallis and Griffith's Islands.
- **Captain Collinson** — commander of consort ship HMS *Enterprise*; his intention to take the *Plover* into the ice if *Investigator* was late reaching Behring's Strait spurred the crew's urgency in the Pacific leg.
- **Mr. Rae** (John Rae) — mentioned as arriving in the same general area roughly ten days after Cresswell's party; his journey along the coast is referenced as corroborating evidence for the geography.
- **John A. Miertsching** — "Esquimaux Interpreter," per the ship's company list at the front of the book (OCR renders the surname "If eirtKdung"). In the body text he is almost always referred to only as "the interpreter," present at nearly every native encounter (Point Drew, Point Warren/Cape Bathurst, etc.), doing the actual language work; the one occasion he is named directly in-line is not clearly legible in this OCR pass.
- **Aitawa** (also spelled "Attawaa" at its second occurrence) — an Esquimaux chief met near Point Warren/mainland coast in mid-August 1850, entrusted with a letter for Russian traders and given a white ensign as a gift; described as "a fine specimen of his race, above the average stature and generally intelligent."
- An unnamed old chief and his family (an "elder of three men," his two sons and principal wife) near Cape Bathurst told Armstrong and McClure of a European murdered near Point Warren, evidenced by an English button stamped "London" worn as an earring.
