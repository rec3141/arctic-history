# Johann August Miertsching, *Reise-Tagebuch* (Gnadau, 1855) — meetings with Inuit

Source: `/data/dev/arctic-history/db/history/mcclure-investigator/files/mcclure-investigator-miertsching1855-de.txt`
(OCR of the 1855 Gnadau first edition, ~496 KB / 13,399 lines, set in Fraktur).
Miertsching was the Moravian missionary-interpreter (fluent in Labrador Inuktitut)
carried aboard HMS *Investigator* (Capt. Robert McClure) 1850–1854 specifically so
the expedition could talk to Inuit. This file covers **only** what his own diary
records about meetings with Inuit/Inuinnait ("Eskimos" in his usage).

**On the OCR.** This is Fraktur OCR and it is rough: long ſ frequently prints as
"f", "ss" as "ß", umlauts are dropped or trail as "aͤ"/"uͤ", words break across
lines with "⸗", and — critically for this file — the scan appears to interleave
two overlapping readings of the same column of type, so many passages repeat
themselves a few lines later in garbled, fragmentary form. I have transcribed
from the first clean pass of each passage and ignored the fragment echoes.
Personal names in particular are short, isolated strings with no surrounding
sentence context to sanity-check them against, so treat every name below as
"the OCR prints it this way" rather than as verified philology.

**On page numbers.** The book prints a running page number as "— NN —" on its
own line. Many of these are themselves misread (digit swaps such as 3/8, 1/7,
0/3/9 are common, and a run of three-digit numbers past p.100 sometimes loses
its leading "1"). I built a monotonic sequence from the ~80 markers that fit a
plausible pages-per-line rate for this book (~50–65 lines/page) and discarded
the rest as garbled; every page number below is either an exact marker (bare
number) or interpolated between the two nearest good markers (prefixed "~").
None is invented. Independent cross-check: Armstrong's English narrative of the
same voyage (`mcclure-investigator-armstrong1857-notes.md` in this folder)
dates the first Esquimaux meeting to 1850-08-08 at "Point Drew" and names the
Point Warren chief "Aitawa"/"Attawaa" — matching Miertsching's 8 August date and
his "Attua" exactly, which gives confidence in the diary dates used here.

| 1851-06-03 | a camp of 5 tents reached after a sledge journey from the ship | Prince Albert Land (Victoria I.), inland from Prince of Wales Strait | a Copper Inuit band, unnamed individuals | Terrified at first sight of strangers ("sivoravogut! erksidlarpogut! — we are afraid!"), the group had never seen an outsider and could not fight even had they wished — no weapons at hand. On a sealskin spread with paper, Miertsching had them draw the coast, correctly extending it to Point Parry and marking Sutton and Liston Islands in Dolphin and Union Strait, and naming further points and the families living on them (names not given in the text). ## 3. What the Inuit told them

- **Coastal geography, Alaska.** The Kogarmiut said the coast was inhabited
  "from Nulka to the river" (they named the river variously Konamut, Yukatan,
  or Collvile). ~p.28, 1850-08-08.
- **Trade network, Alaska.** The Kogarmiut traded with Russian traders "at the
  great river" (Yukon); Attua's people traded at a seasonal meeting place
  called **Nuniwokingak**, and knew of a neighbouring group, the
  **Nunatarmiuts**, by name only. ~p.30, 1850-08-11.
- **Earlier "strangers" story (Point Warren).** Kairoluak's people told of
  boatless "tujormiuts"/"kaimaracjet" who built a driftwood house on a nearby
  point, drove the game away by their presence, starved, and died one by one;
  Kairoluak buried the last. Miertsching himself doubted this described real
  Europeans and guessed it was an old story "descended from a fight with
  Indians." No trace of European occupation was found at the site the next
  day. ~pp.35–37, 1850-08-24/25.
- **Trade partners, Mackenzie/Cape Bathurst coast.** The Cape Bathurst people
  traded with inland **Locheaux (Hare) Indians**, who understood the Eskimo
  language and carried goods on to Fort Good Hope on the Mackenzie. ~p.40,
  1850-08-31.
- **Earlier European contact, Cape Bathurst.** One man described, correctly
  in physical detail (boats, clothing, the fat captain's pacing), a party of
  "Kablunats" he said had visited "the day before yesterday" — McClure
  identified this as really referring to Dr. Richardson's Mackenzie-to-
  Coppermine-River boat expedition of roughly two years earlier (i.e. 1848).
  A woman separately said she had seen Europeans with masted wood-and-hide
  boats who gave out knives and needles "three summers ago"; another woman
  backed her account, though McClure suspected both stories were told partly
  to obtain gifts. ~pp.44–45, 1850-09-01.
- **Place names, Cape Bathurst area.** Cape Bathurst = **Nuvuak**; the
  nearest island = **Akkunek**, the second = **Tuppelioſak**; the water
  between cape and first island = **Koruk**. ~p.43, 1850-08-31.
- **Franklin.** Both Attua's people (Colville R., 11 Aug.) and Kenalualik's
  people (Cape Bathurst, 30–31 Aug.) were asked and knew nothing of Franklin
  or his ships. ~pp.30, 40.
- **Copper.** The Prince Albert Land (Copper Inuit) band's harpoons, knives,
  axes, arrowheads and needles were all worked from native copper, obtained
  by trade from Eskimos living further east, paid for with seal oil and
  narwhal horn. ~p.84, 1851-06-03.
- **Caribou and muskox.** Three men returning from hunting inland reported
  herds of **Tuktut** (caribou) and **Umingmak** (muskox), too shy to
  approach closely; the people hunt them with bow and throwing-spear. ~p.85,
  1851-06-03.
- **Geography south/east of Prince of Wales Strait.** The Prince Albert Land
  band drew the coastline on paper laid over a sealskin, extending it
  correctly to Point Parry (Victoria Land) and correctly marking Sutton and
  Liston Islands in Dolphin and Union Strait; they said many Eskimos lived
  along that southeastern coast and named further points and the families
  living there, though Miertsching does not record those names in the text.
  They also spoke of a large southern land visible in clear weather, which
  they called **"nunavaksuaraluk,"** probably the mainland; and of a high
  inland mountain where their ancestors had once lived, in tents, during "a
  very great water" (a flood tradition). ~p.84, 1851-06-03.
- **Belief that they were the only people alive.** The Prince Albert Land
  band were astonished to learn other inhabited lands existed; "they believed
  they were the only people." ~p.84, 1851-06-03.
- **Violence (secondhand, not Miertsching's own encounter).** Recounted to
  Miertsching in June 1853 via Lt. Mecham's sledge report: before Capt.
  Collinson's *Enterprise* reached the Mackenzie in 1851, a lieutenant and a
  sailor of that ship were murdered by Eskimos; at Cape Bathurst Collinson
  separately learned from the local people which direction *Investigator*
  had sailed the year before. ~p.176, reported June 1853.
- **Violence/Franklin relics (secondhand, not Miertsching's own encounter,
  from Dr. Rae's separate expedition).** The book's closing chapter reports
  that Dr. John Rae, returning from Repulse Bay, had bought various items
  (watches, silver spoons and forks, telescopes) from "a tribe of heathen
  Eskimos" who told him that in 1850 a party of Europeans had starved to
  death near King William Land while travelling by sledge and boat, and that
  a number of unburied bodies lay on a headland. Miertsching flags this as
  unconfirmed, pending the expedition due to be sent out in 1855. ~p.192.

## Notes on reliability

- All dates in section 1 are taken directly from the diary's own day-of-week +
  calendar-date headings, cross-checked for internal weekday consistency
  (1850-08-01 = Thursday, confirmed against the diary's own "Sonntag den 4.
  August" etc.); the single exception is 1850-08-08, whose heading OCRs as
  "Donnerſtag den 7. Auguſt" — Thursday fell on the 8th that year, so I have
  used 8 August and flagged the discrepancy. This also matches Armstrong's
  independent English account of the same voyage, which dates the same
  meeting to 8 August.
- No personal name, date, or page number here was invented; where a string
  is doubtful I have said so in place (see items 5 and 8 in section 4, and
  the "(unnamed)" rows in section 2).
- I was not able to identify any additional named individuals beyond the five
  listed (Attua, Kairoluak, Kunatsiak, Kenalik, Kenalualik) after checking
  every "Eskimo"/"Eskimos"/"Eingeborene"/"Häuptling" occurrence in the file
  (118 + 8 hits respectively) and every "hieß"/"nannten sich" construction.
- Group/ethnonym names that are **not** individuals but appear in the text:
  Kogarmiut, Nunatarmiuts, tujormiuts/kaimaracjet ("strangers," possibly not
  a real ethnonym), Locheaux/Hare Indians.
