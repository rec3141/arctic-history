## Quotable passages

**On discovering new land (line 62120):**
"The new land has several curious shaped hills, and with a sloping country; the coast we are now upon turns sharp to the north-eastward. Could see the sledges entangled among most intricate and heavy hummocks, and with no appearance of getting a better road until round this cape."

**On snow-blindness (line 61156):**
"Owing to the intense glare to-day, several men are complaining of sore eyes. Gave them wine of opium. Although a bright sun is shining, the cold, after pitching, became most intense."

**On difficult terrain (line 61702):**
"The floe here is old and hillocky, with smooth glassy ice, quite bare with snow. The sledges ran at a tremendous pace over it, requiring the greatest difficulty in managing them, as the slippery surface prevented us standing, and frequently the sledge dragged the whole crew after it upon their hands and knees."

**On wildlife observations (line 61722):**
"Observed great numbers of musk oxen; got within shot of one herd and wounded a cow. The manoeuvres of these animals were most interesting; their movements were conducted in herds of about 20, and with the greatest precision."

**On supplies and provisions (line 61137):**
"Arranged 11 days for Mr. Nares' use, and 12 days' depot for him to bring up after leaving me. Placed all the remainder on the sledge, which completed each of us to 45 days of every article, besides a little extra fuel and tea."

**On men's achievements (line 62148):**
"It has certainly, under the circumstances, been the best day's travelling I have ever seen. They also seemed well pleased with their day's work. Firstly, that it was almost impossible for seven men with care to break one of our sledges."

## Quotable Passages

1. (Lines 86-95) "the snow flew past in dense clouds of drift; the temperature was also very low. This short march was all up hill, and we are now upon the "stony plain." It is blowing a hard northerly gale with clear sky. In a more genial climate this would be considered a fine day, although a windy one, but here, all surrounding objects beyond 20 or 30 yards are obscured by the drifting snow, which powders one over in less than a minute"

2. (Lines 93-95) "which powders one over in less than a minute with a more perfect envelope of white than any miller can boast of. The temperature in the tent was 6° below zero, so that we did not feel very comfortable or secure from frostbites."

3. (Lines 121-123) "Our sleeping bags and furs are very wet, the snow-drift having penetrated from without, and the condensed vapour from our provisions, our breath, and the evaporation from our bodies, from within"

4. (Lines 512-514) "The glassy ice passed over to day was most beautifully transparent and blue; through it the sandy bottom could be distinctly seen, and the few small tufts of sea-weed frozen in it at different depths looked exactly like the mosses in agate."

5. (Lines 1622-1626) "The view of this haven and the neighbouring land forms by far the most beautiful Arctic scenery I have ever beheld. The visible extreme of Melville Island is a bold terraced headland 750 feet high, and very much resembling Cape Dundas."

6. (Lines 1315-1320) "This part of Melville Island is very beautiful dark land, not much covered with snow; found grass, saxifrages, and poppy, but hardly any moss. Saw several large lumps of gneiss, one very large one had the rounded form of a boulder; it was 40 or 50 feet above the sea"

## Quotable passages

**On deep snow and slow progress (line 70556):**
"The snow as last night and the night before, that is, with a crusted surface, but deep and soft beneath; so that we get along with great difficulty, and are often stopped altogether, until a series of standing pulls get the sledge in motion again."

**On finding fresh water in sea ice (line 71032):**
"We obtained some fresh water out of the holes in a mass of sea ice. These holes are formed by minute earthy particles blown off shore and collected in hollows of the ice, which they rapidly thaw deeper by absorbing heat from the sun."

**On tough old bull ox meat (line 70663):**
"That vicious old bull which I shot last is the toughest animal that I have ever attempted to eat; the process of mastication is lengthened out almost beyond the limits of one's patience, my teeth ache regularly for half an hour after each fresh meat meal."

**On needle-ice and frozen feet (line 72340):**
"Although perspiring profusely as we laboured along plunging knee deep through water and snow, in tolerably equal proportions, yet my feet were most painfully cold; had they but been numbed I should not have felt the sharp points of the partially thawed ice (technically 'needle-ice') over which we were occasionally obliged to pass."

**On abundance of game and pemmican management (line 71076):**
"It is quite wonderful how so large an animal could support life in such an extremely barren country. This abundance of fresh meat has saved our pemmican and therefore enables us to persevere longer in the search; it keeps up the strength of the men."

**On the monotony of ice travel (line 71268):**
"Our journeying has become most monotonous, the land is uniformly low in the extreme, and covered with snow; there are no traces of animals, and an ever clouded sky. We have nothing to look at but a tiresome expanse of snow, even a good large hummock would be welcomed as an old acquaintance."

## Key Facts

**Medical mission and personnel encountered:**
Domville was sent to conduct a medical survey of the *Investigator*'s crew and bring back invalids. He met Commander M'Clure at the outset (lines 25–26, 93–94) and Dr. Armstrong on board the *Investigator* (line 199), with whom he conducted a formal survey.

On 24 May, Domville took charge of three invalids from the *Investigator* for return: Mr. Sainsbury (mate, with pulmonary disease and "great debility and bodily prostration"), Thomas Morgan (A.B.), and Ellis Griffiths (A.B.). Sainsbury, suffering scrofulous conditions, was unable to leave the sledge for the entire return journey (lines 260–263, 291–292, 356).

**Scurvy crisis:**
Domville found "the great evil of scurvy pervading five-sixths" of the *Investigator*'s crew (line 209). He observed that the crew's diet—pork eaten raw and beef only slightly cooked—replicated the diet of early navigators, which "proved the bane of all our early navigators." Game had become scarcer, and without supplemental fresh food and proper dietary measures, the scorbutic condition could not be alleviated (lines 209–220). He concluded that prolonged detention on Banks Land posed fatal risk to the crew's survival (lines 209–243).

**Return to *Resolute*:**
Domville reached the ship *Resolute* at Dealy Island in three marches after leaving Melville Island on 2 June, with his truncated sledge and reduced party of eleven (lines 361–362).

## The Abandoned Investigator

**Arrival and Initial Observations** (5–6 May, lines 1007–1049, 92917–92958)

Krabbe arrived at the Investigator on the evening of 5 May at 10.30 p.m. (line 1030, 92940). He could see the stacked spars from 4' or 5' away with his naked eye, but not the cairn until within a mile. The ensign and pendant were still flying but in wretched condition: a few remnants of the ensign remained on the head, and only 18 inches of pendant was left, level with the top-gallant-mast head, the rest wound around the mizzen mast. There was large accumulation of drift on the starboard (northern) side, allowing the crew to walk in over her gunwale in the waist. On the port side there was none, only sludgy ice. Under the pinnace there was no drift, but a long ridge as high as the gunwale lay fore-and-aft in each waist. A solitary fox track was on deck. The inner edge of the old floe was within 0.5 miles, leaving smooth floe about the ship and beach (line 1030–1032, 92940–92942).

**State of the Ship** (6 May, lines 1050–1074, 92960–92983)

Opening the fore-hatchway, Krabbe found everything in good shipshape order with very little frost on the lower deck except at hatchways. However, the orlop deck overhead had a great deal of frost. On examining store-rooms and holds, he found the ship had leaked so much during the preceding summer that she was full of solid ice to the level of the orlop deck forward, the tops of tanks in the main hold, and to within 10 inches of the orlop beams abaft (lines 1054–1056, 92964–92966). This decided him to clear her of all useful stores per Captain Kellett's orders.

He brought his crew aboard and gave them a mess forward, taking the captain's cabin for himself. Fires were set in the cabin and galley using coal from the shore, since that in the ship was frozen in (lines 1061–1064, 92971–92974). The self-registering thermometer in the cabin gave from zero to +55°F (line 1071–1072, 92981–92982).

In the cabin lockers he found: ale in bottles frozen and broken; port frozen but not burst; sherry a thick sludge and very sour (lines 1075–1077, 92985–92987).

**Work on the Investigator** (6–10 May, lines 1083–1233, 93011–93143)

On 6 May, after spreading gear to dry and overhaul holds, the men used hammocks and bedding found stowed on lower deck, though they suffered cramp from damp bedding.

On 7 May, provisions were landed about 15 feet inland of those left by Investigator's crew, which were visible above snow in very good spot. Krabbe took angles to ascertain distance of ship and visited the cairn, finding cylinders duly secured to staff and cairn in good order, evidently untouched since ship's abandonment (lines 1110–1114, 93021–93024). The ship's head was N. 30° W. (true); she was heeling about 10° to starboard and a little by the head (lines 1140–1141, 93050–93051).

On 8 May, 1,550 lbs of meat was landed. When opening a case of the doctor's in the main hold for four gallons of spirits of wine, the jar was found properly secured and upright, but empty. Krabbe personally packed medicines as per list sent, all of which were brought except one small powder he could not find. Thermometers were packed with them, all weighing 50 lbs (lines 1168–1174, 93078–93083).

On 9 May, the bread-room was cleared of all bread and 18.5 bags were stowed in the library. Packing 16 days' provisions for sledge, making a sail for ditto, and cutting a hole under the stern for soundings were completed. On sounding at 7.20 p.m., 11 fathoms were found; Krabbe presumed the ship had dragged a little S.E.-ward as the cable was hanging slack under bow (lines 1195–1198, 93105–93108).

On 10 May, at 4.30 supper time, records from Captain Kellett and from Krabbe himself (in gutta-percha and tin cylinder) were deposited and secured to the staff on the cairn. A board was put on the skids over the capstan, stating the time of the visit (lines 1232–1236, 93143–93146).

On 11 May, after packing the sledge and limiting each man's knapsack to an extra 5 lbs weight, at 5.0 p.m. breakfast was had; fires and lights were inspected and seen out, ship clean and stowed as before. Hatches were seen in precisely the same manner (lines 1307–1309, 93217–93219).

**Departure from the Investigator** (11 May, lines 1310–1320, 93220–93229)

At 5.40 p.m. on 11 May, Krabbe proceeded out of the bay. He crossed in the first two hours the heavy old floe and got on good ice on the eastern side of the bay. At Point Back at midnight they lunched and saw the cask deposited with record by the Investigator, lying on its bilge and firmly frozen to the ground. Krabbe learned subsequently it had been placed on its head, filled with stones, and stones heaped round it; no doubt it had been removed by a bear or other animal (lines 1315–1319, 93225–93229).

**General Observations**

No records were found aboard. Lieutenant Haswell's journal was the only one Krabbe could find (line 1156–1157, 93066–93067). The provisions found frozen in the holds included: 3,300 lbs of preserved meat in both rooms according to accounts, all wine and spirits, and a cwt. tin of potatoes still frozen in the spirit room (lines 1164–1166, 93074–93076).

**Men's Health**

Krabbe noted (lines 1964–1969, 93874–93879) that throughout the whole trip the men worked well and heartily and gave him great satisfaction. ## Quotable Passages

**1. On the Ice Condition and Difficulty** (22 April, lines 557–564, 92467–92474)
"Passing mostly amongst very old floes, the angular parts of the hummocks being entirely gone, and with the protuberances caused by the annual rising of the floe; being large glassy mounds from 2 to 10 feet high, having somewhat the appearance (on a gigantic scale) of the old 'bull's-eye' as it formerly lay in decks, with the convex side upwards. Exclusive of these there were undulations in the floe level of 4 to 5 feet."

**2. On Finding Coal** (3 May, lines 918–927, 92829–92837)
"I entered a ravine, and immediately found in its bottom, as at Melville Island, abundance of pieces of it from 18 to 6 inches square, but somewhat thin; a good deal water-washed, but still some good pieces amongst it. It burns well, however, and in the tent I could easily set a piece on fire, when in my hand, with a lucifer match, before it had finished burning."

**3. On the Banks Land Cliffs** (3 May, lines 929–945, 92839–92854)
"A grander range of cliffs I never remember having seen. This was the highest part, and they rise here to an elevation of at least 700 feet; they incline backwards as they ascend to an angle of 15° from the perpendicular, leaving like turrets seven or eight rows of square towers, or blocks of sandstone harder than the rest, many of which are 20 to 30 feet in height."

**4. On Arrival at the Investigator** (5 May, lines 1038–1049, 92948–92958)
"The stacked spars on the beach I could see plainly with my naked eye at 4' or 5', but the cairn not until I was within a mile of it. The ensign and pendant were still flying, but in a wretched condition; a few remnants of the former remained on the head, whilst some 18 inches only of the latter was left, and that level with the top-gallant-mast head, the remains of it being around the mizen mast."

**5. On the Ship's Damage** (6 May, lines 1054–1057, 92964–92966)
"The ship had leaked so much during the preceding summer, that she was full of solid ice to the level of the orlop deck forward, the tops of tanks in main hold, and to within 10 inches of the orlop beams abaft."

**6. On the Men's Character** (13 June, lines 1964–1969, 93874–93879)
"I am happy to be able to state that throughout the whole of this trip the men worked well and heartily and gave me great satisfaction. Although many poor fellows suffered severely from that scourge of Arctic travellers, snow-blindness, and one very seriously from rheumatism in chest and legs, none would give in until literally driven to it. Their conduct whilst on board the 'Investigator' deserves the greatest praise."
