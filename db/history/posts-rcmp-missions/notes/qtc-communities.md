### Caveats before using this file

- **The QTC reports are scoped to 1950–1975.** Pre-1950 material appears only in
  the short "Early Contacts" and "Changing Patterns of Life" sections of each
  community history. Foundation dates are given tersely and sometimes
  inconsistently between a report's own introduction and its body.
- **Page numbers** below are read off the running heads in the OCR text
  (`Pond Inlet | 17`, `18 | Qikiqtani Truth Commission…`). They are the printed
  page numbers of the standalone community booklets.
- **Every report carries an Errata page** correcting its own text. Where an
  erratum touches a date or a fact used here it is quoted.
- **OCR is raw.** Quotations below are transcribed as they appear; a couple of
  obvious OCR artefacts are marked `[sic]`. Check against the PDF before
  publishing a quotation.
- `qtc-arctic_bay.pdf` and `qtc-kinngait.pdf` in staging are **not PDFs** — both
  are 19,362-byte HTML error pages from a failed download. Nothing was extracted
  from them.

| Igarjuaq | — | site of the 1903 Scottish whaling/shore station |

### Establishment dates

Summary paragraph, **p. 10**:

> "Trading establishments have only been here since 1903, when Scottish
> entrepreneurs set up a small whaling station at Igarjuaq. Over the next twenty
> years, Qallunaat managers opened a number of small stations scattered around
> this region. The Hudson's Bay Company (HBC) settled at the present site in 1921
> and bought out all its rivals by 1923. The RCMP post dates to 1922 and two
> missions (Roman Catholic and Anglican) came in 1929."

- **1903** — shore whaling station at Igarjuaq, set up by Capt. **James Mutch**
  ("Jimi Maasi to the Tununirmiut"), a Dundee whaler (p. 16). Mutch imported two
  Inuit whaling crews from Cumberland Sound; "They returned south after about
  five years, because very few bowhead whales remained to be caught" (p. 16).
  No closing year given for the station itself; steam whalers stopped visiting
  in **1912**.
- **1912** — **Capt. Joseph-Elzéar Bernier** (Kapitaikallak) returns "as a
  private trader"; he had made three earlier voyages in the government vessel
  *Arctic* (p. 17). No end date given.
- Other traders: **Henry Toke Munn**, "an English adventurer"; **Robert Janes**,
  "an unfortunate Newfoundlander" (p. 17). No dates in QTC. (RCMP 1923 report,
  p. 33: Janes "established himself as a trader at Patricia River, Eclipse sound,
  about thirty-five miles from Ponds Inlet" **in 1916**.)
- **1921** — HBC post at the present site of Pond Inlet; **by 1923** it "bought
  out all its rivals" (pp. 10, 17). Corroborated GRISE p. 16: "the Hudson's Bay
  Company (HBC) had established a post at Pond Inlet in 1921."
- **1922** — RCMP post, "beside the HBC store in Pond Inlet" (pp. 10, 17).
- **1929** — Anglican **and** Roman Catholic missions, both in 1929 (pp. 10, 17):
  "In 1929, this stability encouraged the Anglican and Catholic churches to send
  missionaries to Pond Inlet in 1929, though the Tununirmiut were already mostly
  Anglican." (The doubled "in 1929" is in the printed text.)
- **1934–36** — HBC moved 52 Inuit from Baffin Island to Dundas Harbour, Devon
  Island; 18 of them from Pond Inlet. "The experiment was unprofitable because
  ice conditions made hunting and trapping hazardous, and two years later the
  Cape Dorset and Pond Inlet families were sent to Arctic Bay to help support a
  new trading post there" (pp. 17–18).

### Named Inuit individuals (pre-1950)

- **Takijualuk**, "whom traders knew as **Tom Kunuk**" — "Janes was stopped from
  killing another man by Takijualuk" (p. 17). Endnote cites Kenn Harper,
  "Arctic Secrets: Jamie Florence at Bylot Island – Part 2," *Nunatsiaq News*,
  25 Oct 2012.
- **Nuqallaq** (spelled **Naqullaq** in the community timeline, p. 11) — convicted
  in the 1923 trial; see §7.
- **Qattuuq**, his wife **Ulaajuq**, and their four children — 1922, the first
  Tununirmiut hired for the High Arctic (p. 17). (Rendered **Kakto** and
  **Oo-ar-loo** in GRISE p. 16, where they are the first Inuit special constable
  family at Craig Harbour; their two children died of influenza within a couple
  of months and the couple returned to Pond Inlet.)
- **Qitdlarssuaq** (also **Qillaq**) and **Oqe** — leaders of the 1850s migration
  from Cumberland Peninsula to Greenland; Qitdlarssuaq "died around 1870 at Cape
  Herschel, trying to return to Qikiqtaaluk" (p. 14). Pre-period but named.
- **Joe Panipakuttuk**, his wife **Lydia**, his mother **Panikpak**, his niece
  **Mary Panigusiq** (aged six) — hired 1944 at Pond Inlet by Capt. Henry Larsen
  for the *St. Roch* westward transit of the Northwest Passage: "he hired Inuit
  to hunt, advise on navigation, sew clothing for the crew, and generally assist
  with the passage… Lydia was the first woman to travel through the Northwest
  Passage in both directions" (p. 18). Panipakuttuk brought the party home over
  2,500 km by sled over two years.
- **Special Constable Lazaroosie Kyak** — RCMP special constable, father of Mary
  Panigusiq (p. 18).
- **Joseph Idlout** and his wife **Kidlak** — subjects of Doug Wilkinson's *Land
  of the Long Day* (1952–53), living at Aulatsivik (p. 21).
- Post-1950, named in captions and text: Harold Kalluk, Gedeon Qitsualik, Daniel
  Komangaapik, Uirngut, Paul Idlout, Rebecca Qillaq Idlout, Angutirjuaq, Mary
  Aaluluuq Kilukuishak, Jobie Nutarak, Jimaima Angiliq, Asina Qamaniqj,
  Ullatitaq, Panikpakuttuk and Qajaaq (RCMP detachment workers), Simon
  Akpaliapik, Samuel Arnakallak, Jaybeddie Amagoalik (1953 High Arctic
  relocations), Alain Maktar, Gamailie Kilukishak, Moses Kasarnak, Apphia
  Kiliktee, Elizabeth Kyak, Elisapee Ootoova, Manasie Amagoalik, Utoova,
  Daniellie, Qamaniq and Josephie (1966 pirate radio CHPI).

### QTC framing

See §6 for the full quotations. The Pond Inlet report's most pointed line is the
purpose of the 1923 trial (p. 17): "The trial was intended to show Inuit—and the
rest of the world—that Canada would protect Qallunaat and enforce its own laws
in the Arctic islands."

| **Uumanarjuaq / Umanaqjuaq** | Blacklead Island | whaling station and Peck's mission. Report spells it *Umanaqjuaq* (p. 12); the errata for p. 12 keeps *Umanaqjuaq*. |
| **Qikiqtat** | Kekerten / Kekerton Island | whaling station; errata for p. 12 corrects "Kekerton" to "**Kekerten (Qikiqtat)**" |
| Saumia | Cape Mercy | station site |
| — | Ushualuk / Ooshooaluk | pre-1921 trading post (Duval) |
| Aggidjen | Durban Harbour | whaler anchorage on Davis Strait |
| Kivitoo | — | former trading post (Niaqutsiaq) |
| — | Abraham Bay | former trading post (Kanaka) |
| — | Kingmiksok / Kingniksoon | home of Inuluapik |
| Uqqurmiut / Uqqumiut | "people of the Lee Side" | both spellings appear |

### Establishment dates

Introduction, **p. 9**: "It grew around a Hudson's Bay Company (HBC) trading post
that attracted the RCMP in 1923, an Anglican Mission in 1926, and a government
hospital in 1930." — **corrected by the report's own errata**: "p. 9: It grew
around a Hudson's Bay Company (HBC) trading post that attracted the RCMP in 1923,
an Anglican Mission in 1926, and a **government-assisted hospital in 1931**."

- **c. 1860** — "Scottish and American whalers established permanent stations in
  Cumberland Sound" (p. 9). Whaling era dated **1824 to 1919**; organised annual
  whale hunts "ended by 1919" (pp. 9, 13).
- **1877/1880s–1921** — the small Scottish/English stations. p. 17: "There were
  four stations near the mouth of Frobisher Bay, one at Blacklead Island, one at
  Ushualuk, one at Kekerten Island, and another at Saumia (Cape Mercy)."
  **Errata for p. 17 corrects "four" to "two"**: "There were two stations near the
  mouth of Frobisher Bay, one at Blacklead Island, one at Ushualuk, one at
  Kekerten Island, and another at Saumia (Cape Mercy)." All ended **1921**: "With
  the arrival of the HBC in 1921, these stations could not compete with the
  powerful HBC monopoly" (p. 17). p. 12 dates the two main stations' population
  attachment "until 1921."
- **1894** — Rev. **Edmund Peck**, Anglican, "introduced Christianity to
  Qikiqtaaluk in 1894" (p. 17). QTC gives no station name or end date for Peck's
  mission; the Blacklead Island connection has to be supplied from elsewhere
  (`peck-lewis.txt` records the mission party at Blacklead Island from 1894 —
  "On May 8, 1894, the two brethren were com[missioned]", and repeated chapters
  on "The Settlement on Blacklead Island, Cumberland Sound").
- **9 September 1921** — HBC **Netchilik Post** landed from the SS *Bay Chimo*
  (p. 18): "With help from three boatloads of Inuit, the first buildings and cargo
  went ashore on September 9 and the vessel sailed away before dawn on the
  twelfth." An HBCA log note quoted in the endnotes records "at 05:30 on
  8 September, 'Anchored at "Netchelik" ("Pangnirtung Harbour")'".
- **1923** — RCMP detachment: "In 1923, the RCMP also established a detachment at
  the same site and renamed the place Pangnirtung" (p. 18). Corroborated by the
  RCMP 1924 annual report, p. 33: "Inspector Wilcox this summer established a new
  detachment at Pangnirtung Fiord, in Cumberland gulf."
- **1926** — Anglican Mission (p. 9).
- **1930–31** — **St. Luke's Hospital**: "In 1930–31 the government and the
  Anglican Church collaborated in building and staffing St. Luke's hospital at
  Pangnirtung. With a year-round resident doctor and four southern nurses, it was
  the Qikiqtaaluk region's only hospital for over thirty years" (p. 22). Closed
  **1974**, replaced by a nursing station (p. 44).
- **1927** — HBC facilities at Pangnirtung "to chop and render whale blubber
  quickly and to prepare the skins for export" (p. 27).
- **1936** — "Pangnirtung had a kind of day school since 1936" (p. 25).
- **1921–1962** — the "contact–traditional" phase. Body text p. 18 says
  "1921 to 1966"; **errata for p. 18 corrects it**: "In the Pangnirtung trading
  area, the contact–traditional phase lasted from **1921 to 1962**."

### Named Inuit individuals (pre-1950)

- **Attagoyuk** — "the HBC party allowed an Inuk from Kekerten, Attagoyuk, to lead
  them up Pangnirtung Fiord" to the site of the present community, 1921 (p. 18).
- **Inuluapik** — "In 1839–40, a young man from Kingmiksok, Inuluapik, convinced a
  Scottish whaler to enter Cumberland Sound" (p. 15). Pre-period.
- **Angmallik / Angmarlik** — photo caption, "Angmallik distributing biscuits to
  Inuit who unloaded Hudson's Bay Company supplies from RMS *Nascopie*" (p. 17);
  named as the Kekerten figure in Table 1 and commemorated in the Angmarlik
  Centre (pp. 20, 42).
- **Etuangat / Etooangat (Amosee Etooangat)** — "The medical officer's assistant,
  Etuangat, convinced Inuit to use the medical services and taught successive
  doctors to understand and respect their patients" (p. 22); later employed by
  the Department of Health and Welfare (p. 32).
- **Jim Netsiapik Kilabuk (1902–1985)** — long-serving Inuk employee of the
  southern agencies; see Keith Crowe quotation in §6.
- **Duval** (Ushualuk), **Niaqutsiaq** (Kivitoo), **Kanaka** (Abraham Bay) — named
  in Table 1 as the traders associated with those former posts (p. 20).
- Later: Levi Evic, Jaco Evic, Annie Okalik, Raigalee Angnakok, Elijah Kakkee /
  Kakee, John Dialla, Adam Pudloo, Simo Veevee, Peterossee Karpik, Meeka Kilabuk,
  May Akulukjuk, Lypa Pitsiulak, Elijah Irkloo, Tagak Curley.

### Nascopie

- **1941** — "In 1941, the RMS *Nascopie* brought a disease, thought to be
  paratyphoid (a salmonella infection), which killed forty-two people. This was a
  disaster on a scale not seen since the 1850s, and worse than anything that
  followed" (p. 22).
- **1947** — "In 1947, the HBC lost prestige with the sinking of the annual supply
  vessel, the *Nascopie*" (p. 25). Replaced by the government's *C.D. Howe*, 1949.
- Photo captions, p. 22–23: "Inuit women in new parkas awaiting the arrival of
  passengers from RMS *Nascopie*, August 1946."
- **NUUT p. 17**: "In August 1934, the HBC ship R. M. S. *Nascopie* picked up
  fifty-two people and one hundred and nine qimmiit from Cape Dorset,
  Pangnirtung, and Pond Inlet, along with a seasoned HBC clerk, Chesley Russell"
  — the Dundas Harbour relocation. "The HBC post closed after two years and the
  *Nascopie* returned to pick up the relocatees."
- **RCMP 1925 report, p. 37** (not QTC): after the 1923 trial "the C.G.S. *Arctic*
  sailed south on September 3, 1923; the Hudson Bay Company' ship *Nascopie* left
  on September 18, for Montreal, and thereafter Ponds Inlet had no communication
  with the outside world until August of 1924."
- **HBCA ship history** (`nascopie-hbca.txt`), for the span: built 1911–12 at the
  Neptune Works, Wallsend-on-Tyne, for the Nascopie Steamship Co. Ltd. (HBC and
  Job Brothers, formed 1911); Eastern Arctic supply vessel and Newfoundland seal
  fishery 1912–30; laid up and refitted 1931–32; Eastern Arctic Patrol vessel
  1933–47; combined Northwest Passage transit with M.S. *Aklavik*, 1937;
  **1947 wrecked on an uncharted reef at the entrance to Cape Dorset harbour**.

## 3. Kimmirut / Lake Harbour — `qtc-kimmirut.txt`

### Place names

| current Inuktitut | historic English | note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Kimmirut ("heel") | **Lake Harbour** | "Originally known as Lake Harbour, in 1996 the hamlet changed its name to Kimmirut" (p. 9). People: Kimmirummiut. |
| Pituqqiq / Pitiqqiq / Pituqqik | — | large ilagiit nunagivaktangat (spelled three ways in the report) |
| Appatuuqjuaq | — | camp Oola Kiponik's family was moved to "so they could get lots of furs" |
| — | Spicer Island | seasonal whaling station 1877, year-round after 1880 |
| — | Glasgow Inlet, North Bay, Westbourne Bay | harbour geography |

### Establishment dates

- **1877** — "the first seasonal whaling station was established at Spicer Island,
  about 100 kilometres west of Kimmirut, and it operated **year-round after
  1880**. … The station was abandoned in the **early 1890s** due to a decline in
  the number of whales and a reduced demand for whale products" (p. 15).
- **1900–1913** — mica mine: "Beginning in 1900, a Scottish firm, the **Tay Whale
  Fishing Company**, began operating a mica mine at Kimmirut, which was the first
  mine on Baffin Island to employ Inuit labour. … The Tay Whale mine (and dozens
  of other small mica mines in Canada) **closed in 1913**" (p. 15). Production
  1902–07 ran two to seventeen tons a year, with Inuit and Qallunaat miners.
- **1909** — Anglican mission: "The foundation of the Qallunaat enclave that
  preceded the hamlet of Kimmirut was an Anglican mission set up in 1909 by
  **W. J. Bilby** and **Archibald Fleming**" (p. 17). No closing year for the
  original mission; the Anglican minister was withdrawn to Cape Dorset in **1960**
  and "In 1966, the Anglican mission re-opened in Kimmirut" (pp. 28, 33).
- **1911** — HBC post: "In 1911, the HBC also arrived in the area, intent on taking
  advantage of the increasingly profitable fur trade. … The new post, established
  under the management of **William Ford**, provided a more permanent place for
  trade in lieu of the intermittent and now declining whaling presence" (p. 19).
  Still open through the period; government feared closure in the 1960s.
- **1922** — "The Eastern Arctic Patrol (EAP) was established in 1922 to carry
  administrators, RCMP officers, supplies, and medical service to missionary and
  trade enclaves in the region" (p. 19).
- **1924** — RCMP post: "In 1924, an RCMP post was erected at Kimmirut" (p. 19).
- **c. 1942–1945** — "during the Second World War, the United States established a
  radio and weather station at Kimmirut that closed down at the end of the war"
  (p. 20).
- **1949–50** — first government (welfare-teacher) school, Isabell D. Erickson,
  autumn 1949, discontinued end of July 1950 (pp. 21–22).
- **1950–1953** — first nurse, **Nurse Rundle**, arrived 1950, "killed in February
  1953 after a rafter in the supply warehouse collapsed on her"; nursing station
  closed summer 1959 (pp. 25, 28).

### Named Inuit individuals (pre-1950)

- **Ikidluak** — earliest memories aboard the whaling ship *Active* (p. 15–16,
  quoting Dorothy Eber).
- **Navalio** — "Piloted by an Inuk man, Navalio, the HMS *Scarborough* visited
  Kimmirut in 1937" (pp. 19–20).
- **Oola Kiponik** — "recalled how her family was moved to the ilagiit
  nunagivaktangit, Appatuuqjuaq, 'so they could get lots of furs' for the
  company" (p. 19).
- **Akavok, Davidii, Santee** — Elders and Inuit Anglican leaders "named in 1966"
  who "were also responsible for overseeing religious services" after the
  minister left (p. 30). Graburn called the system "very self-sufficient" and
  "thoroughly integrated with the traditional authority structure."
- **Joanasie Lyta** — camp leader at the camp Goteleak Judea's family left (p. 33).
- Boat-building trainees 1951–53: **Napatchee, Noah, Kapee** (Kimmirut),
  **Sheookjuke, Davidie** (Cape Dorset) (p. 22).
- Naming practice, p. 17: "Whalers who were unable to pronounce Inuit names simply
  assigned new names to the workers. Names such as Charlie, Jim, and Adam became
  popular, and Inuit soon adapted them into Inuktitut. John became Joanasie,
  Jacob became Jaykeepee, and Adam became Aatamii."
- Later: Pauloosie Lyta, Maliktoo Lyta, Goteleak/Judea Goteleak, Perry Ikkidluak,
  Joannie Ikkidluak, Elijah Padluq, Elijah Mike, Elisapee Itulu, Mary Pudlat and
  her husband Jutai, Taqialuk Temela, George Pitsula, the Killiktee family,
  Elijah Michael, Simeonie Aqpik, Pauloosie Padluq (carvers). Qallunaat: HBC
  manager **Gordon Rennie** (arrived 1949, married into a Kimmirut family),
  Rev. **Mike Gardener**, Cst. **G. C. Barr**, Cst. **Terry Jenkin**.

### Establishment dates

- **1903** — "the establishment of trading posts at Pond Inlet (a small fur-trading
  post in 1903) and Kivitoo (Sabellum Company in **1916**) drew Inuit away from
  the Clyde River region" (p. 15). **Conflict:** NUUT p. 38 dates the Kivitoo post
  "from **1912 to 1927**". Flagged; do not treat 1916 as settled.
- **1923** — both the Sabellum and HBC posts: "This changed in 1923 when the
  Sabellum Company and the HBC moved in. The Sabellum Company established a post
  on Henry Kater Peninsula, and the HBC on the site of what would become the
  original community of Clyde River, on the east side of Patricia Bay" (p. 15).
- **1926** — Sabellum closes: "When the Sabellum Company closed in 1926 after the
  death of Mr. **Pitchforth**, the post trader, the HBC became the primary
  Qallunaat influence in the area" (pp. 15–16).
- **1935–1940** — "the HBC relocated a number of trappers and their families from
  Lake Harbour, Cape Dorset, Iqaluit, and Pangnirtung between 1935 and 1940 in an
  attempt to increase the post's profitability" (p. 17). "The family that had
  formally [sic] assisted the Sabellum Company at Henry Kater was one of the
  relocated families."
- **1942** — US Army Air Corps weather station for the Crimson Route, on the east
  shore of Patricia Bay next to the HBC post; transferred to the Canadian
  Department of Transport April **1945**; "the station was eventually closed in
  **1963**" (pp. 17–18).
- **1953–54** — LORAN station at Cape Christian (first "DOPE 2"), and RCMP
  detachment. Intro p. 10: "the Long Range Navigation station (LORAN) and RCMP
  detachment in 1953–54." Body p. 19: "The RCMP arrived to open a new permanent
  detachment at Cape Christian in 1953." LORAN abandoned **1974**; detachment
  moved from Cape Christian to the new Clyde River townsite after the **1970**
  settlement move (p. 30).
- No mission is recorded at Clyde River in the pre-1950 period.

### Named Inuit individuals

Almost all post-1950: Sam Palituq, Johanasie Apak, Jason Palluq, Levi Illingayuk,
Jacobie Iqalukjuak, Mary Iqaqrialuk, Arnaq Illauq, Larry Okkumaluk, Simonie
Michael. No pre-1950 Inuit are named in this report; the one pre-1950 Inuit
presence recorded is the unnamed "family that had formally assisted the Sabellum
Company at Henry Kater" (p. 17).

## 5. Ellesmere / High Arctic detachments — `qtc-grise.txt` (context for the RCMP span)

- **1922** — "the Canadian government moved to establish RCMP detachments on
  Ellesmere Island in an attempt to assert their claim to the area. At that time,
  there was only one other RCMP post north of the mainland, located at Herschel
  Island… The first detachment was located at Craig Harbour" (p. 16).
- **1926–1933** — "In 1926, a second post was established on the Bache Peninsula,
  but was abandoned in 1933" (p. 16).
- **1940** — "With the onset of the Second World War… the Craig Harbour RCMP
  detachment was also closed in 1940" (p. 16). **Reopened August 1951** (p. 17);
  moved to Grise Fiord 1956.
- Special constables, p. 16: "The RCMP often employed Inuit families at the
  detachments as special constables to help hunt, serve as guides, and assist in
  maintaining the post. The first Inuit special constable at Craig Harbour was
  **Kakto**, from Pond Inlet. Kakto brought with him his spouse, **Oo-ar-loo**,
  and their two children. Unfortunately, after only a couple of months, the two
  children died from influenza. … After that, all special constables were
  recruited from Greenland until the post's closure in 1940."
- **1934–1947**, Dundas Harbour and after (NUUT pp. 16–18): the HBC "agreed to
  operate a trading post at Dundas Harbour, importing Inuit to hunt and trap
  there, in exchange for government permission to re-open a post at Arctic Bay."
  Post closed after two years; Pangnirtung families returned home, the others
  transferred to Admiralty Inlet, then in **1937** to the HBC's new **Fort Ross**
  post on Bellot Strait, then in **1947** to Taloyoak. "In a dozen years, these
  people were relocated four times."

## 6. Quotations

Verbatim from the QTC reports. Page numbers from the running heads of the
standalone community booklets; where a report gives no page marker the section
heading is named.

### On dependence on trade and the fur trade economy

**Q1 — KIMM, p. 16** ("Changing Patterns of Life")

> "Whalers had introduced new technologies and forms of exchange in the Arctic.
> New materials, such as firearms, became important parts of the Inuit economy
> and changed hunting techniques. The need to acquire ammunition committed Inuit
> to trade relationships. The introduction of the whaleboat had less of an
> impact, but still influenced hunting techniques. Inuit found whaleboats more
> durable, convenient, and efficient than their umiaq. As a result, people became
> more dependent on trade in daily life."

**Q2 — PANG, p. 19** — the HBC's break with whaling-era reciprocity

> "The HBC was more interested in fox furs than whale products or sealskins, and
> for twenty years struggled to make Uqqurmiut focus more on trapping. This was
> difficult, partly because of mistrust. The HBC had abandoned the long-term
> reciprocal sharing that prevailed in whaling days and instituted a straight
> barter system. Inuit also complained of being cheated when they had to pay for
> things they were convinced they had been offered as gifts or had already paid
> for."

**Q3 — PANG, p. 19** — hunters' priorities against the trapping economy

> "HBC managers frequently complained that Inuit wanted to hunt, not trap, and
> that many fox skins were lost by lack of effort. On the other hand, Inuit
> hunters wanted to ensure that they and their relatives obtained the essentials
> of life, namely meat and oil, before shifting their effort to trap foxes."

**Q4 — KIMM, p. 23** — the monopoly

> "The need to keep the 'Company' (the HBC) happy was closely linked to fears
> that it would close its Kimmirut operations. The HBC was a monopoly—Inuit could
> only purchase rifles and food and sell skins, carvings, and handicrafts through
> the post. In 2008, Elisapee Itulu told the QTC that her father had felt
> 'controlled' by the HBC and was made to come to Kimmirut to help with the
> boat-building project."

**Q5 — NUUT, p. 14** — how the map of posts made the map of communities

> "Ten of the thirteen present-day community sites in Qikiqtaaluk were chosen
> before 1950, mainly by RCMP, missionaries, and trading companies. These became
> administrative centres for regional management and delivery of services. Each
> place is on saltwater and is accessible by ships or large boats at least once a
> year. From 1909 onwards, the Hudson's Bay Company (HBC), with a near monopoly,
> aimed to have a single trading post in each productive hunting area."

**Q6 — NUUT, p. 14** — "settlements" were enclaves

> "What Qallunaat called settlements were the places where outside agencies,
> always including the HBC, maintained a few small wooden buildings to provide
> lodging and storage space for the handful of Qallunaat who lived there. These
> settlements could more correctly be called enclaves. While a few employed Inuit
> and their families may have lived there, these enclaves were surrounded and
> heavily outnumbered by Inuit."

**Q7 — POND, p. 26** — trading areas fixed which settlement a camp would move to
(QTC quoting the *Inuit Land Use and Occupancy Project*)

> "By the time day and residential schools had been established in each
> settlement, every camp had clearly come to be seen as within the province of
> one or another HBC post. And when the occupants of a camp decided to move into
> a settlement, it was clear, in virtually all cases, which settlement it would
> be. Thus the Tununirmiut looked to Pond Inlet."

### On the police as the face of Canadian law

**Q8 — POND, p. 17** — the point of the 1923 trial

> "In 1923, the government's Eastern Arctic Patrol (EAP) came ashore with a
> magistrate at Pond Inlet to try three of Janes's Inuit companions for murder.
> One of them, Nuqallaq, was sentenced to ten years in prison in Manitoba. The
> trial was intended to show Inuit—and the rest of the world—that Canada would
> protect Qallunaat and enforce its own laws in the Arctic islands."

**Q9 — KIMM, p. 19** — the three institutions in place

> "By the 1920s, the Canadian government had begun to direct more of its
> attention towards Qikiqtaaluk. The Eastern Arctic Patrol (EAP) was established
> in 1922 to carry administrators, RCMP officers, supplies, and medical service
> to missionary and trade enclaves in the region. In 1924, an RCMP post was
> erected at Kimmirut. The three main Qallunaat institutions—missionaries,
> traders, and RCMP—were now firmly in place at Kimmirut and prepared to keep
> watch over Inuit."

**Q10 — PANG, p. 24** — the balance of power in the enclave

> "Until the end of the Second World War, the HBC was the dominant economic force
> in Pangnirtung, and the Mission had great cultural influence. The RCMP made
> patrols and were respected and feared, especially for their influence over the
> issue of relief to invalids and poor hunters. … Overall, however, the balance
> of power was evident in transport and communications—it was the HBC that
> imported government supplies from 1932 to 1947 and controlled the messages
> going out on the settlement's only radio transmitter."

**Q11 — PANG, p. 19** — clearing the enclave of the old and infirm, 1928

> "The Inuit population at the Pangnirtung post grew slowly, limited to hired
> employees and a handful of Elders and a few others who could not hunt. In 1928,
> the RCMP and traders sent the aged and infirm out to their relatives in ilagiit
> nunagivaktangit, relieving the government and the HBC of the cost of feeding
> them."

**Q12 — PANG, p. 28** — the settlement made unwelcoming (QTC's own summation,
after quoting Cst. G. C. Barr on "loitering")

> "Johnson's report shows hard-nosed attitudes towards family allowance, a
> universal social program and an entitlement of anyone with children, and
> 'relief,' social assistance available to those in genuine need. Inuit who came
> to trade were not welcome to stay after they transacted their business. Inuit
> were Canadians with the right to live where they pleased, but the RCMP,
> traders, and missionaries made the settlement an unwelcoming place except for
> people who were on business or were in obvious need due to poor health or old
> age."

**Q13 — GRISE, p. 18** — the three institutions named as disrupters

> "While all the relocatees had experience with Qallunaat institutions and
> culture, the extent to which those contacts had disrupted patterns of Inuit
> living and knowing varied. In northern Quebec, Itivimiut had been very well
> acquainted with the three institutions that played a large role in disrupting
> Inuit life—traders, missionaries, and RCMP."

### On missionaries and conversion

**Q14 — PANG, p. 17** — Peck and syllabics

> "Other than whalers, the most important Qallunaaq to live in the region was

**Q18 — GRISE, p. 18** — missionary competition

## 7. The killing of Robert Janes (1920) and the 1923 Pond Inlet trial

### What the QTC actually says

The QTC community history for Pond Inlet devotes **two short passages** to this,
on **p. 17**, and gives no month, no place name for the killing, and no account
of what became of Nuqallaq after sentencing. Both passages verbatim:

> "Other competitors included an English adventurer, Henry Toke Munn, and an
> unfortunate Newfoundlander named Robert Janes. Janes was abandoned by his
> southern backers, and quarrelled with and threatened his Inuit companions, who
> put him to death to protect themselves. Janes was stopped from killing another
> man by Takijualuk (whom traders knew as Tom Kunuk)."

> "This became widely known when the HBC installed a post at the present site of
> Pond Inlet in 1921, and a landmark trial in Canadian Arctic history followed.
> In 1923, the government's Eastern Arctic Patrol (EAP) came ashore with a
> magistrate at Pond Inlet to try three of Janes's Inuit companions for murder.
> One of them, Nuqallaq, was sentenced to ten years in prison in Manitoba. The
> trial was intended to show Inuit—and the rest of the world—that Canada would
> protect Qallunaat and enforce its own laws in the Arctic islands."

The community timeline (p. 11) lists "Trial of **Naqullaq**" among the outsider
events of the 1921–45 band. The report's endnotes point to **Shelagh D. Grant,
*Arctic Justice: On Trial for Murder, Pond Inlet, 1923*** (McGill-Queen's, 2002)
as its source; CLYDE's endnotes cite the same book (p. 39). **Grant is not in
staging** — if fuller QTC-aligned narrative is wanted, that is the book to get.

QTC's framing is unambiguous on two points: the killing was **self-protective**
("who put him to death to protect themselves"), and the trial was a **display of
sovereignty** rather than of justice ("intended to show Inuit—and the rest of the
world—that Canada would protect Qallunaat and enforce its own laws").

### Supplementary: RCMP Commissioner's annual reports (NOT QTC)

Dates, names, verdicts and sentences that the QTC omits, from
`rcmp-1923v59i5p21_1673.txt` (Appendix A, "The Murder of Robert S. Janes",
pp. 33–35) and `rcmp-1924v60i4p21_1714.txt` (pp. 32–34). These are the colonial
record in its own voice; quote with that framing.

- **1916** — Janes "established himself as a trader at Patricia River, Eclipse
  sound, about thirty-five miles from Ponds Inlet." Previously second officer of
  the *Arctic* on the 1910–11 expedition. His backing in St. John's fell through,
  ships failed to arrive, "and the natives ceased to trade with him."
- **Early 1920** — Janes and his driver, an Inuk named **Oo-Took-ito**, set out
  for Chesterfield Inlet; "arrived on **March 14** at a large native encampment at
  **Cape Crawford**." Killed there "on or about the end of March in the year one
  thousand nine hundred and twenty."
- **7–26 December 1921** — Staff-Sergeant **A. H. Joy** patrols to Cape Crawford
  and recovers the body, which had been buried in the snow by four Inuit —
  **Oo-Orloo, Ewah-lah, Jock** and Jock's son **Panik-pah** — an act Joy called
  "an admirable and christian-like act."
- **23 January – 11 February 1922** — coroner's inquest at Pond Inlet, Joy sitting
  as coroner, jury of three traders plus a fourth sworn as special constable;
  eight Inuit examined. Verdict: **Noo-kud-lah, alias Ki-wat-soo** did "kill and
  murder the said Robert Janes by shooting him through the body and head with a
  rifle"; **Oo-roo-re-ung-nak** and **Ah-tee-tah** "did feloniously and of their
  malice aforethought aid and abet."
- **Arrests 1922** — Oo-roo-re-ung-nak 29 May, Ah-tee-tah 12 June, Noo-kud-lah
  10 July (formal arrest postponed a week until an interpreter could be found).
  Preliminary inquiry 10–20 July 1922; committed for trial 20 July.
- Joy on the caution given to the accused: "The accused were given the statutory
  warning, and although the form was complied with and the best explanation
  possible given them, I was convinced that it was beyond their comprehension and
  each made a statement without examination."
- Defence evidence: Janes "had threatened violence, and at one time had assaulted
  Noo-kud-lah's father, and further that he had taken some fur from Ah-tee-tah
  against his will… had had a dispute with the natives, had been very angry, and
  had threatened to shoot their dogs and to shoot some of them." "The Eskimos
  insisted that they were greatly in fear of Janes."
- **Trial, 25 August 1923**, at the police detachment, Pond Inlet. Judicial party
  left Quebec in the SS *Arctic* 7 July, arrived 21 August, returned 4 October:
  **Judge L. A. Rivet** (Montreal); **A. Falardeau** (Quebec), Crown prosecutor;
  **Leopold Tellier** (Montreal), defence; **F. X. Biron**, clerk. Jury drawn from
  the crew of the *Arctic*.
- **Verdicts and sentences:**
  - **Noo-kud-lah, alias Ki-wat-soon** — guilty of **manslaughter**; **ten years'
    imprisonment in Stony Mountain penitentiary** (Manitoba).
  - **Oo-roo-re-ung-nak** — guilty of manslaughter, recommended to clemency;
    **two years' hard labour in the RCMP guardroom at Ponds Inlet**.
  - **Ah-tee-tah** — **acquitted**.
- The Crown "stated that in civilization he would ask for a verdict of murder, but
  taking into consideration the ignorance of the prisoners, he only asked for a
  verdict of manslaughter."
- Inspector C. E. Wilcox on the removal, and on the trial's intended effect —
  the passage that most directly corroborates the QTC's reading:

  > "The effects of the sentence, I believe, will have a more beneficial effect
  > than a sentence of death. The prisoner Noo-kud-lah was led away immediately
  > after sentence was passed, to the ship, through a gazing crowd of his own
  > people, without being given a chance to communicate with any of them. It is
  > hardly possible that a native with the prestige that Noo-kud-lah must have had
  > with the other Eskimo at the time he killed Janes could have been subjected to
  > greater humiliation than to be led away directly under the eyes of not less
  > than one hundred of his relatives and friends."

  > "After the termination of the trial, the judge addressed the entire Eskimo
  > population outside the detachment, telling what they had to expect from the
  > representatives of the Government, that they could expect kindness and
  > protection from the police if they behaved well, but if they committed any
  > crime they could expect to be punished."

- **Afterwards** — "The prisoner Noo-kud-lah was escorted by a member of the force
  to Quebec by the ss. *Arctic*, and thence to Stony Mountain penitentiary, where
  he was taken into custody on **October 8**" [1923]. The staged RCMP reports do
  **not** record his release, tuberculosis or death; the 1925 report mentions only
  that the trial was concluded. Neither does the QTC. **Gap: what happened to
  Nuqallaq after October 1923 is not answered by anything in staging.**

### ⚠ Name correction

The brief named the third accused as "**Ouangwak**". The RCMP reports name the
three Pond Inlet accused as **Noo-kud-lah (Nuqallaq)**, **Ah-tee-tah (Aatitaaq)**
and **Oo-roo-re-ung-nak** — modern sources render the last as *Ululijarnaat*.
**Ouangwak is a different man entirely**: `rcmp-1923…txt` p. 37 records "the fate
of Ouangwak the absconding murderer" — a fugitive in the Chesterfield Inlet /
Keewatin district whose body was found near Lake Shekoligyouak in 1922, having
perished in a blizzard. He has no connection to the Janes case.
