Between the whalers and the government era, three institutions came north to
Qikiqtaaluk (Baffin Island) and the islands beyond it: the trading post, the
mission and the police detachment. They arrived roughly in that order, usually
a decade or two apart, and by 1950 there was hardly a stretch of the Baffin
coast that did not lie within a sled journey of all three. Almost nobody lived
at them. Inuit lived in ilagiit nunagivaktangit — the camps they had always
used — and came in to trade. But the sites those buildings occupied are where
the communities of Nunavut stand today.

## Why they came

The whale fishery that had drawn Scottish and American ships into Cumberland
Sound and Davis Strait since the 1820s collapsed at the turn of the century;
the Cumberland Sound hunts ended in 1919. See [[topic:whaling]]. What replaced
whale oil and baleen was white fox fur, and the men who had run whaling
stations turned to buying it. [[person:Crawford Noble]] of Aberdeen, who owned
the stations at [[place:Uumanarjuaq]] and [[place:Kekerten]], and Robert Kinnes
of Dundee, whose station at [[place:Albert Harbour]] was run by
[[person:James Mutch]], are the hinge between the two eras: the same buildings,
the same men, a different commodity.

The Hudson's Bay Company came late to the eastern Arctic and then came
everywhere, opening [[place:Kimmirut]] in 1911, [[place:Kinngait]] in 1913,
and then in two years — 1921 and 1923 — [[place:Panniqtuuq]],
[[place:Mittimatalik]], [[place:Kangiqtugaapik]] and half a dozen outposts,
buying out every rival by 1925. See [[page:the-trading-posts]].

The police came for a different reason. Canada had been handed the Arctic
islands by Britain in 1880 and had done very little about them. Norwegian and
American expeditions wintered in the archipelago; Greenlandic hunters crossed
Smith Sound to take muskoxen on Ellesmere; reports reached Ottawa that in law
the place was nobody's. The answer was a police post.
[[place:Mittimatalik]] in 1921, [[place:Craig Harbour]] on Ellesmere Island and
[[place:Panniqtuuq]] in 1922-23, [[place:Dundas Harbour]] on Devon Island in
1924 and [[place:Bache Peninsula]] in 1926 were about a flag, an occupied
building and a sledge patrol that could be written up in a sessional paper. See
[[page:the-police-detachments]] and [[page:the-sledge-patrols]].

## The missions

[[person:Edmund James Peck]] landed at [[place:Uumanarjuaq]] (Blacklead Island)
on 21 August 1894 with a medical colleague, fifteen tons of coal and a syllabic
New Testament. He was not the first missionary in the Arctic, but he was the
first to settle permanently on Baffin Island, and the writing he brought did
more than the preaching: the syllabic characters, adapted from
[[person:James Evans]]'s Cree script, could be taught to an adult in eight or
nine weeks, and Inuktitut literacy became general within a generation. It is
still the writing of Nunavut. See [[page:peck-and-the-syllabics]].

[[person:Archibald Lang Fleming]] and [[person:Julian W. Bilby]] opened the
Kimmirut mission in 1909; Panniqtuuq followed in 1926 and Mittimatalik — both
Anglican and Roman Catholic in the same year — in 1929.

## The Janes case and the first court

In March 1920 the Newfoundland trader [[person:Robert Janes]] was shot near Cape
Crauford on Admiralty Inlet by [[person:Nuqallaq]], after the camp had decided
that Janes, who was threatening people and their dogs over debts, was a danger
to it. The trial held at [[place:Mittimatalik]] in August 1923 was the first
criminal court on Baffin Island, and the
[[artifact:posts-rcmp-missions-007|Qikiqtani Truth Commission]] is direct about
its purpose: to show Inuit, and the rest of the world, that Canada would protect
Qallunaat and enforce its own laws in the Arctic islands. Nuqallaq got ten years
at Stony Mountain, contracted tuberculosis there, was released in 1925 and died
at home that December. See [[page:the-janes-case]].

## The ship

None of it worked without a ship. The [[artifact:posts-rcmp-missions-005|RMS
Nascopie]] supplied the posts, carried the police, brought the Eastern Arctic
Patrol with its doctors and its magistrate, and did it once a year. When she
struck an uncharted reef off [[place:Kinngait]] on 21 July 1947 the eastern
Arctic lost the thread that had held it to the south. See
[[page:the-nascopie]], and for the wreck itself
[[topic:wrecks-disasters]].

## What followed

This is the direct precursor of the settlement era. The
[[artifact:posts-rcmp-missions-009|Qikiqtani Truth Commission]] counts ten of
the thirteen present-day communities in Qikiqtaaluk as standing on sites chosen
before 1950 by traders, missionaries and police. The Hudson's Bay Company's own
relocations came first — fifty-two people moved from Baffin Island to
[[place:Dundas Harbour]] in 1934 to trap for a post that failed within two
years, and on to [[place:Ikpiarjuk]] (Arctic Bay) in 1936. Craig Harbour, a
sovereignty post of 1922, was where the families of the High Arctic relocation
were landed in 1953, and the detachment moved from there to
[[place:Ausuittuq]] (Grise Fiord) in 1956. For all of that, see
[[topic:relocations-qtc]].

## The record, and its limits

Most of what is written down about these fifty years was written by the people
who came, not the people who were here. The five RCMP annual reports quoted
throughout this topic are the force's own account of itself. Peter Usher's
[[source:usher1971fur|catalogue of trading posts]] is a departmental compilation
whose question marks are honest but frequent. [[source:lewis1904life|Lewis's
life of Peck]] is a missionary book. The corrective is the Qikiqtani Truth
Commission, which worked from Inuit testimony, and where the two accounts of the
same event differ, this topic says so rather than choosing.
