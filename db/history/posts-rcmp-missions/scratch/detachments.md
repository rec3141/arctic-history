Canada acquired the Arctic islands from Britain in 1880 and for forty years did
almost nothing with them. Foreign expeditions wintered in the archipelago and
named things; Greenlandic hunters crossed Smith Sound to take muskoxen on
Ellesmere; the Danish explorer Knud Rasmussen's associates reported that
Ellesmere was, in law, nobody's. What Ottawa did about it was to send
policemen.

## Ponds Inlet, 1921

The first of the new posts was at [[place:Mittimatalik]].
[[person:Alfred Herbert Joy]] was given his instructions on 6 July 1921, left
Montreal on the 16th, and landed on 29 August. He then spent the winter as the
only member of the force north of Chesterfield Inlet on the eastern side of the
Arctic. The annual report was pleased with itself about it:

> Whereas last year Tree River, on Coronation gulf, was our most remote
> outpost, that place has been taken by Ponds Inlet, adjacent to Lancaster
> sound, and at the northern end of Baffin island.

It also said, unusually plainly, why:
[[artifact:posts-rcmp-missions-015|the desirability of extending the
administrative jurisdiction of the Dominion to these regions]], and the killing
of the trader [[person:Robert Janes]]. Sovereignty and a homicide, in that
order.

## Craig Harbour, 1922

The following summer the government sent the CGS *Arctic*, with
[[person:Joseph-Elzear Bernier]] as master and [[person:John Davidson Craig]]
of the Department of the Interior in charge, to plant posts on Baffin, Devon
and Ellesmere Islands. She could not carry material for three, so the Devon
Island stores were left in Quebec.
[[artifact:posts-rcmp-missions-001|Her track that summer]] is the founding
document of the whole system.

She left Quebec on 18 July, was stopped by pack ice off Pond Inlet on 15
August, and pushed north instead, entering Jones Sound on the 20th. The
intended site, Fram Fiord, was blocked with ice; a usable anchorage was found
opposite Smith Island and named [[place:Craig Harbour]] for the expedition's
commander. Stores went ashore from the 22nd, and on 28 August
[[person:Charles Edward Wilcox]] and six men stayed:

> Inspector Wilcox left the *Arctic* and entered upon his sojourn there on
> August 28; he bade farewell to the ship in a blinding snowstorm. With him are
> one non-commissioned officer and five constables.

The report's next sentence is the point of the whole exercise, and reads oddly
now: *"No further news has been received from Inspector Wilcox, nor can any be
expected until late in 1923."* A post office was established, with Wilcox as
postmaster, on a coast where no mail could arrive for a year.

## What the first winter was like

[[artifact:posts-rcmp-missions-017|Wilcox's own account]] is exact:

> The sun was last seen on October 25 and did not again appear until February
> 13, a period of 109 days of darkness and intense cold. Severe blizzards were
> frequent and during January the wind blew for 21 days without a break, at
> times with the violence of a gale.

They were cut off from 28 August 1922 until 6 August 1923, and then again from
15 August 1923 until 9 August 1924. In that second winter, on 22 February 1924,
the detachment building burned down in a north-east blizzard with all three men
inside. The fire extinguishers were frozen and there was almost no water.

> Although in mid afternoon, a flag pole which had been erected as a wireless
> mast within five yards of the building could not be seen through the blinding
> snow. Articles were subsequently found scattered over the ice to a distance
> of nearly two miles.

The day after the fire the thermometer stood at 55 below zero. They moved into
the blubber shed, and four days later a badly fitted stove nearly killed all
three: Constable Lee fainted at the door, Corporal Michelson collapsed, and
Constable Anstead got outside and revived the others. Two Inuit families
brought from Etah in north Greenland kept the detachment supplied with meat and
clothing through the year; the report does not name them.

## Pangnirtung, Dundas Harbour, Kane Basin

Wilcox founded a detachment at [[place:Panniqtuuq]] on 11 September 1923, and
it became the headquarters of what the force called the Ellesmere Island
sub-district — a command that by 1924 ran from Cumberland Sound to 78 degrees
42 minutes north.

In the summer of 1924 the *Arctic* — after a heavy sea off Belle Isle flooded
her engine room, put out her fires and forced the crew to throw the deck coal
overboard — planted two more posts. [[place:Dundas Harbour]] on the south coast
of Devon Island, Constable E. Anstead in charge, was the site Craig had
recommended two years before because Lancaster Sound stays open longer than
Jones Sound. And on Rice Strait, opposite Pim Island at the southern end of
Kane Basin, at 78 degrees 42 minutes north, a sub-post of Craig Harbour was
built "about 200 miles farther north than Craig Harbour", to be visited by
sledge patrol in the winter.

## Bache Peninsula: the loneliest post in the empire

[[place:Bache Peninsula]], opened in 1926 and abandoned in 1933, is the
northernmost police post that has ever been permanently occupied anywhere. The
phrase attached to it — the loneliest post in the empire — was a press phrase
and the force did not discourage it. In several years the supply ship could not
reach it at all and it had to be provisioned by sledge from Craig Harbour, a
journey of some 200 miles each way. Its purpose was not policing in any
ordinary sense. There was no permanent Inuit population on that coast; the
Inuit who lived at the post were Inughuit families brought from Etah in
Greenland, whose hunting kept the detachment alive. The post existed so that
Canada could say the place was occupied.

*Note:* the 1924 annual report calls the new sub-post the Kane Basin
detachment and puts it on Rice Strait, and does not use the name Bache
Peninsula at all. The 1926 post that the Qikiqtani Truth Commission and later
writers call Bache Peninsula is in the same district but the relationship
between the two buildings is not settled by the sources held here.

## Patrols

The visible work of these posts was the sledge patrol, and the annual reports
print the narratives at length in their appendices.
[[page:the-sledge-patrols]] follows three of them from the winter of 1923-24.

## Afterwards

Craig Harbour closed about 1940, reopened in August 1951, and moved to
[[place:Ausuittuq]] (Grise Fiord) in 1956. It was at Craig Harbour that the
families of the High Arctic relocation were landed in 1953 —
see [[topic:relocations-qtc]]. The buildings the police put up for sovereignty
in the 1920s became, thirty years later, the reason a particular stretch of
coast was chosen to put people on.
