[[person:Edmund James Peck]] was a sailor before he was a missionary. He
joined the Church Missionary Society, and in 1876 the Society sent him to
Little Whale River on the east coast of Hudson Bay, where Inuit came in to
trade. He learned the language fast — fast enough that when he reached
Cumberland Sound eighteen years later he found he could be understood there
without effort, which surprised everybody, himself included.

## The characters

The thing Peck carried north was not his own invention. In the 1840s the
Methodist [[person:James Evans]], at Norway House on Lake Winnipeg, had
devised a syllabic script for Cree because he could not teach hunters to read
the Roman alphabet in the few days a year they were at the post. Each
character stands for a syllable; there are no silent letters and no spelling.
Evans cast his first type from the sheets of lead that lined the fur traders'
tea chests, made ink from chimney soot and printed on birch bark.

Peck transcribed portions of the Moravians' Labrador Inuktitut Testament into
those characters as soon as his first winter was over, and the effect was
immediate. On 9 April 1877 he wrote the sentence that is
[[artifact:posts-rcmp-missions-002|the best short account of what happened]]:

> I have succeeded in teaching several of the Eskimos to read in the Syllabic
> characters; they are very eager to learn. One of them said that he was 'mad
> to learn.' There are twelve Eskimos who can now read the 3rd chapter of St.
> John's Gospel in their own tongue.

His biographer put the mechanics plainly: by this means an ordinarily
intelligent adult could be taught to read in eight or nine weeks, which "would
be quite impossible with the Roman characters, especially considering that
many of the people come into the trading ports for a few days only at a time."
That is the whole argument. Syllabics were adopted because they fitted a life
lived on the land, not at a school desk — and they spread the way news
spreads, one reader teaching the next, far ahead of any missionary. Inuktitut
literacy in the eastern Arctic became general within a generation and remains
so; the script on Nunavut's road signs, legislature and newspapers is Evans's,
by way of Peck.

It should be said what the books were. They were scripture, hymns and prayer.
Literacy arrived bound to conversion, and reading and Christianity were, for
most people who learned in these years, one thing.

## Uumanarjuaq

By the early 1890s Peck wanted a station among Inuit who had never met a
missionary. Through a Glasgow clergyman he was introduced to
[[person:Crawford Noble]], an Aberdeen shipowner who had — about a week earlier — bought the whaling
station on [[place:Uumanarjuaq]] (Blacklead Island) in Cumberland Sound.
Noble offered a free passage in his ship *Alert* and leave to build on the
island. The Church Missionary Society agreed on condition that a colleague go
too, and at the Society's May meetings in Exeter Hall a medically trained
former student, [[person:Joseph Cameron Parker]], stood up and volunteered.

They left London on 27 June 1894 in
[[artifact:posts-rcmp-missions-027|Noble's whaler *Alert*]] and sighted Cape
Mercy on 18 August.
[[artifact:posts-rcmp-missions-003|Peck's diary for 21 August]] records the
anchorage:

> We dropped anchor off Blacklead Island on August 21. Eskimo boats were soon
> alongside, and we had an early introduction to the Arctic aristocracy. The
> island, as its name indicates, contains the mineral blacklead. It is a
> small, high, barren rock. It is a two hours' walk round it on the frozen
> sea.

He was blunter later: "In very truth this island is a gloomy-looking spot,
almost absolutely nothing to be seen but rocks, and the bones of whales which
strew the place everywhere."

The *Alert* went across to [[place:Kekerten]], Noble's other station, without landing
the mission's fifteen tons of coal; she came back on 20 September holed by ice
in a gale, and on the 21st everyone — Inuit, missionaries and crew — unloaded
her to keep her afloat. The coal came ashore. Peck's diary: "What a mercy that
we were not left without fuel in this miserable region!" On 29 September the
ship sailed, and the two men expected to hear nothing from anyone for two
years. [[artifact:posts-rcmp-missions-004|The entry for that day]] is the one
worth reading for what a mission station in 1894 actually was. As it happened
two whalers called on 10 October and carried later letters out; the two years
turned out to be one.

## The church of whalebone

The building they eventually put up was framed on whale ribs, and Peck's
readers at home called it the whalebone church. The book's own photograph of
[[artifact:posts-rcmp-missions-025|the settlement]] shows the whole of it: two
or three wooden buildings on a low snow-covered shore, a boat, and nothing
else. Most of the people he wrote about lived in
[[artifact:posts-rcmp-missions-028|snow houses]] nearby. Conversion was slow for six
years and then came in a rush. Through the winter of 1900-01 Peck split the
congregation: [[person:Julian W. Bilby]] took the ordinary service in the church while Peck taught
the candidates in the mission house. On the day after he first raised the
subject, twenty-six people — two men and twenty-four women — asked to be
enrolled for baptism. The pattern of that list is worth noticing: the first
Christians of Cumberland Sound were overwhelmingly women, and Peck's diary
credits them with bringing their husbands afterwards. In February 1902 he
baptised seven in a day and then, on the 10th, a man whose wife Eve Nooeyout
had been among the first the year before — "she has, I believe, used her
influence for Christ and has thus led her husband to make a public confession
of his faith."

The [[source:lewis1904life|1904 biography]] is a missionary book and reads
like one; God gets the credit for the weather. But it prints Peck's diary at
length, and the diary is a first-hand record of Cumberland Sound in the
1890s — sickness, starvation years, the angakkuit he argued with, the boats
that did not come back.

## Afterwards

Peck left Cumberland Sound for good in 1905. The mission at Uumanarjuaq
continued under others and was eventually moved across to
[[place:Panniqtuuq]], where the Hudson's Bay Company had opened a post in
1921 and the RCMP a detachment in 1923. The Anglican mission there dates from
1926, and St Luke's hospital - the first hospital in the eastern Arctic -
from 1931. The three institutions together made the settlement. The island itself is
now the Blacklead Island Whaling Station National Historic Site.

Peck's other monument is a grammar and a dictionary, and every syllabic text
printed in the eastern Arctic since.

The book carries eighteen plates, and
[[artifact:posts-rcmp-missions-026|the one of the children outside the mission]]
is worth looking at for what it does not say: twenty or so children in
caribou-skin clothing, none of them named anywhere in the book.
