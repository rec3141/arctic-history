The annual reports of the Royal Canadian Mounted Police print their sledge
patrol narratives in an appendix, day by day, and they are the best written
record of travel in the eastern Arctic in the 1920s. They are also, read
carefully, a record of how completely the police depended on Inuit. The winter
of 1923-24 produced three of them.

## Joy's attempt on Ellesmere, 5 March - 20 April 1924

The object, in [[person:Alfred Herbert Joy]]'s words, was "to test the
practicability of communicating with the Ellesmere Island detachment" — that
is, whether [[place:Craig Harbour]] could be reached overland from
[[place:Mittimatalik]] if a ship could not get through. It could not.
[[artifact:posts-rcmp-missions-023|The track]] runs 650 miles in 47 days.

He left at noon on 5 March with [[person:Ululijarnaat]] and fourteen dogs, and
with Constable Friel and [[person:Kachoo]] behind them with a second team of ten
carrying dog feed for the first days. They went up Navy Board Inlet, out into
Lancaster Sound, and west along the coast past the mouth of Admiralty Inlet.
Friel and Kachoo turned back on 15 March from a village of six families at the
floe edge, "all short of meat and oil". [[person:Aatitaaq]] came up with his
family on the night of the 17th. [[person:Sinnikah]] and [[person:Ivahlung]]
arrived while the party was storm-bound at Sargent Point on the 20th and 21st,
and Sinnikah scouted the ice.

On 24 March they struck out across Lancaster Sound for the west end of Devon
Island. Two bears were killed that day and "devoured in under five minutes by 80
hungry dogs." On the 26th they met a lead of open water between twenty-five and
four hundred yards wide, could find no crossing, and were out of dog feed. Joy
turned back. Sinnikah and Ivahlung carried on south-west toward Leopold Island
on their own.

The return was worse than the outward journey. Leads cut them off on drifting
ice; on the 29th and 30th the pressure threw up "a perpendicular wall of ice 30
to 40 feet high" against the ice-foot, and it took until noon to get the outfits
onto firm ice. Between 1 and 3 April they hunted without success, out of oil,
meat and blubber, the dogs starving. On the 4th Ululijarnaat killed a very large
oogjuk and saved the patrol. Ululijarnaat was at this moment seven months into
the two-year sentence he had been given at
[[page:the-janes-case|the trial the previous August]]; Aatitaaq had been
acquitted at the same assize.

On 10 April, on Admiralty Inlet, they met Peter Freuchen of Knud Rasmussen's
Fifth Thule Expedition travelling with two families bound for Cape York in
Greenland, and stayed with him about three hours. Joy caught a chill talking in
the open and was ill for three days. On the 16th he camped at Low Point:

> This place was described by a member of one of the Arctic expeditions as being
> "the coldest place on earth," and I fully concur, for the time being, with the
> man who made the statement. On my two previous visits here I saw the Eskimos
> lie face downwards on the ice to protect themselves from the fierce,
> penetrating wind.

They reached the detachment on the afternoon of 20 April. "It had taken us 27
hours to cover about 20 miles."

## The Home Bay patrol, 29 February - 15 April 1924

Corporal F. McInnes and Constable W. B. MacGregor left the
[[place:Panniqtuuq]] detachment at eleven in the morning on 29 February with
[[person:Kidlappik]] and Koodloo, twenty-five dogs on two sledges and about
1,100 pounds of load. They went up Pangnirtung Fiord, into the Pangnirtung
stream, hauled the sledges up the falls that MacGregor had named
[[person:Oo-nee-ak-sah-gah|Oo-nee-ak-sah-gah Falls]] for the man who had gone
ahead with him to lay the cache, climbed two more falls, crossed the height of
land, and reached [[place:Kevetuk]] on Home Bay on 8 March. About 510 miles in
47 days.

They were there to investigate three killings. In the winter of 1921-22 a man
named Neakoteah, in charge of the [[person:Hector Pitchforth|Sabellum Trading
Company]]'s outpost, had become deranged and claimed to be Christ; his
derangement first became dangerous at a dance on Christmas Day 1921. On his
order two men, Mungeuk and Seeming, were killed. Kidlappik — the same man
driving the police sledge — then shot Neakoteah. The camp numbered ten
families, thirty-five to forty people.

The patrol took twelve statements over a month, with MacGregor interpreting, and
Inspector Wilcox recommended that no charges be laid: the expense of bringing a
court up would not be warranted, and the people had been warned. It is worth
setting that decision beside [[page:the-janes-case|the Janes case]]. Three Inuit
killed by Inuit produced a recommendation and a warning. One trader killed by
Inuit produced a judge, a Crown prosecutor, a defence counsel, a clerk, a jury
brought from Quebec, and ten years in a penitentiary.

On the way home, on 13 April, the patrol met Pitchforth himself with seven Inuit
travelling with him — Kedluk and his wife Nahsoogahlooahng, Ahtahkahleeng and
his wife Koonoo, Petahotoong and his wife Peneloo, and Peraketoong of Padlee —
all of them short of food. The police gave them meat, blubber, coal oil and dog
meat. Pitchforth died alone at the Cape Henry Kater post two years later, and
that ended the Sabellum trade on the east Baffin coast.

## Friel to Clyde River, 31 March - 3 May 1924

Constable H. P. Friel went from Mittimatalik down the east coast to
[[place:Kangiqtugaapik]] and back, about 550 miles in 34 days. The report gives
the distance and the dates and no narrative at all — which is itself
characteristic. A journey of that length in April was routine enough not to be
worth writing up.

## What the patrols were for

Very little of this was law enforcement. The patrols showed the flag, carried
mail, took a census of camps, checked on people who were short of food, and
established that the ground had been covered — which mattered in Ottawa more
than anything found on it. They also, unavoidably, made the police the
institution that decided who received relief; the
[[artifact:posts-rcmp-missions-011|Qikiqtani Truth Commission]] records that
Inuit were "respected and feared" for exactly that reason.

And they could not have happened at all without Inuit dogs, Inuit clothing,
Inuit ice knowledge and Inuit hunting. Every one of these three patrols was
kept alive by a hunter whose name the report gives in a spelling nobody now
uses.
