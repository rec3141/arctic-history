In August 1923 a judge, a Crown prosecutor, a defence counsel and a clerk of
court were carried from Quebec to [[place:Mittimatalik]] in the CGS *Arctic*,
and a jury was made up out of the ship's crew. They were there to try three
Tununirmiut men for the killing of a Newfoundland trader three years earlier. It
was the first criminal court held on Baffin Island, and the
[[artifact:posts-rcmp-missions-007|Qikiqtani Truth Commission]] says plainly
what it was for:

> The trial was intended to show Inuit — and the rest of the world — that
> Canada would protect Qallunaat and enforce its own laws in the Arctic
> islands.

## Janes

[[person:Robert Janes]] was a Newfoundlander who had been second officer of the
*Arctic* on [[person:Joseph-Elzear Bernier]]'s expedition of 1910-11. In 1916 he
set up as a trader on his own account at the Patricia River on Eclipse Sound,
about thirty-five miles from Mittimatalik. The trade did not work. His backers
in St John's fell away, he ran out of goods, and by the winter of 1919-20 he was
travelling the camps demanding furs he said were owed him.

The QTC calls him "an unfortunate Newfoundlander". The RCMP's own summary of the
defence evidence is harsher: that Janes had threatened violence; that he had
assaulted [[person:Nuqallaq]]'s father; that he had taken fur from
[[person:Aatitaaq]] against his will; and that at Cape Crauford he threatened to
shoot the people's dogs and some of the people. Dogs were not property in the
sense a court in Quebec would understand. A camp without dogs in March could not
move, could not hunt, and would starve. The QTC's Pond Inlet report adds that
Janes had earlier been stopped from killing another man by
[[person:Takijualuk]].

In early 1920 Janes set out with his driver [[person:Oo-Took-ito]] to walk out
of the Arctic — overland to Chesterfield Inlet by the west coast of Baffin
Island, Melville Peninsula and Wager Bay, a journey that would have been very
unlikely to succeed. On 14 March 1920 they reached a large camp at Cape
Crauford, on the northern corner of Baffin Island. Nuqallaq was in that camp.

## The killing

Some days later — the coroner's jury would put it "on or about the end of March,
the precise date being to the jurors unknown" — the camp decided that Janes had
to be stopped. Ululijarnaat went into the snowhouse and told him another man had
skins to sell. As Janes came out, Nuqallaq shot him through the body. He did not
fall; Aatitaaq pushed him down; Nuqallaq fired again through the head.

The police report notes, as evidence of guilt, that "after the murder had taken
place a number of the Eskimos gave the murderer skins as a reward." Read the
other way, it is evidence of what the killing was: not a private crime but a
decision taken by a camp, in the way Inuit law had always dealt with a man who
was dangerous and could not be restrained, and the gifts were the camp
acknowledging the act as its own. Nobody hid. The body was buried in the snow by
four men — Oo-Orloo, Ewah-lah, Jock and Panik-pah — and left where the police
would find it.

## The investigation

[[person:Alfred Herbert Joy]] had been at Mittimatalik less than four months
when he was ordered to look into it. He left the detachment on 7 December 1921,
reached Cape Crauford on the 21st, and found the body on 26 December. He
performed the autopsy himself and found two bullet wounds. He then held a
coroner's inquest at Mittimatalik from 23 January to 11 February 1922, with a
jury of three found among the traders and a fourth man sworn in as a special
constable to make up the number. Eight Inuit were examined. The verdict named
Nuqallaq as the killer and Ululijarnaat and Aatitaaq as aiders and abettors.

[[artifact:posts-rcmp-missions-018|The force's own report]] is candid about what
this involved:

> While carrying out these operations, which necessitated his assuming in turn
> the characters of a policeman, a coroner and a magistrate, Staff-Sergeant Joy
> was alone, so far as this force is concerned; the only other white men in the
> vicinity were those belonging to the two trading stations at Ponds Inlet.

Making the arrests took another five months, because the people concerned were
living where they lived. Nuqallaq had moved to Iglulik on Fury and Hecla Strait,
"nearly five hundred miles from Ponds Inlet if the main coast line be followed";
the witnesses were scattered over a district three hundred miles by two hundred;
and a dog epidemic was running through the region. Ululijarnaat was arrested on
29 May 1922, Aatitaaq on 12 June, Nuqallaq on 10 July — his formal arrest was
put off for a week after he arrived until a satisfactory interpreter could be
found. A preliminary inquiry before Joy as justice of the peace ran from 10 to
20 July and committed all three.

## The court

The judicial party left Quebec on 7 July 1923 and reached Mittimatalik on 21
August. The court opened at the police detachment on the 25th: His Honour
[[person:Louis-Arthur Rivet]] of Montreal presiding, A. Falardeau of Quebec for
the Crown, Leopold Tellier of Montreal for the defence, F. X. Biron clerk, and a
jury drawn from the crew of the ship that had brought them.

The Crown told the jury that "in civilization he would ask for a verdict of
murder, but taking into consideration the ignorance of the prisoners, he only
asked for a verdict of manslaughter." That sentence carries the whole theory of
the proceeding: that Inuit were subject to Canadian law but not yet, in the
court's view, capable of it.

- **Nuqallaq** — guilty of manslaughter; **ten years** in Stony Mountain
  penitentiary, Manitoba.
- **Ululijarnaat** — guilty of manslaughter, recommended to the clemency of the
  court; **two years' hard labour in the RCMP guardroom at Ponds Inlet**.
- **Aatitaaq** — **acquitted**.

The sentences were designed to be seen. One man was taken thousands of miles
away; one served his time in a building in the middle of the community; one
walked free. [[person:Charles Edward Wilcox]]
[[artifact:posts-rcmp-missions-019|described the end of it]] with evident
satisfaction:

> The prisoner Noo-kud-lah was led away immediately after sentence was passed,
> to the ship, through a gazing crowd of his own people, without being given a
> chance to communicate with any of them.

Nuqallaq was taken south in the *Arctic*, which sailed on 3 September, and was
received at Stony Mountain on 8 October 1923.

## What followed

Seven months later, in March 1924, Ululijarnaat — still serving his sentence —
drove Joy's lead team of fourteen dogs on
[[artifact:posts-rcmp-missions-023|the attempt to reach Ellesmere Island]], and
kept the party alive: a seal on 11 March, another on the 16th, and a very large
oogjuk on 4 April when the dogs were starving. Aatitaaq, acquitted, followed
with his family and joined the patrol on the 17th. The men the court had tried
in August were the men the force depended on in March.

Nuqallaq did not serve his ten years. He contracted tuberculosis in the
penitentiary — his half-sister's explanation, quoted by
[[source:harper2006nuqallaq|Kenn Harper]], was "He worked so hard that he got
tuberculosis" — and in March 1924 the prison doctors found bacilli and warned
that he would fail rapidly if he stayed confined. He was released on ticket of
leave in the late spring of 1925 and came ashore at Mittimatalik on 3 September,
dressed in a white man's suit, and was met on the beach by his young wife
[[person:Ataguttiaq]]. He died on 5 December 1925, three months after coming
home. Harper's judgement is that the sentence was in effect a death sentence.

The fullest account of the whole affair, and the one the QTC relies on, is
Shelagh Grant's *Arctic Justice: On Trial for Murder, Pond Inlet, 1923* (2002),
which is not in this collection. A request has been filed for it.

## Why it matters

The killing of Robert Janes is often told as the moment Canadian law arrived in
the Arctic. It is more accurate to say it was the moment Canadian law was
performed there. The detachment at Mittimatalik existed
[[artifact:posts-rcmp-missions-015|partly because of this case]]; the case in
turn justified the detachment. And the pattern set in 1923 — a decision made in
Ottawa, executed on a ship, explained to a community afterwards — is the same
pattern that runs through the dog killings, the relocations and the residential
schools that [[topic:relocations-qtc]] documents.
