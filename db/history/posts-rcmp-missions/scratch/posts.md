Peter Usher's [[source:usher1971fur|catalogue of every fur trade post in the
Northwest Territories]] lists fifty-six trading sites in the eastern Arctic
between 1870 and 1970. Almost all of them were opened between 1911 and 1940,
and more than half were shut again within fifteen years. The map of Nunavut
today is what was left standing.

## Whalers first, traders after

There was no fur trade post on Baffin Island in the nineteenth century.
British and American ships whaled off the east coast for fifty years and kept
shore stations, particularly in Cumberland Sound, and bought some fox and seal
alongside the oil and bone; but the post as an institution is a twentieth-
century arrival. Usher puts it plainly:

> Such places as Cape Haven, Blacklead Island and Kekerten were whaling
> stations long before trading posts were established there.

When the whale fishery failed — the Cumberland Sound hunts ended in 1919 —
the men and the buildings were already in place. Robert Kinnes of Dundee, who
had run whalers, ran the stations at Kekerten and at Albert Harbour near
Mittimatalik. Crawford Noble of Aberdeen owned [[place:Uumanarjuaq]] and
Kekerten. The Sabellum Trading Company put small posts along the east coast at
[[place:Kivitoo]], Cape Mercy, Cape Haven and Cape Henry Kater, most of them
run by a single man, often an Inuk employed by the company, with the ship
calling once a year. [[person:Henry Toke Munn]]'s Arctic Gold Exploration
Syndicate traded at Button Point on Bylot Island, at Albert Harbour and at
Oshualuk in Cumberland Sound. [[person:Joseph-Elzear Bernier]], who had
claimed the archipelago for Canada from the deck of the CGS *Arctic*, bought
the Albert Harbour and Salmon River posts and traded on his own account from
1912 to 1919. [[person:Robert Janes]] had a post of his own on Eclipse Sound
from 1916.

## The Company arrives, and then is everywhere

The Hudson's Bay Company came to Baffin Island from the south. Its first post
was at [[place:Kimmirut]] (Lake Harbour) in 1911; [[place:Kinngait]] (Cape
Dorset) followed in 1913, and Amadjuak, Hall Bay and Ward Inlet in the years
after. Then, in the two years 1921 and 1923, it took the whole island:
[[place:Panniqtuuq]] and [[place:Mittimatalik]] in 1921,
[[place:Kangiqtugaapik]] (Clyde River) in 1923, with outposts at Blacklead
Island, Kekerten, Livingstone Fiord, Sirmilling Bay and Oshualuk. It bought
Kinnes out in 1923 and the Arctic Gold Exploration Syndicate the same year.
Usher's summary:

> While ex-whalers were opening up the fur trade on the east coast of Baffin
> Island, the Hudson's Bay Company was penetrating new territory on the south
> coast. The Company extended its operations to eastern and northern Baffin
> early in the 1920s, and by 1925, with the withdrawal of several smaller
> whaling and trading companies, had obtained an effective monopoly throughout
> the Eastern Arctic.

Twenty-three posts were opened in the region before 1940 and nine were still
open at the end of that year. Usher explains the pattern without sentiment:
posts were cheap to open when fur prices were high, they were opened to shut
competitors out, and once the clientele was established and the competition
gone, the Company could rationalise.

Government policy pushed the same way. From 1926 the whole region lay inside
the Arctic Islands Preserve, and the Department of the Interior restricted
where posts could stand. The Company's withdrawal from Port Leopold and from
[[place:Ikpiarjuk]] (Arctic Bay) in 1927 — two posts a year old, opened with
the names Sikinik, Moon, and Tukik, Sun — was a consequence of that policy,
not of the market. Arctic Bay reopened in 1936.

## What a post did to the people around it

The [[artifact:posts-rcmp-missions-010|Qikiqtani Truth Commission]] describes
the change in the terms of trade when the Company replaced the whalers:

> The HBC had abandoned the long-term reciprocal sharing that prevailed in
> whaling days and instituted a straight barter system. Inuit also complained
> of being cheated when they had to pay for things they were convinced they
> had been offered as gifts or had already paid for.

There was a deeper mismatch. The post wanted white fox, which is trapped in
winter, at some distance from the good seal ice, and which nobody eats. Inuit
wanted meat and oil first.

> HBC managers frequently complained that Inuit wanted to hunt, not trap, and
> that many fox skins were lost by lack of effort. On the other hand, Inuit
> hunters wanted to ensure that they and their relatives obtained the
> essentials of life, namely meat and oil, before shifting their effort to
> trap foxes.

What tied people to the post was not the fox but the rifle. Once a family
hunted with a gun it needed cartridges, and cartridges came only from the
trader. As the QTC's Kimmirut report puts it, "the need to acquire ammunition
committed Inuit to trade relationships."

The Company also moved people. Where it wanted furs from a district with no
trappers in it, it recruited families and shipped them: to
[[place:Dundas Harbour]] in 1934 from Kinngait, Panniqtuuq and Mittimatalik;
to Arctic Bay when Dundas failed in 1936; to Fort Ross in 1937. These were
company relocations, done for fur, a generation before the government
relocations that [[topic:relocations-qtc]] covers, and they set the precedent.

## The shape that was left

Ten of the thirteen communities in the Qikiqtaaluk region today stand where a
trader, a missionary or a policeman put a building before 1950. The QTC is
careful about what those places were:

> What Qallunaat called settlements were the places where outside agencies,
> always including the HBC, maintained a few small wooden buildings to provide
> lodging and storage space for the handful of Qallunaat who lived there.
> These settlements could more correctly be called enclaves ... these enclaves
> were surrounded and heavily outnumbered by Inuit.

Almost nobody lived at a post year-round in this period. Families lived in
ilagiit nunagivaktangit, the camps they had always used, and came in to trade.
The move into the settlements is the story of the years after 1950, and it
belongs to [[topic:relocations-qtc]].

## A note on the dates

Usher's question marks are his own and are kept in this database. Many of the
small east-coast posts have no surviving records at all and their dates come
from a departmental map, an RCMP patrol report or a trader's memoir. Where two
sources disagree — the Kivitoo post is dated 1911?, 1912 and 1916 by three
different authorities — both figures are recorded and the disagreement is
noted rather than resolved.
