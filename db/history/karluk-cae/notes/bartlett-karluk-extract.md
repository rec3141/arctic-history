# Extraction notes — Bartlett & Hale, *The Last Voyage of the Karluk* (1916)

Source file: `db/history/karluk-cae/files/karluk-cae-bartlett1916-karluk.txt` (raw OCR).
Page numbers below are the book's own printed page numbers (recovered from the
running headers the OCR preserves, e.g. "THE SINKING OF THE KARLUK 85"), not
OCR line numbers, and not the Table of Contents alone — except where noted "(TOC)".
OCR is noisy (long‑s/ligature garbles, "Karluk"→"Karluh"/"Karlvk", double‑spacing,
"Wrangell"→"Wrangell"/"Wrangel," "Chukches"→"Churches" in running heads, etc.);
I have silently normalized obvious letter-drops when quoting only where the
reading is unambiguous, and flagged anywhere the reading is genuinely in doubt.

**Important caveat on coordinates:** the book itself gives an explicit
latitude/longitude fix on only **two** occasions in the whole narrative
(marked "TEXT" below). For every other position I either (a) have only a
place name and a narrative description of distance/bearing from a known
point (marked "NARRATIVE" — I give the numbers the text actually states,
e.g. "sixty miles north Herald Island"), or (b) have nothing from the text
at all and am supplying the modern real-world coordinates of the named
place from general geographic knowledge, clearly marked "EXTERNAL, not in
text" and rounded to about a tenth of a degree. Do not treat the EXTERNAL
figures as anything Bartlett measured — they are for map-orientation only.
West longitude is negative throughout; Chukotka/Wrangel Island points that
lie just west of the antimeridian (180°) are also given as negative, per
the standard −180…+180 convention.

**Note on the 1914‑01‑07 fix:** the text prints "Lat. 72.11 N., Long. 174.36
W." This is genuinely ambiguous OCR/typesetting of an old navigational
convention. Read as **degrees.minutes** (the usual period-as-separator style
of the era, i.e. 72°11′N, 174°36′W) it converts to **72.183°N, −174.600°W**.
Read literally as **decimal degrees** it would be 72.110°N, −174.360°W. The
two readings differ by only about 0.07° in latitude and 0.24° in longitude
(≈8–15 nautical miles), so it does not change the story, but I cannot tell
from the OCR which the book intended, and I did not find a third occurrence
of the same style elsewhere to disambiguate.

**Note on "Cape North/Billings":** the book never uses "Cape Billings." It
uses "Cape North" throughout (the point Captain Cook named in 1778, sailing
the *Resolution* — book p. 227), which is generally identified with modern
Cape Schmidt (Mys Shmidta). If "Cape Billings" was meant as a distinct
waypoint, it is not in this text.

**Note on "East Cape/Uelen":** the book's spelling is "Ugelen," used exactly
once (p. 300), and only for the *Bear*'s later August 1914 coastal call
(Emma Town → East Cape → **Ugelen** → across to Teller, Alaska) — not on
Bartlett and Kataktovick's original spring walk, which reached "Emma Town"
near East Cape on April 24 and never separately names Uelen.

## 2. The ship's company, as the book lists it

**Master and crew** (p. 8–9, "The crew consisted of the following"):
- R. A. Bartlett — master
- Alexander Anderson — first officer (also called "the mate")
- Charles Barker — second officer
- John Munro — chief engineer
- Robert J. Williamson — second engineer
- Robert Templeman — steward
- Ernest F. Chafe — messroom boy
- John Brady — able seaman
- S. Stanley Morris — able seaman
- A. King — able seaman
- H. Williams — able seaman
- F. W. Maurer — fireman
- G. Breddy — fireman
- John Hadley — carpenter ("One other member of the crew was added in
  Alaska, — John Hadley, who signed on as carpenter," p. 9; he had been in
  charge of Charles Brower's whaling station at Cape Smythe)

**Scientific/expedition staff who sailed on the Karluk** (p. 5–9 and the
sailing list on p. 11, "we had also... Stefansson and his secretary, Burt
McConnell, with Murray, Mamen, Malloch, Jenness, Beuchat, McKinlay and Dr.
Mackay"):
- Vilhjalmar Stefansson — commander of the expedition, anthropologist
- Burt McConnell — Stefansson's secretary
- James Murray — oceanographer (previously with Shackleton's Antarctic
  expedition)
- Bjarne Mamen — forester, of Christiania, Norway
- George Malloch — geologist (Canadian Geological Survey)
- Henri Beuchat — anthropologist, of Paris
- Diamond Jenness — anthropologist, Oxford Rhodes Scholar, New Zealand
- William Laird McKinlay — magnetician, University of Glasgow
- George H. Wilkins — photographer, New Zealander
- Alister Forbes Mackay — expedition doctor, University of Edinburgh
  (previously with Shackleton's Antarctic expedition)

(J. J. O'Neill, Kenneth Chipman, J. R. Cox, and Fritz Johansen are named on
p. 5–8 as members of the wider scientific staff but are never again
mentioned as being aboard the Karluk itself — they belonged to the southern
party traveling on the *Mary Sachs* and *Alaska*, not the ship this book
follows.)

**The Inuit family and Kataktovick** (all joined at or near Point
Barrow/Cape Smythe, p. 19–20, spellings as printed):
- Kerdrillo — "or Kuralluk," about 35, hunter, later put in general charge
  of hunting and reconnaissance on Wrangell Island
- Keruk — his wife, about 28, the party's seamstress; called "Auntie" by
  the crew
- Helen — their daughter, age 7 (walked the entire route on foot,
  sometimes helping haul the sledge)
- Mugpi — their baby daughter, "not much over a year old" (carried on
  Keruk's back the whole way to Wrangell Island)
- Kataktovick — "an Eskimo... between eighteen and twenty years old, who
  was already a widower, with a baby girl whom he had left with his
  mother"

**Also briefly aboard, from Point Hope** (boarded July 31, 1913, departed
with Stefansson Sept. 20, 1913, and never returned to the ship): Panyurak
("Jimmy") and Asatshak ("Jerry"), two Eskimo dog-drivers/hunters, "eighteen
or twenty years old" (p. 13).

**An unnamed white dog-driver** joined at Port Clarence and was discharged
at Cape Smythe/Point Barrow (p. 11, 20) — never named in the text.

The ship's black cat, a mascot picked up at Esquimalt, rode out the whole
drift, sinking, sledge journey and rescue (p. 138–139, 322) — not a person,
but the book treats her almost as one of the company.

## 3. Every death

**The doctor's party (Mackay, Murray, Beuchat, Sailor Morris)** — left
Shipwreck Camp Feb. 5, 1914, on their own initiative and against Bartlett's
advice, man-hauling their own sledge, having signed a letter (dated Feb. 1,
1914, p. 128) absolving Bartlett of responsibility. Last seen alive about
20 miles from Herald Island by Chafe's returning party (mid-February 1914):
"Mackay, Murray and Morris were drawing the sledge; Beuchat was a mile and
a half behind, with hands and feet frozen and partly delirious from
suffering. Morris had cut his left hand with a knife and blood-poisoning
had already set in.... Chafe's opinion was that Beuchat would die that
night" (p. 134). None of the four was ever seen again. The book states no
cause of death beyond this — they simply never arrived anywhere and were
never found: "It was as certain as anything could be that both parties had
long since perished" (p. 317, referring jointly to this party and the
mate's party below).

**The mate's/supporting party toward Herald Island (First Mate Alexander
Anderson, Second Mate Charles Barker, Sailors A. King and John Brady)** —
sent from Shipwreck Camp Jan. 21, 1914, with a supporting party (Mamen and
two Eskimo) that turned back after seeing them to open water near Herald
Island, expecting them to land there or push on to Wrangell. No one ever
found any trace of them: Kerdrillo scouted Berry Spit for "traces of the
mate's party or the Mackay party" and found none (p. 163); the *Corwin*
later cruised all around Herald Island "without seeing any evidences that
any one had been there" (p. 317); McKinlay and Munro had earlier searched
the same area on foot. Cause and manner of death are never established —
they are simply never seen again. (NB: the book never calls Anderson
"Sandy" — he is "Alexander Anderson" at his single full-name mention, p.
478/9 of the front matter, and "the mate" or "Mr. Anderson" throughout.)

**Deaths on Wrangell Island (three, all named explicitly at the moment of
rescue, p. 314: "No," he answered; "Malloch and Mamen and Breddy died on
the island."):**

- **George Malloch** — died of nephritis, "towards the end of May" 1914, at
  Rodgers Harbor, Wrangell Island — the first of the two nephritis deaths.
  "Following their instructions to divide into smaller parties, for
  general harmony and larger hunting areas, Mamen, Malloch and Templeman
  left the main party on Icy Spit and went down around the southeastern
  shore of the island to Rodgers Harbor.... Towards the end of May Malloch
  and Mamen became ill with nephritis and died, Malloch first and Mamen
  only a few days later." (p. 319)
- **Bjarne Mamen** — died of nephritis "only a few days" after Malloch,
  same place, same period (late May 1914), Rodgers Harbor (p. 319).
- **George Breddy** — died at Waring Point, Wrangell Island, cause stated
  exactly as: **"Breddy accidentally shot himself later on, making the
  third death on the island."** (p. 320, verbatim). No further detail —
  date, weapon, or circumstances — is given anywhere in the book.

Total named deaths in the book: George Malloch, Bjarne Mamen, and George
Breddy (confirmed, on Wrangell Island), plus the eight men never found —
Alister Forbes Mackay, James Murray, Henri Beuchat, S. Stanley Morris,
Alexander Anderson, Charles Barker, A. King, John Brady — all presumed
dead but with no confirmed fate recorded in the text.

## 4. Verbatim pinned-map quotations (all under 70 words)

1. **The crushing/sinking of the ship** — Shipwreck floe, ~60 mi north of
   Herald Island, Jan. 11, 1914:
   > "It was at 4 p. m. on January 11, 1914, with the blue Canadian
   > Government ensign at her main-topmast-head, blowing out straight and
   > cutting the water as it disappeared, and the Victrola in the galley
   > sending out the strains of Chopin's Funeral March, that the Karluk
   > sank, going down by the head in thirty-eight fathoms of water."
   (p. 91, 56 words)

2. **The abandonment of the ship** — same place, evening of Jan. 10, 1914:
   > "I went on deck again and gave the order, 'All hands abandon ship.'"
   (p. 88, 12 words)

3. **The parting of the parties** — Icy Spit, Wrangell Island, March 18,
   1914 (Bartlett's written orders to Munro before he and Kataktovick set
   off for Siberia):
   > "You will assemble at Rodgers Harbor about the middle of July where I
   > hope to meet you with a ship."
   (p. 170, 20 words)

4. **The Siberian walk** — on the moving ice between Wrangell Island and
   Siberia, c. March 30–31, 1914, Kataktovick's fear of never returning:
   > "We see no land, we no get to land; my mother, my father, tell me
   > long time ago Eskimo get out on ice and drive away from Point Barrow
   > never come back."
   (p. 195, 32 words)

5. **First sight of the Siberian coast**, ~April 1, 1914:
   > "Me see him, me see him noona (land)," he said.
   (p. 196, 12 words)

6. **The Breddy death, Waring Point, Wrangell Island**, summer 1914:
   > "Breddy accidentally shot himself later on, making the third death on
   > the island."
   (p. 320, 13 words)

7. **The rescue**, off Wrangell Island, aboard the *King and Winge*, Sept.
   8, 1914 — Bartlett's first question and McKinlay's answer:
   > "'All of you here?' ... 'No,' he answered; 'Malloch and Mamen and
   > Breddy died on the island.'"
   (p. 314, 17 words)

8. **The rescue at Rodgers Harbor**, Wrangell Island, morning of Sept. 7,
   1914:
   > "The rescue, both here and at Rodgers Harbor, was effected just in
   > time."
   (p. 322, 13 words)

## 5. Kataktovick as a person

- **Spelling**: the book consistently spells his name **Kataktovick**
  (never "Kataktovik").
- **Age**: not given exactly — Bartlett introduces him as "an Eskimo named
  Kataktovick, between eighteen and twenty years old" (p. 19–20).
- **Home**: joined the ship at Cape Smythe, near Point Barrow, Alaska,
  along with the Kerdrillo family and John Hadley (p. 19–20). At the end of
  the book he tells Bartlett he wants to go to Point Hope rather than back
  to Point Barrow (p. 268), and is later found there again in August 1914,
  brought over from East Cape by Captain Pederson on the *Herman* (p. 301).
- **Family**: "already a widower, with a baby girl whom he had left with
  his mother" when he joined the expedition (p. 20). When Bartlett meets
  him again at Point Hope in August 1914, "he was feeling well, he said,
  and from the looks of things seemed about to be married" (p. 301).
- **Character, as Bartlett describes him**: eager to learn — asks for a
  book to read, returns it after a fortnight and asks for magazines; asks
  for a fountain pen instead of a pencil "to write letters to his Eskimo
  friends" (p. 64–65); on first joining, when told the party bathed
  regularly, he said, "I like my bath" (p. 60). Bartlett calls him
  "sufficiently experienced in ice travel and inured to the hardships of
  life in the Arctic to know how to take care of himself in the constantly
  recurring emergencies" when choosing him as the sole companion for the
  Siberian crossing (p. 165). He built the igloos, hunted seal and bear,
  and "understood perfectly how to use the Primus stove" (p. 198). He was
  visibly and repeatedly afraid — of dying adrift like Point Barrow Eskimo
  he'd heard of who never returned (p. 195, quoted above, and again p. 207:
  "My father my mother told me long time ago Eskimo from Point Barrow go
  to Siberia, never come back, Siberian Eskimo kill him"), and of the
  Siberian natives themselves, until their hospitality reassured him.
  Bartlett judged him game but green: "As far as physical endurance went I
  think he was as well able to survive a fall in the water as I would have
  been but his experience had been less than mine and if he had fallen
  through he would have been so completely terrified that I believe he
  would have died of fright" (p. 188–189). He also worried on Bartlett's
  behalf about giving offense to Siberian hosts: "Kataktovick... would say:
  'You must not talk that way.' He was afraid they would misunderstand my
  earnestness and take offense" (p. 240). At their parting at East Cape
  (May 10, 1914), Bartlett "thanked him... for all that he had done, and
  told him how greatly I was indebted to him for his constant help and for
  his faith and trust in me," gave him the rifle and other gear they had
  carried, and promised his wages would follow (p. 270). Of the whole
  company, Kataktovick and Bartlett were the only two who made it to
  Siberia at all; every other survivor's return depended on the outcome of
  that walk.

## Ambiguities and OCR flags (summary)

- **72.11 N. / 174.36 W. (p. 85)**: ambiguous between decimal-degree and
  degree-minute notation; see note in §1.
- **"Cape Jakan" (pp. 207, 226)**: almost certainly OCR/typesetting for
  "Cape Yakan," but I have left the book's own spelling as printed and
  flagged it rather than silently "correcting" it.
- **"April 19" appears twice, on consecutive pages (pp. 251–252)** — once
  for a rest day at Koliuchin Island ("Kataktovick complained of pains in
  his legs and wanted to stay... for a day to rest") and again, at the
  start of Chapter XXVI, for the departure the "next morning." This reads
  as an error in the original book's dating (or a date the OCR has
  reproduced faithfully from a print error), not an OCR artifact I can
  resolve from context; I have not tried to silently fix it.
- **Running heads mangle "Chukches" as "Churches"** throughout Chapters
  XXII–XXV (e.g. "WE MEET THE CHURCHES"); the body text itself consistently
  spells it "Chukches."
- **"Wrangell Island"** is the book's own spelling throughout (two L's),
  not "Wrangel."
- **"Waring Point"** is the only form used; "Cape Waring" does not occur in
  the text.
- Several running-head page numbers are themselves OCR-garbled digits
  (e.g. "815" for 315, "<m" for 226, "<OT" for 277/283) — I have resolved
  these from sequence/context and cited the true printed page number.
