Source: `/data/dev/arctic-history/db/history/karluk-cae/files/karluk-cae-stefansson1921-friendly-arctic.txt` (raw OCR, 44,076 lines). Page numbers are the printed page numbers recovered from "NNN THE FRIENDLY ARCTIC" / "THE FRIENDLY ARCTIC NNN" running heads, which are themselves noisy OCR — treat them as approximate to ±1 page in a few spots. All decimal-degree conversions are mine; the book gives degrees/minutes only. West longitudes are given as negative. **Notes on the 1914 table:**
- Only three points carry an explicit numeric lat/long in the text: April 7 (70°13′N, 140°30′W — precise, called out as the support party's parting position), April 23 (75°15′N, latitude only), and the "vicinity of" 73°N/141°W around April 25–26.
- **Discrepancy 1 (latitude regression):** the ice-area described on April 23 at 75°15′N is *farther north* than the "vicinity of north latitude 73°" given for the course-change roughly two days later (~April 25–26). That is a drop of about 2°15′ (~155 miles) in two days, which is not plausible either as forward travel or as drift. Either one of the two figures is a compositor/OCR error in the original 1921 text (my best guess, given the otherwise steady ~20 mi/day pace from 70°13′N on April 7, is that "75° 15′" may be a misprint), or the two statements are simply inconsistent in Stefansson's own account. I have not silently corrected it — both figures are reproduced as printed.
- **Discrepancy 2 (day count):** Chapter XXII is titled "LAND AFTER NINETY-THREE DAYS ON DRIFTING ICE," but the chapter's own first sentence says they landed "ninety-six days out from the Alaska coast." I report both; the body text (96) looks like the more carefully composed figure since it appears alongside the mileage reckoning, but the discrepancy is in the original, not an OCR artifact (both "ninety-three" and "ninety-six" are spelled out in full, not digits).
- **Landfall island:** the narrative sequence briefly seems ambiguous (approaching, "the southward slope of Norway Island" is visible ahead, implying they had not yet reached Norway Island) but the text later resolves it explicitly: "At Norway Island we had erected the day after landing a conspicuous beacon..." (p. 235) — so June 25 landfall was on Norway Island itself, and Bernard Island (discovered days later) is the separate, larger island to the east.
- Martin Point's own coordinates are never given numerically anywhere in the surrounding text.

**Notes on the 1915 table:**
- No landing-point coordinates are given for the actual moment of discovery/possession of the new land (June 18–20, 1915); the text explicitly says they could not get a noon latitude sight and "could not wait longer." The only numeric positions tied to this leg are the outbound apex (76°40′N, ~131°W, undated) and the June 4 landing on Prince Patrick Island (76°09′N, longitude not given) — both drawn from the party's own summary in the June 16 cairn-copy record, not from a position fixed at the new-land site itself.
- **"Brock Island" does not appear anywhere in this file.** I searched the full text (`grep -n "Brock"`); every occurrence refers to R. W. Brock, Director of the Geological Survey of Canada (a person, not a place). Only **Borden Island** is discovered and described in these chapters (Chapter XXXIII, "The Discovery of New Land," pp. 324–330; confirmed by the book's own index entry "Borden Island, discovery of, 324–330"). No explicit sentence narrating *when or by whom* the name "Borden Island" was assigned appears in the surrounding text — it is simply used as an established name from later chapters onward. If your source material for "Brock Island" is a different Stefansson text or a later map/gazetteer, it is not in this file.
- **No "Winter Harbour" visit occurs in the 1915 spring journey as narrated here.** Winter Harbour and Parry's Rock are mentioned only historically (McClure's 1852 message to Kellett's men, Mercy Bay chapter, p. 360). The book's actual visits to Winter Harbour occur in later chapters (roughly lines 30,000–33,700 of the file), which belong to the 1916–17 seasons — outside the 1915 spring-journey scope you asked for. I did not include that material.
- The party size: seven men left Kellett in February 1915 (implying Wilkins, Crawford, Natkusiak, Storkerson, Thomsen, Andreasen, Stefansson); three (Wilkins, Crawford, Natkusiak) were sent back once the party reached Cape Alfred (p. 294), leaving four for the ice journey proper.
- **Discrepancy 3:** the text says the advance party left "under the command of Wilkins on February 9th, and the rest of us started a few days later" (p. 293), but the party's own June 1915 record gives February 20th for the main party's departure (p. 322) — eleven days, not "a few."

## 5. Cairn/record texts reproduced verbatim in these chapters

### (a) Bernard Island beacon record, dated June 30 P.M., 1914 (p. 236–237)
> "June 30, P. M., 1914.
> "Storker Storkerson, Ole Andreasen and myself landed on the island next offshore from this one June 25th—men, dogs and gear all in good condition. Shall proceed to-morrow SE to the mainland. According to circumstances we may go up the river, in the mouth of which this island lies, to explore it; or we may go south along the coast towards Kellett. If no traces of us have been found farther south, any vessel of the Canadian Arctic Expedition finding this should proceed south along the mainland ten or fifteen miles in search of a beacon with further information. If none is found, the vessel should erect a beacon or two with information and then go back to this island or some place near it and prepare to winter. Wood should be energetically gathered from the beach within 20 miles each way and caribou should be hunted early to provide fat meat. There appears to be a good harbor on the SE side of the island (just beyond the prominent hill on the S corner). There seems also a harbor on the east of the island offshore from this one and there may be others on the mainland. If no suitable harbor is found, the vessel should look for one to the north rather than the south. The Karluk, should she come, might try to reach Prince Patrick Island if her commander thinks it advisable; the North Star and Mary Sachs should not go beyond Banks Island (except after picking us up). If no traces of us are found, small caches with things not likely to be destroyed by bears might be made for our use in two or three places. We have over 200 rounds of ammunition and both rifles are in good order, so there need be no fear for us on the score of starvation.
> "V. Stefansson."

## 6. General caveats

- Page numbers come from OCR'd running heads and are reliable but not hand-verified against a page-image scan; a small number could be off by one page where the running head itself was garbled.
- Several key events (the moment of possession on the new land, the June 20 noon observation, Martin Point itself, Cape Kellett itself) have **no numeric coordinates anywhere in the surrounding text** — I have marked these clearly rather than supplying outside/real-world coordinates.
- Two apparent internal inconsistencies in the source (the April 23 vs. April 25–26 latitudes, and the "ninety-three days" chapter title vs. "ninety-six days" body text) are reproduced as printed and flagged, not resolved.
- "Brock Island" is not in this file; only Borden Island is discovered/named here. No Winter Harbour visit occurs within the 1915 spring/summer journey as covered by this file — those visits belong to later (1916–17) chapters not in scope.
