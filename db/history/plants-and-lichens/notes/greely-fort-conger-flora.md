From nares-greely/files/nares-greely-lfb-report-v1.txt (Greely 1888 report) and

Appendix IX, Botany, by Lieutenant A. W. Greely: 64 numbered species collected in the summers of
1882 and 1883 near Fort Conger, 81 deg 44 min N, 64 deg 45 min W, identified by Asa Gray and
Sereno Watson of Harvard and George Vasey of the Department of Agriculture. Appendix X, mosses and
lichens, by the Rev. E. Lehnert and Greely. The lichen and Cladonia collections were abandoned at
Fort Conger on the retreat.

## Cape Sabine, the starvation winter 1883-84
- April 1884: "the tips of the purple saxifrage (Saxifraga oppositifolia) commenced to show green,
  and many ate of it in quantities, and several times a considerable amount of it was stewed."
- 21 May 1884, 78 deg 50 min N: a saxifrage seen in blossom; the party mixing saxifrage in the
  stews, "fully nineteen-twentieths of it is the dead plant, with but the faintest tinge of green".
- 5 June 1884: "I got to-day a large quantity of tripe de Roche lichen, and found them very
  nutritious; they certainly are very palatable."
- 7 June 1884: "Everybody is now collecting reindeer-moss, tripe de Roche, and saxifrage, all of
  which it is possible for us to eat."
- 8 June 1884: about two quarts of tripe de roche picked in five hours; a bunch of purple saxifrage
  found in full bloom.
- 9 June 1884: a quart of tripe de roche in six hours; Connell gathered five pounds of saxifrage.
- 10 June 1884: "The stewed tripe de Roche to-day was delicious ... It leaves a sweetish taste."
- Also at Discovery Harbour, late May and June 1882: rock lichens gathered and eaten, mostly boiled,
  some raw; "good tasting, but caused in the beginning a little diarrhea".
- Lemmings recorded as feeding principally on Saxifraga oppositifolia.

# The Nares expedition's botany (1875-76) and Sutherland's list (1850-51)
From nares-greely/files/nares-greely-narrativeofvoyag02nare.txt, nares-greely-moss-shores.txt and
franklin-search/files/franklin-search-sutherland-v2.txt.

Nares, Narrative vol. 2, botanical appendix (pp. 301-313):
- Flowering plants and ferns: 69 identifiable species from lat. 80-83 N, collected by Captain
  H. W. Feilden and Mr. H. C. Hart, listed by Professor Daniel Oliver under Sir Joseph Hooker,
  with about six more too imperfect to name.
- Mosses: Feilden's collection of twenty-two species, examined by W. Mitten. Distichium inclinatum
  from Floeberg Beach, lat. 82 deg 27 min N; Dicranoweisia crispula and Rhacomitrium lanuginosum
  from Payer Harbour, lat. 78 deg 42 min N.
- Lichens: about ninety species from north of lat. 81 N, examined by Professor Theodor Fries of
  Uppsala; at least three new to science.
- Fungi by the Rev. M. J. Berkeley; algae and diatoms by Professor George Dickie.
Dated:
- June-early July 1876, Discovery Bay: "Purple saxifrage is now in blossom, and the sloping grounds
  are fairly carpeted with its bright patches." Dwarf sorrel beginning to sprout. (p. 70)
- 17 July 1876: ranunculus and draba, poppy, mountain avens, small yellow saxifrage and Cerastium
  alpinum among grass and mosses. (p. 70)
- 19 July 1876: "convalescents have been able to gather a small daily ration of dwarf sorrel
  sufficient for their sick comrades." (p. 71)
Moss, Shores of the Polar Sea, p. 70: the order of the season - magenta saxifrage, then yellow
drabas, white coltsfoot and woolly willow catkins, then the yellow poppies and Dryas; and
"four men searching all day could just gather one plateful of the valuable sorrel."

Sutherland, Journal vol. 2: flowering-plant list of 54 species determined by Sir W. J. Hooker,
from Assistance Bay, Bushman Island, Berry Island, Davis Strait and Northumberland Inlet
(Hogarth Sound). Sutherland on the antiscorbutics: "leaves of crucifers in full bloom, sorrel,
and scurvy grass ... I believe persons labouring under scorbutus ought to obtain a sufficient
quantity to effect a beneficial change in the disease." (p. 235)
