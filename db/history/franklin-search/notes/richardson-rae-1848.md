> **Scope warning — this is important.** Volume I is only the *outward* journal. It narrates the 1848 journey from England down the Mackenzie, along the coast, and on foot to Fort Confidence, arriving **15 September 1848** (p. 336), followed by a few pages of arrangements for the winter (pp. 337–338) and then two long general-ethnography chapters (XI–XII) that close the volume. **It does not contain a day-by-day narrative of the winter of 1848–49, nor of Richardson's return to England in 1849.** Those belong to Volume II, which is not among the files here. What Volume I *does* say about the winter — buildings, the muster of "forty-two souls," the men sent away to lighten the mess, the fishery arrangements — is all given below (Section D) and is everything the source contains on the subject; there is no sick list in this volume. Likewise, Richardson's own 1849 return voyage is not narrated; what plans for 1849 exist in Volume I come only from the printed Admiralty instructions (Section B) and from a handful of retrospective footnotes Richardson added while writing up in 1850–51, noting things Rae confirmed "in the following summer of 1849" (Section F).

**How coordinates are marked.** Richardson gives a substantial number of his own latitudes and longitudes, mostly meridional-altitude sights taken on the march or the boat voyage; these are marked **[printed]** and quoted close to his own wording. Where I give a modern approximate position for a place he only names, it is marked **`[inferred from place name]`** and is not in the text.

## B. The plan and the orders

**Origin.** After no word came of Franklin by the autumn of 1847, three search efforts were organized: a sea squadron under Sir James Clark Ross (from the east), a Bering Strait squadron (*Herald*/*Plover*, from the west), and this overland party (p. 18–24). Richardson had suggested to Lord Auckland, First Lord of the Admiralty, in November 1847, that John Rae, a Hudson's Bay Company chief trader "thoroughly versed in all the methods of developing… the natural products of the country, a skilful hunter… an accurate observer with the sextant," be appointed second-in-command; Rae was approved "with the assent of the Governor and Committee of the Hudson's Bay Company" (p. 48–49).

**The object.** "The main object of the searching party entrusted to my charge was to trace the coast between the Mackenzie and Coppermine Rivers, and the shores of Victoria and Wollaston Lands lying opposite to Cape Krusenstern" (p. 22–23).

**Boats and men.** Four boats were built by order of the Admiralty — two at Portsmouth Dockyard, two at Camper's Yard, Gosport — to William Rice's design: two of 30 ft × 6 ft beam × 2 ft 10 in depth, 63 cwt (½ ton with fittings); two of 28 ft × 5 ft 6 in × 2 ft 8 in, 51 cwt. All clinker-built of Norway fir with English-oak stem/stern-posts and rock-elm gunwales, fitted for a false keel to be bolted on before reaching the Arctic Sea (p. 40–41). "Five seamen and fifteen sappers and miners were selected in the month of May [1847]" as boat crews — twenty men in all, mostly tradesmen (six joiners/sawyers, four blacksmiths/armourers) so they could double as builders in winter quarters (p. 42). This party of twenty, under Mr. John Bell, sailed from the Thames on the *Prince of Wales* and *Westminster* on 15 June 1847, reaching York Factory only by early September; Bell's total party (with voyageurs, wives and children) came to forty-five, in five boats (p. 45–46). Bell wintered on the way and reached Fort Confidence's site on 17 August 1848, ahead of Richardson (p. 336).

**Command.** Richardson in overall command; Rae second. Richardson and Rae themselves followed by the fast overland route (steamer, canoe, HBC brigades) to overtake Bell's boats, per instructions: "you are hereby required and directed to take under your orders Mr. Rae… and to leave England on the 25th [of March]… by the mail steamer for Halifax… and New York," expecting to "overtake the boats now under charge of Mr. Bell, in July 1848, somewhere near Isle à la Crosse, or perhaps the Methy Portage" (p. 26–27).

**Depots.** Instructions specified caches of pemican at **Point Separation, Cape Bathurst, Cape Parry, and Cape Krusenstern**, and — at Sir James Ross's request, for a party he meant to send south from Melville Island in spring 1849 — a further depot at **Fort Good Hope** (p. 28–29). Richardson buried the Point Separation cache himself on 1 August 1848 (p. 224), and mentions caches "deposited" at Cape Bathurst and near Cape Parry in passing during the coastal voyage.

**Coordination with James Clark Ross.** Ross's own printed plan (as adopted, p. 20–21) called for one of his ships to winter near Melville Island/Banks's Land and send out two spring sledge parties: "The first should trace the western coast of Banks's Land, and, proceeding to Cape Bathurst, or some other conspicuous point of the continent, previously agreed on with Sir John Richardson, reach the Hudson's Bay Company's settlement of Fort Good Hope." "The second party should explore the eastern shore of Banks's Land, and, making for Cape Krusenstern, communicate with Sir John Richardson's party on its descending the Coppermine River, and either assist him in completing the examination of Wollaston and Victoria Land, or return to England by any route he should direct." Richardson notes drily that these two parties "would pass over that space in which most probably the ships have become involved" (p. 20–21).

**The Admiralty's planned timetable for 1849,** quoted verbatim from the printed instructions (p. 28–31):
- "Should it appear necessary to continue the search a second summer (1849), and should the boats have been housed on the Coppermine, you are to descend that river on the breaking up of the ice in June 1849, and to examine the passages between Wollaston and Banks's and Victoria Lands, so as to cross the routes of some of Sir James C. Ross's detached parties, and to return to Great Bear Lake in September 1849, and withdraw the whole party from thence to winter on Great Slave Lake" (p. 29).
- "Should you have found it necessary to return to the Mackenzie (September 1848), instead of pushing on to the Coppermine, the search in the summer of 1849 would, of course, have to be commenced from the former river again" (p. 29).
- "It is on this account you are to understand that your search is not to be prolonged after the winter of 1849, and which will be past on the Great Slave Lake; but that, at the earliest practicable moment after the breaking up of the weather in the spring of 1850, you will take such steps for the return of the party under your orders to England" (p. 31).
- The Admiralty also gave leave to depart from the coastal plan on Inuit report: "you have also our permission to deviate from the line of route along the coast, should you receive accounts from the Eskimos, which may appear credible, of the crews of the 'Erebus' and 'Terror,' or some part of them, being in some other direction" (p. 28). The same passage gave leave to fall back on Fort Good Hope, depositing two boats there and proceeding "with the other two boats, and the whole of the crews, to winter quarters on Great Bear Lake," if it proved unsafe to reach the Coppermine that season (p. 28).

Note that these instructions, being addressed to "your search," implicitly cover both 1848 *and* a possible 1849 continuation under the same command — which is exactly what happened, except that Richardson himself went home after the first winter and Rae carried on alone in 1849 (see Section F).

## C. The boat voyage, summer 1848

### Down the Mackenzie (mid-July – 31 July)

Richardson and Rae, having overtaken Bell's boats, resumed the descent from the Clear-Water River on 6 July. Day-by-day places and dates are in Section A. Notable incidents along the river:

- At Fort Simpson (p. 163) Chief Factor "M'Pherson had most kindly set aside… a cask of excellent corned beef… and some bags of very fine potatoes raised at Fort Liard" for the winter at Fort Confidence (p. 130, recalling this arrangement).
- An **old Indian woman at Fort Simpson** nearly died after mistaking the poisonous root of *Hedysarum Mackenzii* for the edible *H. boreale*; she recovered because it acted as an emetic (p. 220–221).
- At the **Ramparts** (28 July) "a large body of Hare Indians" were found encamped, drawn by the salmon fishery above the defile, but "complaining of want of food" because the river had not fallen as usual (p. 211).
- At **Fort Good Hope** (29 July), Mr M'Beath supplied venison from his ice-cellar and passed on two pieces of news: a rumour "that a rumour of guns having been heard on the coast of the Arctic Sea, and supposed to have been fired from the Discovery ships, originated in a story brought by the Kutchin or Loucheux to Peel's River Fort, but that the officer in charge placed no reliance upon it" — and that three Eskimos had been shot dead the previous summer by Kutchin at Peel's River after an ascent "surmised, with hostile intentions" (p. 214–215).
- **31 July**, Point Separation: the head of the Mackenzie delta, confirmed against "Franklin's map of 67°49′ north latitude" (p. 223); pemican cache buried there 1 August (p. 224).

### Along the coast, Mackenzie estuary to the Coppermine (3 August – 1 September)

The party — in the surviving small boats, Richardson's and a second under Duncan Clark as coxswain of the third boat — entered the estuary on 3 August and coasted east past Point Warren, Point Atkinson, Cape Brown, Cape Dalhousie, Liverpool Bay, Baillie Islands, Cape Bathurst, Franklin Bay, Cape Parry, and on toward Cape Krusenstern and Coronation Gulf (see the day-by-day table). Encounters with Inuvialuit people are detailed in Section E.

Ice thickened progressively from mid-August. Off **Point Cockburn** on 22 August a sudden storm forced the boats to scud "under the goose-wing of the mainsail" into shelter among grounded ice (p. 289). From 26–30 August the party manhandled boats over ice and portaged around Cape Krusenstern; on 27 August Richardson "determined on lightening the labours of the men, by leaving one boat and her cargo on a rocky point… twelve miles" from the cape, with pemican, an arm-chest and other stores cached on it (p. 293–294). By 1 September, eight miles short of Cape Kendall, "finding that we could not advance further, in the present condition of the ice, without pulling the boats to pieces… I determined, after consulting with Mr. Rae, to leave the boats… and commence the overland march" (p. 298–299).

**Why the crossing to Wollaston/Victoria Land failed.** Richardson is explicit: "The sole hinderance to my crossing Dolphin and Union Straits was the impracticable condition of the close-packed drift-ice… the ice that obstructed our way was composed of hummocky pieces, of irregular shape, and consequently ready to revolve if carelessly loaded or trod upon" (p. 301–303). He notes that in four earlier seasons of record (Franklin 1820, himself 1826, Dease and Simpson 1836–38) the ice off the Coppermine had never been so bad so late in August, and blames "the north-west winds having driven the ice down from the north in the first instance, and… the easterly gales, which afterwards set in, pressing it into that bight of Coronation Gulf" (p. 303–304).

### The walk to Fort Confidence (3–15 September)

The 13-day march from the abandoned boats to Fort Confidence, with loads of ~60–70 lb per man distributed by lot, is given day by day in Section A: Back's Inlet → the newly named Rae River (Eskimos ferry the party across in lashed kaiyak-pairs) → Richardson's River (crossed in Lieutenant Halkett's inflatable boat) → along the Coppermine past Bloody Fall → the Kendall River (crossed by raft, 10–11 Sept) → a night on a bare rock in fog with no fuel → the Dismal Lakes country, where a camp of Indians was met on 13 September → Fort Confidence, reached 4 p.m. on **15 September 1848**.

## D. The winter of 1848–49 at Fort Confidence

*(Everything Volume I contains on this subject — the narrative proper ends here; see the Scope warning above.)*

**Arrival and buildings.** Bell had reached the site on 17 August 1848 and had already erected "an ample storehouse, two houses for the men, and a dwelling house for the officers, consisting of a hall, three sleeping apartments, and store-closet." The officers' house was roofed but not floored or ceiled when Richardson arrived; the kitchen was still to be built. "All the houses erected by Dease and Simpson had been burnt down, except part of the men's dwelling" (p. 336).

**Quarters.** Bell and Rae quartered with the guide Bruce in the store-room; Richardson took a sleeping room "put temporarily in order," its walls not yet clayed, "so that the snow drifted in between every log" (p. 336–337).

**The muster.** On 17 September (Sunday), with the outlying fishermen come in, "we had in all forty-two souls to provide for, exclusive of Indians coming casually on our store" — Bell's fourteen men, three women and four children, plus Richardson's coastal party (p. 337).

**Reducing the mess.** On 18 September the despatch packet was sent off toward Isle à la Crosse in charge of François Chartier and Louis La Ronde (bound for the latter's home), accompanied by three Canadians (Henry Smith, Josephe Plante, Henry Wilson) detached to winter at the Big Island fishery, Great Slave Lake, and by eleven Englishmen "whose services could be well dispensed with at our winter quarters": Stairs, Sully and Clarke (seamen) and Frazer, Dall, Dodd, Sulter, Hobbs, Ralph, Geddes, Webb, Weddell and Bugbee (sappers and miners) — eighteen people in all sent away, "relieved from the maintenance of eighteen people, the resources of the post were considered equal to feeding the remainder" (p. 337–338).

**Fishery.** Two fishermen were kept, by Richardson's order, at the west end of Great Bear Lake near its outlet, both to help feed the party if a retreat up the Mackenzie proved necessary, and to serve travellers between Fort Confidence and the Mackenzie posts, or aid the fort's own fishery "should our fisheries near the fort fail" (p. 338). No fishery totals, and no sick list for the winter itself, appear anywhere in Volume I.

**Sunday service.** 17 September: "we assembled in the hall, where I read divine service and returned thanks to the Almighty for our safety." The Canadians, "though Roman Catholics, were present on the occasion; and most of them regularly attended our Sunday services in the winter" (p. 337) — the only forward-looking remark in the volume about the winter that followed.

**Visitors from inland.** The ethnographic chapters add one retrospective note tying Fort Confidence to the wider country: "our presence at Fort Confidence was sufficient to determine various bands of **Hare Indians, Dog-ribs and Martin-lake Indians**, to resort to the north-eastern arm of Great Bear Lake; and but for a deadly feud with the Dog-ribs, which twenty years ago greatly reduced the numbers of our old friends, **the Copper Indians**, we should have had their company also" (p. 351–352). A newspaper from Honolulu, forwarded by Mr M'Pherson from Fort Simpson, reached Fort Confidence "in the winter" with news of the California gold rush (p. 130, recalled from the July stop at Athabasca Lake).

## E. Named Indigenous people and interpreters

**Interpreters.** The expedition's own interpreter, named repeatedly through the boat voyage and the march (from p. 88 onward), is called simply **"Albert"** — "Albert, our Eskimo interpreter" (p. 88, and very often thereafter, e.g. pp. 310–331). He is described as "a native of East Main" (the HBC post on James Bay) who, on first seeing musk-ox tracks near the Kendall River, was "now for the first time" approaching the animal's range, since the muskox does not occur near his own country (p. 322–323). *The text never gives a surname or "One-Eye" for him — if the historically remembered "Albert One-Eye" is this man, that fuller name is not printed anywhere in this volume.* The volume's other named interpreter, **"Augustus,"** belongs to an **earlier** expedition, not this one: "In 1825, I devoted the whole winter to the formation of an Eskimo vocabulary and grammar, with the aid of our very intelligent interpreter Augustus, who was a native of the shores of Sir Thomas Roe's Welcome, and having resided at Churchill, had acquired the power of expressing his meaning in very tolerable English" (p. 361); elsewhere called "our interpreter in 1826" (p. 356, 366). *William Ouligbuck (junior) is not named anywhere in this volume by that spelling or any recognisable variant* — a targeted search for "Ouligbuck / One-eye / William" found no match; if he served on this expedition, Volume I does not record it under that name.

**Named canoe-men, La Chine to Cumberland House (p. 52–53)** — printed in two lists, spellings exactly as given, nationalities as printed:

*First canoe:* Thomas Karahonton (*dit* Gros Thomas), Iroquois guide; Laxard Tacanajazé, Iroquois; Thomas Nahanajaze; Francois Monegon; Thomas Anackera; Sauveur St. Martin, Canadian; Thomas Cadrant, Half-breed; Joseph Dinduvant.

**Beaulieu**, at the mouth of Salt River (16 July): "guide and hunter to Sir John Franklin on his second overland journey, and who has built a house at the mouth of Salt River," supplying salt and ice to travellers (p. 149).

**Hare Indians**, the Ramparts (28 July): "a large body… encamped," complaining of a poor fishing season; described generally as "a tribe of the Tinne or Chepewyan nation," "a timid race, [who] live in continual dread of the Eskimos" (p. 211–212). No individual named.

**A Hare Indian killed by a brown bear** (recalled near Fort Good Hope, undated): "having set several snares for bears, went to visit them alone… found a large bear, caught by the head and leg, and endeavoured to kill it with arrows"; found by his son (about sixteen) "torn limb from limb, and mostly eaten, except the head." Neither father nor son is named in the text (p. ~217, OCR-doubtful digit — printed running head reads "21g," almost certainly "217" given the surrounding sequence 216→218).

**Kutchin (Loucheux)** met in small starving parties along the river, 30 July: "seemingly all in want of provisions, owing to the high water spoiling their fishery… These families are the most easterly of the Kutchin" (p. 221). No individuals named at this point. The ethnographic Chapter XII (pp. 377–413) discusses Kutchin customs, band-names and a chief's portrait plate captioned (badly garbled by OCR) apparently "…Chief of the Kutcha-Kutchin" at p. 391 — the name that should read "Saviah" per the List of Plates (p. x) is not legible with confidence in the running OCR text itself; treat the personal name **Saviah** as coming from the plate caption in the front-matter List of Plates only, *not* as independently confirmed in the chapter text.

**Inuvialuit/Inuit on the coast** (Mackenzie estuary eastward, 3–11 August) — no personal name is recorded for anyone met on the coast; individuals are given only by description:
- **An old woman**, Point Atkinson (6 Aug.): gave Richardson five or six herring-salmon; "not exceeding four feet" in height when standing, though seated she looked merely "a little bowed down" (p. 254–255).
- **An old man**, Point Atkinson (6 Aug.): approached slowly, feigned age, infirmity and near-blindness "which was by no means the truth," then asked Rae to blow in his ears and touch his eyes as a charm against the strangers' possible ill influence; his two adult sons had been seen the day before (p. 256–257).
- **Another, still older, woman, and an aged blind man**, who joined the village on 6 August (p. 258).
- **Three young men** who brought ducks to trade, 6 August, "modest and cheerful manners… neither forward nor troublesome" (p. 258–259).
- A man on the coast who lied about seeing "white men," pointing to Richard's Island (where Richardson had himself just been) as inhabited by such a party, "clearly to induce us to put about and go on shore" (p. 241).

**Eskimos recognised from Simpson's 1838 voyage**, at the Rae River ferry crossing (4 Sept.): "two mentioned by Mr. Simpson, one having a wen on his forehead, and the other being a very old man who walked on crutches" — the man with the wen, described as the group's leader, was paid two hatchets for ferrying the party (p. 312–313).

**Indians met in the Dismal Lakes country** (13 Sept.): an unnamed "party of Indians" who had seen the messenger James Hope five days before; a young man from the camp was engaged to guide the expedition the rest of the way to Fort Confidence (p. 333–335). Not named.

**James Hope**: repeatedly mentioned as a messenger sent ahead with "two or three Indians" to leave word at the Kendall River — his own status (Canadian, Métis, or Indigenous) is not stated in the pages read; he is named but his ethnicity is not given (p. 329–330, 333).

### What Richardson records being told by Inuit/Dene about ships, strangers, or wreckage

- **Fort Good Hope, 29 July 1848** (told by Mr M'Beath, an HBC officer, relaying Kutchin report): "a rumour of guns having been heard on the coast of the Arctic Sea, and supposed to have been fired from the Discovery ships, originated in a story brought by the Kutchin or Loucheux to Peel's River Fort, but that the officer in charge placed no reliance upon it" (p. 214).
- **Along the coast, 3 August onward** (told directly by Inuvialuit people encountered): "Our inquiries were directed chiefly to obtaining information of the Discovery Ships, but the Eskimos, one and all, denied having ever seen any white people, or heard of any vessels having been on their coast" (p. 241–242); repeated at Point Atkinson and elsewhere, e.g. "These people… declared that no large ships nor boats had been seen on their coasts, and that we were the first white men they had ever beheld" (p. 261).
- **Retrospective, 1849** (told to Rae the following year, reported by Richardson writing up in 1850–51): "In the following summer of 1849 Mr. Rae ascertained that the Eskimo inhabitants of Wollaston Land had seen neither the ships nor white men" (p. 305–307).

## F. What Richardson concluded, and what he handed on to Rae

**On the cause of the failed crossing:** the ice, not the party's own conduct, was to blame — see the quotations in Section C. He adds the broader hope/conjecture: "It can be stated only as a conjecture… that Sir John Franklin entered Lancaster Sound at the close of a group of warm years… and that since then the annual heat has attained its minimum, probably in 1847 or 1848, and may now be increasing again… the zealous and enterprising officers now on his track[,] will not encounter obstructions equal to those which prevented their… predecessor" (p. 302–304).

**On what the voyage had proved:** "One great purpose of the expedition which I conducted along the coast was to afford relief to such detached parties, or to the entire crews, had they directed their way to the continent, and our researches proved at least that none of the party, having gained that coast, were dragging out a miserable existence among the Eskimos, without the means of repairing to the fur posts" (p. 306).

**Effect on Admiralty strategy:** "In the following summer of 1849 Mr. Rae ascertained that the Eskimo inhabitants of Wollaston Land had seen neither the ships nor white men. The knowledge of these facts had an influence with the Admiralty in concentrating the future search in the vicinity of Melville Island; Captain Collinson and Commander Pullen being directed to approach its coasts from the westward, while Captain Austin… were to trace the Discovery ships from the eastward" (p. 306–307).

**The handoff.** Volume I does not narrate a formal handoff scene — that belongs to Volume II. But the printed instructions (Section B) already specified that if a second summer were needed, the party (by implication, now under Rae, since Richardson's own 1849 return is not narrated here) was to redescend the Coppermine on the 1849 break-up, examine the Wollaston/Victoria Land passages, and return to Great Bear Lake by September 1849 (p. 29). A depot of two men was deliberately left at the west end of Great Bear Lake over the winter to assist "parties travelling to and fro" in 1849 (p. 338) — the one piece of Volume I's own text that is explicitly forward-looking to Rae's continued search.
