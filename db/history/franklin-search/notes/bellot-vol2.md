## Findings So Far

### 1. DISCOVERY OF THE STRAIT, April 6-7, 1852

**Location and Date**: April 7, 1852, near Brown's Island (probably Boss's Long Island)

**Key Entry - Page 175-176**:
On April 7, Bellot and J. Smith explored north of "Brown's Island." 
- Saw "an irregular strait between the island on which we had encamped and the land further west, which is probably Boss's Long Island" (p. 175-176)
- His previous impulse on April 7: "Having come with the preconceived and deeply cherished notion of finding a passage, my first impulse was an exclamation of joy at the sight of the sea; but the sun shining directly above it showed that it lay to the south, and not to the west" (p. 174-175)
- He mistook the water for a lake initially
- Found "irregular strait" on return journey (p. 175)

**Interpretation**: Bellot discovered the strait but seemed confused about whether it was a true passage or just a bay. He thought it led south, not west. Kennedy is noted as going westward separately to "look for a passage to the western sea" on April 7.

### 3. WINTER AT BATTY BAY 1851-52

**November-December 1850**

**The Teetotal Ship** (Page 374-375, letter dated May 15, 1851):
Bellot writes: "We should really not have known where to stow all that kindness presses upon us, were we not vowed to the teetotal principle; that is to say, the most absolute temperance. No wine, no beer, no cider in our stores: no spirits, save in the apothecary's shop."

He adds with French wit: "(O, Bacchus! O, Pomona! turn away your eyes, ye vengeful divinities of my dear Saintonge! Spare your faithless child!)"

**Health and Food** (Page 59-60, December 3-4 entries):
- Main enemy was not cold but damp, which "occasions scurvy and rheumatism"
- Extensive efforts to ventilate ship and prevent moisture
- Diet critical: "The means of resisting cold consist not alone in a judicious economy of fuel or in clothes, but still more in a well-devised system of feeding"
- Required "great consumption of animal substances — of those which contain the most sugar and fat"
- Average temperature in officers' cabins: 20°C (68°F)
- Fires put out at bedtime; no night-time heating

**Bellot's Relationship with Kennedy** (Page 31, November 3 entry):
"I love and admire this excellent man; so truly pious and Christian, so energetic and devoted."

Kennedy described (p. 375) as "a captain in the Hudson's Company's service; a man of an ancient stock; a scion of those Puritans whose dauntless courage has its source in the most lively faith"

**Crew** (Page 375): 
- "Hardy Scots of the Orcades, or Shetland Isles"
- John Hepburn: had followed Franklin on Coppermine and Mackenzie Rivers
- Leask: ice-master, pilot of North Star, knew "Baffin and Barrow Straits as well as you do your library"
- Gideon Smith: skilled at building snow houses

### 6. BELLOT'S PERSPECTIVE AS A FRENCHMAN - CRITICAL COMMENTARY ON ENGLISH EXPEDITIONS

**Location**: Page 50-51, December 1 entry

**Main observations**:
"The most erroneous notions are entertained in France as to the information possessed by the English Admiralty."

**Specific criticisms**:
1. "The Hudson Bay Company, which makes a mystery of all its operations, will not allow anything to be published"
2. English naval expeditions "has possessed the means of travelling by  land"
3. "their men, spoiled by a winter passed by the fireside, did not accomplish all that might have been expected of them"
4. "It does not appear that they ever had with them Hudson Bay men — regular Arctic travellers; they had men experienced in navigation among the ice — good ice-masters — but that is all"
5. "in this country, where the ground is solid during two-thirds of the year, a voyage of discovery should be prosecuted by land as well as by  sea"

**Bellot's balanced view**: Despite criticism, "I have too high an esteem for Sir Edward Parry and the two Rosses: I do not attack them, but criticise a system, the defects of which nothing could make plain to them"

**French phrase found**:
- "à la montagne russe" (like a Russian mountain/roller coaster) - p. 39, November 14 entry, describing the pleasure of sliding down glaciers

**Reference to home region**:
- "my dear Saintonge" - his native region in France (p. 375, in letter context)

## KEY QUOTATIONS (under 70 words each, with page numbers)

**1. The Strait Discovery (April 7, 1852) - Page 175-176**
"On our journey homeward, we found an irregular strait between the island on which we had encamped and the land further west, which is probably Boss's Long Island."
*Context*: Bellot thought the water was a lake initially; Kennedy was exploring westward looking for passage to sea at same time.

**2. Initial Reaction to Discovering Water (April 7) - Page 174-175**
"Having come with the preconceived and deeply cherished notion of finding a passage, my first impulse was an exclamation of joy at the sight of the sea; but the sun shining directly above it showed that it lay to the south, and not to the west."
*Context*: Bellot couldn't determine true direction; at mid-day sun was south, not west.

**3. Winter - Admiration for Kennedy (November 3, 1850) - Page 31**
"I love and admire this excellent man; so truly pious and Christian, so energetic and devoted."
*Context*: After Kennedy's statement: "here we are in Batty Bay, all in good health and spirits; how can we be otherwise than grateful?"

**4. Winter - The Teetotal Ship (May 15, 1851 letter) - Page 374-375**
"We should really not have known where to stow all that kindness presses upon us, were we not vowed to the teetotal principle; that is to say, the most absolute temperance. No wine, no beer, no cider in our stores: no spirits, save in the apothecary's shop."

**5. Winter - Damp and Diet (December 3-4, 1850) - Page 59-60**
"The real enemy, when wintering on board ship in the Arctic regions, is not the cold, but damp, which occasions scurvy and rheumatism. The means of resisting cold consist not alone in judicious economy of fuel or clothes, but still more in a well-devised system of feeding."

**6. Bellot as Frenchman - Critique of English System (December 1, 1850) - Page 50-51**
"The most erroneous notions are entertained in France as to the information possessed by the English Admiralty. Not one of its naval expeditions has possessed the means of travelling by land, and it is not surprising that each has produced so little, since their season of operations was always restricted to very short time."

**7. Winter - French Wit on Temperance (May 15, 1851 letter) - Page 375**
"(O, Bacchus! O, Pomona! turn away your eyes, ye vengeful divinities of my dear Saintonge! Spare your faithless child!)"
*Context*: Bellot's humorous lament about teetotal principle, invoking gods of wine (Bacchus) and fruit (Pomona) of his native Saintonge region.

**8. Bellot's Death - The Farewell Letter (September 9, 1853) - Page 379-381**
"If you receive this letter I shall have ceased to exist, but shall have quitted life in the performance of a mission of peril and honour...death may probably draw upon the different members of my family the consideration of men, and the blessings of Heaven."
*Note*: Written before "one of the most perilous events of his voyage (see Journal, date 10th September)"; recovered two years after the letter's date (circa 1855).
