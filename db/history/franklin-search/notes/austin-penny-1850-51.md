# Austin's squadron and the *Prince Albert*, 1850–51

Working notes drawn from two on-disk OCR texts:

- **Osborn** = Sherard Osborn, *Stray Leaves from an Arctic Journal; or, Eighteen Months in the Polar Regions, in Search of Sir John Franklin's Expedition, in the Years 1850-51* (New York: G. P. Putnam, 1852). File: `db/history/franklin-search/files/franklin-search-osborn-strayleaves.txt`. Osborn commanded the screw steam tender HMS *Pioneer* in Captain Horatio T. Austin's four-ship squadron (*Resolute*, *Assistance*, *Pioneer*, *Intrepid*), plus the transport *Emma Eugenia*.
- **Snow** = W. Parker Snow, *Voyage of the Prince Albert in Search of Sir John Franklin* (London: Longman, 1851). File: `db/history/franklin-search/files/franklin-search-snow-princealbert.txt`. Snow sailed as Lady Franklin's private secretary/supernumerary in the ketch *Prince Albert* under Commander Charles Codrington Forsyth, RN, 1850.

Page numbers are the **printed** page numbers preserved in the djvu running heads. OCR is raw; quotations are given as printed (including the OCR's rendering of names — e.g. Osborn prints "J. Hart well" for John Hartnell, "Ommaney"/"Ommanney", "Uppernavik", "M'Clintock").

## A note on coordinates in these tables

**Neither author gives a running position table.** Osborn states almost no latitudes or longitudes at all; Snow gives roughly a dozen noon positions. Therefore:

- Coordinates printed **without** a marker are **read from the text** (the author states them).
- Coordinates marked **`[inferred from place name]`** are *not* in the source. They are modern coordinates for the place the author names, supplied here so the tables can be plotted. They are approximations of the place, **not** of the ship's position.
- A few are marked `[inferred from stated bearing/distance]` — computed from a bearing-and-distance the author *does* give from a named landmark.

Everything in the "Note" column, and every date, comes from the text.

## C. The Beechey Island discovery, August 1850

### C.1 Sequence of events and dates

| Date (1850) | Who | What |
|---|---|---|
| 5 Aug | Austin | *Assistance* (Capt. Erasmus Ommanney) ordered, with *Intrepid* (Lieut. Bertie Cator), to examine the north shore of Lancaster Sound and Wellington Channel and rejoin between Cape Hotham and Cape Walker (Osborn p. 67–68) |
| 14 Aug | — | *Assistance* and *Intrepid* detached from the squadron off Wolstenholme (so Ommanney's own notice; Osborn dates the parting to the midnight ending 14 Aug). They examine Wolstenholme Sound and find the *North Star*'s winter quarters — nothing to support Adam Beck's tale (Osborn p. 82; Snow p. 315) |
| **23 Aug** | **Ommanney, with the officers of *Assistance* and *Intrepid*** | **Landed at Cape Riley** and found "traces of an encampment": rag, rope, broken bottles, a long-handled dredging instrument, marks of a tent-place. A cairn was then seen on Beechey Island; the *Intrepid* went to it, "the cairn torn down, every stone turned over, the ground underneath dug up a little, and yet, alas! no document or record found." A second tent-place found a short distance within Cape Riley; coast examined up to Cape Innis; a cairn built at the base of Cape Riley with a document in it; the two vessels then crossed towards Cape Hotham (Osborn p. 82–83; Ommanney's notice, dated on board *Assistance* off Cape Riley 23 Aug, quoted verbatim in Snow p. 315) |
| ~24 Aug | Americans, then Penny | The American squadron (Lieut. E. J. De Haven, *Advance* and *Rescue*) communicates with Ommanney's division first and hears of the traces; the Americans then inform Penny, who was pushing for Wellington Channel. Penny catches the *Assistance*, sees the traces, and returns "to take up the search from Cape Riley like a blood-hound" (Osborn p. 83–84) |
| **25 Aug** | De Haven | *Advance* touches at Cape Riley "on the morning of the 25th, to examine a cairn," leaves her own notice, and proceeds to Cape Hotham (De Haven's notice, quoted in Snow p. 316 footnote). Osborn independently dates the Americans reaching Cape Riley to 25 Aug, "close on the heels of the 'Assistance' and 'Intrepid'" (p. 108) |
| 25 Aug | Snow / Forsyth | *Prince Albert* lands Snow at Cape Riley; he copies Ommanney's and De Haven's notices, deposits his own, and searches (see B above and quote 6 below) |
| ~25–26 Aug | Penny | At **Cape Spencer**, Penny finds the ground-plan of a tent, its floor "neatly and carefully paved with small smooth stones," bird bones and meat-canister remnants — a shooting station and look-out commanding Barrow's Strait and Wellington Channel — and a scrap of paper written "to be called", "evidently the fragments of an officer's night orders" (Osborn p. 84) |
| ~26 Aug | De Haven | Sledge marks pointing north from Cape Spencer followed on foot by American parties; the trail ceased before Cape Bowden, with one empty bottle and a piece of newspaper the last things found (Osborn p. 85) |
| **26–27 Aug** | **Penny's squadron — parties from the *Lady Franklin* and *Sophia*** | Made fast between Beechey Island and Cape Spencer in what is now called **Union Bay**, where they found the *Felix* schooner (Sir John Ross, with Cmdr. Phillips) already lying. Parties started for Beechey Island and found, on the southern slope, a multitude of preserved-meat tins and **a carefully constructed cairn of layers of meat-tins filled with gravel**; then, along the northern shore, the house embankment, carpenter's and armourer's working-places, washing-tubs, coal-bags, old clothing, rope — **and the three graves** (Osborn p. 85) |
| **27 Aug (evening)** | Osborn | *Pioneer*, towing *Resolute*, past Cape Hurd and Radstock Bay; off Gascoigne Inlet a boat from the *Sophia* with **Capt. Stewart and Dr. Sutherland** brings the whole news. Off Cape Riley a *Pioneer* boat examines the *Assistance*'s record and learns the *Prince Albert* had already visited it. *Pioneer* runs aground rounding Beechey Island that night; "three brigs and a schooner" seen fast to the land ice in Union Bay (Osborn p. 82, 85–86) |
| 28 Aug | Penny / Osborn | "Wednesday, 28th August. — I was awoke by a hearty shake, and Captain Penny's warm 'Good-morning;'" — Penny brings out Sir John Ross's launch *Mary* to lighten the *Pioneer* and offers the *Sophia* to take her stores. Penny reports nothing found to suggest a party in distress retreating, and that Franklin's harbour was "an excellent one"; Wellington Channel blocked, Barrow's Strait to the westward choked, *Assistance* and *Intrepid* visible off Barlow Inlet (Osborn p. 86–87) |
| **29 Aug** | **Osborn / Austin** | *Pioneer* afloat again after forty-eight hours' labour; Osborn walks across the ice of Union Bay to Franklin's winter quarters. He examines the garden, the storehouse ground-plan (north side 61½ feet long), the armourer's cinders, the washing-tubs, and finds **a pair of Cashmere gloves laid out to dry with two small stones on the palms**, there since 1846. Captain Austin assures him the traces are only "the ordinary traces of a winter station" and show no haste in departure (Osborn p. 87–90) |
| — | Osborn | Later walk with Penny from Cape Spencer along the terraces to the sledge-mark rendezvous east of "Erebus and Terror Bay", and a circular tent-site (shooting party) by a brackish lake — again no record (Osborn p. 94–96) |
| 1851 | Mr. John Stuart, surgeon of the *Lady Franklin* | Re-examines the 1850 traces and follows the sledge tracks to **Caswell's Tower** in Radstock Bay, where two carefully constructed cairns were found — **but no record in them** (Osborn p. 176) |

### C.2 What was found, and what was not

**Found on and about Beechey Island** (Osborn p. 85, 87–95): a cairn built of layers of meat-tins filled with gravel on the ridge of the southern slope; a multitude of preserved-meat tins strewn everywhere ("hardly a foot of the beach-line which did not show signs of their having been there before us… usually in the shape of a preserved-meat tin"); the embankment of a house/storehouse with an exterior and interior embankment into which oak and elm scantling had been sunk, coal-sacks and wood-shavings inside, and a carpenter's workshop; carpenter's and armourer's working-places, the latter a heap of cinders and scraps of iron near the beach; washing-tubs made of the ends of salt-meat casks along an old water-course; coal-bags, old clothing, rope; **a garden** on the eastern slope of the ridge, "its neatly-shaped oval outline, the border carefully formed of moss, lichen, poppies, and anemones, transplanted from some more genial part of this dreary region", already dug up in the search; a pair of Cashmere gloves; a flannel; two boarding-pikes, one with a finger nailed on as a direction-post; a small cairn on the crest apparently a meridian mark; the large cairn on the southern verge of the table-land, several times pulled down and dug under and carefully replaced; a paved tent-place at the eastern extreme with a pigmy terrace decorated with moss and poppy plants, and an adjacent shooting-gallery with a large tin marked "Soup and Bouilli" perforated with balls as a target; **the three graves**.

**Not found**: any written record or document of any kind. The cairn on Beechey Island, though "carefully constructed" and conspicuously placed, held nothing — the record that would have said where Franklin went was never there, or never survived.

### C.3 The three graves — the inscriptions as Osborn prints them (p. 91)

> "Sacred to the memory of J. Torrington, who departed this life, January 1st, 1846, on board of H. M. S. 'Terror,' aged 20 years."

> "Sacred to the memory of Wm. Braine, R. M., of H. M. S. 'Erebus;' died April 3d, 1846, aged 32 years. — 'Choose ye this day whom ye will serve.' — Josh, xxiv. 15."

> "Sacred to the memory of J. Hart well, A. B., of H. M. S. 'Erebus;' died January 4th, 1846, aged 25 years. — 'Thus saith the Lord of Hosts, consider your ways.' — Haggai i. 7."

(The third is John Hartnell; "Hart well" is the OCR/compositor's rendering.) Osborn: the graves "like all that English seamen construct, were scrupulously neat," with an oaken head and foot-board to each, and the good taste of the officers had spared them "any long and childish epitaphs, or the doggerel of a lower-deck poet" (p. 90–91).

### C.5 Verbatim passages

**1. The moment the graves were found** (Penny's parties, Beechey Island):

> "the embankment of a house with carpenter and armourer's working-places, washing-tubs, coal-bags, pieces of old clothing, rope, and, lastly, the graves of three of the crew of the 'Erebus' and 'Terror,'—placing it beyond all doubt, that the missing ships had indeed been there, and bearing date of the winter of 1845-46."
> — Osborn, p. 85

**2. What that meant:**

> "We, therefore, now had ascertained the first winter quarters of Sir John Franklin! Here fell to the ground all the evil forebodings of those who had, in England, consigned his expedition to the depths of Baffin's Bay, on its outward voyage."
> — Osborn, p. 85

**3. The cairn that held nothing** (the *Intrepid*'s party, 23 August):

> "we can picture the anxiety with which the steep was scaled and the cairn torn down, every stone turned over, the ground underneath dug up a little, and yet, alas! no document or record found."
> — Osborn, p. 82–83

**4. Osborn ashore on Beechey Island, 29 August:**

> "there was a sickening anxiety of the heart as one involuntarily clutched at every relic they of Franklin's squadron had left behind, in the vain hope that some clue as to the route they had taken hence might be found."
> — Osborn, p. 87

**5. The frustration, stated plainly:**

> "But, no; as sharp eyes, as anxious hearts, had already been there, and I was obliged to be content with the information, which my observation proved to be true, that the search had been close and careful, but that nothing was to be found in the shape of written record."
> — Osborn, p. 88

**6. The silent cliff:**

> "some clue, mayhap, to the information we so dearly longed for,—the route taken by Franklin on sailing hence, whether to Cape Walker or up Wellington Channel. But, no; the silent cliff bore no mark"
> — Osborn, p. 93

**7. The same disappointment at the shooting-party's tent on the way to Caswell's Tower:**

> "Eagerly did we run from one object to the other, in the hope of finding some stray note or record, to say whether all had been well with them, and whither they had gone. No, not a line was to be found. Disappointed, but not beaten, we turned to follow up the trail."
> — Osborn, p. 95

**8. Ommanney's own notice, as Snow copied it at Cape Riley on 25 August** (the only contemporary document of the discovery quoted in either book):

> "This is to certify that Captain Ommanney, with the officers of her Majesty's ships Assistance and Intrepid, landed at Cape Riley on the 23rd of August, 1850, where he found traces of an encampment, and collected the remains of materials which evidently prove that some party belonging to her Majesty's ships have been detained on this spot. Beechey Island was also examined, where traces were found of the same party."
> — Snow, p. 315

**9. Osborn from the top of the cairn, looking at Leopold Island:**

> "This latter spot… looked ridiculously close,—to use a seaman's term, it appeared as if a biscuit might have been tossed upon it; and the thought involuntarily rose to one's mind,—Would to God that, in 1848, Sir James Ross had known that within forty miles of him Franklin had wintered."
> — Osborn, p. 92

**10. The strategic consequence of finding no record:**

> "we should be obliged to take up the three routes which it was probable Franklin might have taken in 1846; viz., S. W. by Cape Walker, N. W. by Wellington Channel, or W. by Melville Island,—a division of force tending to weaken the chance of reaching Franklin as quickly as we could wish"
> — Osborn, p. 93–94

## D. The spring 1851 sledge campaign

### D.1 Dates

- **Autumn 1850 preparation (2 Oct 1850)**: "Lieutenant M'Clintock had very properly urged the necessity of sending travelling parties to forward depots of provisions upon the intended routes of the different parties in 1851: these were this morning despatched" — M'Clintock with Dr. Bradford towards Melville Island; Lieut. Aldrich to Lowther Island via Somerville Island; Lieut. Mecham along Cornwallis Island between Assistance Harbour and Cape Martyr (p. 110). Aldrich back 5 Oct (stopped at Somerville Island); Mecham back 7 Oct; **M'Clintock back 9 Oct, having "placed his depot forty miles in advance, towards Melville"** (p. 112). Osborn's own autumn trip left 10 Oct; 12 Oct was the last autumnal travelling (p. 112–118).
- **First week of April 1851**: Mr. M'Dougall starts with the sledge **"Beaufort"** to replenish M'Clintock's depot and connect the search round the deep bay joining Bathurst and Cornwallis Lands — "for separate islands they were proved by him no longer to be" (p. 156–157).
- **8 April 1851**: routes laid down by the commodore; a fall of temperature on the 6th caused a week's delay (p. 148).
- **12 April 1851**: "the day calm and cold, some 50° below freezing-point" — the sledges mustered and marched in two columns to the starting-point under the north-west bluff of Griffith's Island; tents pitched, luncheon, inspection, a speech, and the leader's thanks to M'Clintock "to whose foresight, whilst in England… we were so entirely indebted for the perfect equipment" (p. 158–159).
- **15 April 1851, evening**: departure. "the breeze slackened, and the temperature only some 14° below freezing-point… a hundred hoarse voices joined in loud cheers, and the divisions of sledges, diverging on their different routes, were soon lost to one another in snow and mist" (p. 159).

### D.2 Force

**Fifteen sledges, 105 officers and men**, leaving 75 souls on board the four vessels — further reduced when Mr. R. C. Allen was sent to search the islands to the westward with the sledge **"Grinnell"** and seven men (p. 157).

### D.4 The arithmetic of the system (p. 153, 157–158)

- One sledge could carry about **forty days' provision**, i.e. an outward journey of about twenty days, "at an average distance of ten miles per diem" — "an extent of coast-line examined by any one sledge of two hundred miles" (p. 153).
- With relays and depots: the support feeds the long party for ten days, then forms a ten-day depot; two supports give the long party fifteen days (150 miles) plus its own twenty days (200 miles) — "makes in all a journey outward of thirty-five days, or **350 miles from the ships**" (p. 158).

### D.5 Distances, furthest points, results

| Party | Out | Result |

Reached **Winter Harbour** and visited all the points known to Parry's squadron — Bushman Cove, Cape Dundas — and searched beyond Winter Harbour. No traces of Franklin, but brought back **a portion of Parry's last wheel** and the hides and heads of musk-oxen; eighty head of oxen and reindeer counted. | **Dr. Bradford**, *Resolute* | 80 days (returned with M'Clintock) | Searched the **eastern side of Melville Island**. Knocked up early by a fall; "for nearly a month the gallant officer was dragged upon his sledge" but carried out the object of the journey (p. 174) |
| **Lieut. R. D. | **Lieut. Browne** | detached 22–24 April | Sent by Ommanney from Cape Walker "to examine the coast of Cape Walker Land, down the channel to the southward" (p. 164). Osborn gives no distance or return date for him |

**Landmarks along the southern route**: Cape Walker, "full 1000 feet in altitude", red sandstone and conglomerate, the eastern and most lofty extreme of a land trending S.W. on its northern coast and S. on its eastern shore; no traces of Franklin in its neighbourhood (p. 164). Sixty miles west of Cape Walker the country passes from red sandstone to limestone (p. 167). On 14 May Ommanney and Osborn from a table-land saw "a wide break in the continuation of the land… a channel full twenty-five miles wide" (p. 169–170). A musk-ox's frozen foot-prints were the only ones seen; about four brace of ptarmigan shot on the whole southern journey (p. 170).

**Casualties**: sixteen men and one officer out of thirty snow-blind at Snow-blind Point on 1 May, in a tent at 18° below zero (p. 167). Over the whole campaign "The total number of accidents from frost-bites amounted to **eighteen**, and amongst them were several cases in which portions of injured feet had to be amputated; only one man had fallen, **John Malcolm, a seaman of the 'Resolute'**", who "appears to have been delicate from the outset, having fainted on his road to the place of inspection and departure, in April, 1851" (p. 173).

**Penny's parallel result**: "on May 16th, Captain Penny had discovered a great extent of water northward of Cornwallis Island"; Captain Stewart of the *Sophia* stopped by a heavy sea beating against precipitous cliffs; Dr. Goodsir's retreat nearly cut off when a southerly breeze blew the ice out to seaward — "and all this, a month before any thing like a disruption had taken place in Barrow's Strait" (p. 175). The disruption in Barrow's Strait came about 25 June 1851 (p. 176).

## E. Named Inuit and Greenlandic people, and Carl Petersen

### "Frederick" — Esquimaux pilot, Whale-Fish Islands (Osborn)

Shipped from the Whale-Fish Islands for the boat trip to Disco with Ommanney's party, starting 21 June 1850 at 2 a.m. He navigated by dead reckoning from the northernmost rock of the group, "which our guide assured us would sink below the horizon the moment of our arrival off Godhaab. He was perfectly right." To every question about Disco he answered only "by and by you see." "He liked rum and biscuit, and was only to be animated by the conversation turning upon seals, or **poussies**, as the natives call them." (Osborn p. 27)

### The unnamed old man at the Whale-Fish Islands and his family (Osborn)

Pressed to act as pilot to Disco, he declined because his wife was very ill and there was no one else to care for the "piccaninny". Osborn and the doctor entered his winter house: his wife, 28 years old but looking thirty-six, lay by a seal-oil lamp brewing medicine for rheumatism; he was 34 and looked forty-four; they had married when she was 20, "the usual age for marriage". Four children — a girl and three fine boys; the daughter was making shoes for her brothers out of cured skin. Osborn gave her a doll sent for the purpose by Mrs. W. of Woolwich, and next day the father came aboard with a small pouch the child had sewn in return. (Osborn p. 22–23)

### **Caloosa** = **Erasmus York** — the "Arctic Highlander" of Cape York (Snow *and* Osborn)

The single most fully documented Inuk in either book, and the two accounts interlock.

**Snow** (p. 192–193, 224–228), at Cape York on **13 August 1850**: two natives ran out over the ice to the landing party. "one seemed of a more inquisitive nature than the other, and that he was withal more pliable, and capable of yielding to impressions. This man's name was afterwards ascertained to be 'Caloosa'… He was, comparatively speaking, a fine-looking young man, and indeed, like all his fellows, always upon the grin". Snow judged him about eighteen to twenty; he found him very difficult to lift at all (Snow was weighing the natives by hand).

The **next day, 14 August**, Ommanney came aboard the *Pioneer* with Caloosa: "not only had he come on board to act as a sort of pilot in pointing out the spot where the vessel had wintered last year, but that he was actually going to remain under the captain's own personal care". The Danish interpreter put the question to him and his companions "as to whether he would like to forsake his natural home and be henceforth among strangers; and both himself and his friends not only agreed to it, but seemed to be much pleased thereat, stating that he was a young man without father or mother, and having no wife. He was, therefore, at once shipped for the cruise, and he parted from his friends with the most stoical indifference."

He recognised Snow at once, and answered "How d'ye do?" in English "with as clear an articulation"; he could not manage "Very well, thank you," getting only "Wer-well-you," and laughed aloud and ran forward. He mimicked Snow parting his hair. **The renaming**: "I soon ascertained that Caloosa was already named anew. As he came from Cape York, his new patron, Captain Ommanney, called him **'York'**, to which another Christian title was added by some of the officers." (Snow p. 227) — i.e. **Erasmus** York, after Erasmus Ommanney.

**Osborn** on the same man, as **Erasmus York**:
- 14 Aug 1850: "our new shipmate, the Esquimaux from Cape York" — Osborn observed that Petersen and the Cape York native understood one another far better than the native could understand Adam Beck (p. 72).
- "our Arctic Highlander, **Erasmus York**, who, as I before said, had shipped as interpreter on board the 'Assistance'" (p. 105).
- The *Intrepid*'s people found many unburied dead in the Esquimaux huts by the *North Star*'s winter quarters in Wolstenholme Sound. "**Poor York, who, amongst the dead, recognized his own brother, described the malady of which they died as one of the chest or lungs**: at any rate, the mortality was great." (p. 202)
- On his geography and origins: "The Arctic Highlander, Erasmus York, who was serving in our squadron, seemed to believe his mother to have dwelt about Smith's Sound: all his ideas of things that he had heard of, but not seen, referred to places northward. He knew a musk-ox when shown a sketch of one, and said that they were spoken of by his brethren: **with a pencil he could sketch the coast-line northward of where he embarked, Cape York, as far as Whale Sound, or even farther, by tradition; but southward he knew of nothing**." (p. 202)
- 1851: "Cape Stair… finding it to be an island, as the Cape York Esquimaux, on board the 'Assistance,' had led us to believe" — his information verified in the field (p. 188).

### **Cheepchow** — the Esquimaux postman (Snow)

At Cape York, 13 August 1850, a third man came running over the ice dragging a sledge without dogs, and handed over a document "well taken care of, and wrapped in a fold of sealskin" — a notice from Captain Penny, who had called there the day before and gleaned nothing. "The bearer of the despatches was a much older and taller man than the other two, and he was, moreover, far from being so good-looking. His hair was more ragged, and his features furrowed… He evidently seemed conscious of the important position he there held, as her Majesty's Arctic postman". Snow put him at about thirty-six to forty. He appeared to be the father of Aladoonga. (Snow p. 193–194) Later, aboard, "Cheepchow" carefully wrapped a gift in a piece of paper and stowed it in his sealskin dress (Snow p. 202).

### **Aladoonga** — a boy of about ten (Snow)

"as fine a chubby-faced boy as I would wish to see; with features well and evenly formed, a fine set of teeth, brilliant eyes"; Snow found him "about twice as heavy as an ordinary boy of the same height and apparent age would weigh in England", and got his name by repetition. Snow gave him a blue cotton handkerchief spotted white; the boy showed no pleasure and went off to his father. Snow wanted to take him to England, both for the boy's own sake and as an interpreter for Boothia the following summer — "But, poor little fellow! it was not so ordained. Away he went from me with his handkerchief round his neck, as I vainly tried to call him back". (Snow p. 195–197)

### **Adam Beck** — Sir John Ross's South Greenland interpreter, in the *Felix*

Brought to the Cape York ice by Commander Phillips and four men from the *Felix* (Snow p. 194). On **14 August 1850** he gave the officers a circumstantial story of the destruction of a European party — "a cock-and-bull story of blood, fire, wreck, and murder". Osborn is savage: "**Adam Beck — may he be branded for a liar! — succeeded, this day, in misleading a large number of Her Majesty's officers (as his attested document proves), and in detaining, for two days, the squadrons in search of Franklin.**" Osborn's evidence: Petersen and the Cape York native understood one another better than the native understood Adam Beck; and Petersen had already communicated with those same natives before the squadron came up "and that no such bloody tale had been told him; in fact, it was the pure coinage of Adam Beck's brain, cunningly devised to keep, at any rate, his own ship on a coast whither he could escape to the neighbourhood of his home in South Greenland." (Osborn p. 72–73)

Snow's version is more measured: the story stunned everyone; Forsyth objected "how is it that Penny, with his interpreter — a man well versed in the Esquimaux language — heard nothing of this?"; but the counter-argument urged was that the natives "would not tell any man speaking their own language, unless he be one of their own kind; and that they would most assuredly have sooner imparted to Adam such news than to Captain Penny, or his interpreter." Ommanney was sent in the *Intrepid* to fetch Penny and Petersen back to Cape York; "As for poor Adam, who was on board, he remained just as firm as ever, and stoutly maintained his fiction. Between him and Petersen (the Danish interpreter) there was a sort of bandying of words in explanation" (Snow p. 208–224). One useful fact did come out of it: that the *North Star* had wintered in Wolstenholme Sound, "or 'Petowack'", and that the natives had been on board her (Osborn p. 73).

### **Mr. (Carl) Petersen** — the Danish interpreter with Penny

- Fetched from Penny's squadron by the *Intrepid* on 13–14 August 1850 specifically to test Adam Beck's story, and the man who broke it. Osborn: "some who have been 'gulled' in England may thank Mr. Petersen that a carrier-pigeon freighted with a cock-and-bull story of blood, fire, wreck, and murder, was not despatched on that memorable day." (Osborn p. 72–73)
- He interviewed Caloosa and his companions before Caloosa was shipped, to make sure the young man understood what he was agreeing to (Snow p. 225–226).
- Snow consulted him on the cause of the "Crimson Cliffs" colouring, and found him in agreement with the received explanations (Snow p. 228).
- **April 1851**: when the sledge campaign was postponed a week, Osborn visited Penny's winter quarters at Assistance Harbour "on the dog-sledge of Mr. Petersen, who happened to be on board our vessel at the time. Nothing, I conceive, can be more exhilarating than dog-sledging in the Arctic regions on a fine day". He records Petersen's running conversation with the dogs, "who well knew their names, as in turn **Sampson! Caniche! Foxey! Terror!**" — and enjoyed Petersen's talk as much as the drive (Osborn p. 148–149).

### Other Inuit encountered but not named

- The natives of **Button Point, Pond's Bay** (21 Aug 1850): away inland killing deer and salmon; unroofed winter habitations, whale-bone rafters, and paired cairns marking seal-blubber caches, which the *Pioneer*'s party pulled down one after another before discovering their purpose. Osborn erected a cairn, left a record and letters "for the natives to put on board whalers at a future season", and left presents in the huts and on the caches (Osborn p. 74–75).
- The Esquimaux who came off to the *Pioneer* near Whale Sound about 1 a.m. on **26 August 1851**, who "had been to the northward, looking for pousies (seals)" (Osborn p. 191–192).
- The dead of Wolstenholme Sound, found unburied in their huts by the *Intrepid*'s people — Erasmus York's people, and among them his brother (Osborn p. 202).

## F. Passages reflecting on how much coastline the search was mapping

**Osborn — the running total of Arctic coastline added since 1819:**

> "It was in 1819 that Parry sailed to commence that magnificent series of discoveries which, since completed by Franklin, Richardson, Beechey, the Rosses, Back, Simpson, and Rae, have left us, after thirty-five years of well-spent toil and devotion, in perfect possession of the geographical features of Arctic America, and added three thousand six hundred and eighty miles of coast-line to our Polar charts. Is this nothing?"
> — Osborn, p. 146

**Osborn — the gap Franklin went to close:**

> "Parry, the prince of Arctic navigators, sailed through this very bay, and discovered new lands extending half of the distance towards Behring's Straits, or about 600 miles. To complete the remaining 600 miles of unknown region, Sir John Franklin and his 140 gallant followers had devoted themselves"
> — Osborn, p. 76 (entering Lancaster Sound, 22 August 1850)

**Osborn — what one sledge could map:**

> "A little calculation soon showed that about forty days' provision was as much as any one sledge could take with it, or for an outward journey of about twenty days; which, at an average distance of ten miles per diem, would only give an extent of coast-line examined by any one sledge of two hundred miles."
> — Osborn, p. 153

**Osborn — the scale of the archipelago still to be threaded:**

> "From Hudson's Straits, in latitude 60° N., to Jones's Sound, in latitude 76° N., a distance of 960 miles, we find on the western hand a mass of islands, of every conceivable shape and size, with long and tortuous channels intersecting the land in every direction"
> — Osborn, p. 197

**Osborn — the cost of dividing the force, once Beechey yielded no record** (see also C.5 quote 10): the three possible routes from Beechey Island meant that the "wonderful resources we had now at hand about Beechey Island" could not be concentrated "in one line of search" (p. 93–94).

**Snow — the ground his own expedition was meant to cover:**

> "The object of the expedition was the thorough search of the west coast of Regent Inlet to the bottom of the Gulf of Boothia, together with the western side of Boothia into James Ross Strait, and down to Simpson's Strait."
> — Snow, p. 6

**Snow — the last known position of the *Erebus* and *Terror*, and the distance still to run:**

> "both vessels made their way together as far as latitude 74° 48′, longitude 66° 13′ W. (a position approaching the middle of Baffin Bay, and about 210 miles from the entrance of Lancaster Sound). It was here that they were seen, moored to an iceberg, on the 26th of July, sixty-eight days after their departure from England."
> — Snow, p. 3–4

**Snow — what the charted "coast" of Davis Strait actually was:**

> "The western shores of Davis's Straits, hitherto supposed to be the sea-coast of large tracts of land, are in all probability but the eastern boundaries of islands hiding behind them vast sheets of water… Look at Home Bay, Clyde Inlet, Hogarth Inlet, the channels north of Hudson Bay, Admiralty and Navy Board Inlets too. What a field for discovery and research do they not one and all present."
> — Snow, p. 415

**Snow — on the northern Greenland coast as a supposition rather than a survey:**

> "Greenland: what is it? A mass of islands, or a continent itself? Whence come those enormous glaciers, of miles and miles in extent, that cover the lofty mountains supposed to be the coast line of northern Greenland?"
> — Snow, p. 415

**Osborn — the corollary he drew from the absence of icebergs west of Powell Inlet**, that the northern polar basin holds no extensive land: icebergs occur over only "some 55 degrees of longitude… In fact, for 1375 miles of longitude we have icebergs, and then for 7635 geographical miles none are met with. This interesting fact is, in my opinion, most cheering, and points strongly to the possibility that no extensive land exists about our northern pole" (p. 78).

## Appendix: which coordinates were inferred

To restate plainly, because it matters for any downstream use of the tables above:

- **Read from the printed text (Osborn)**: 100° W longitude at Snow-blind Point (p. 167); latitude 76° 15′ N for Aldrich's furthest on Bathurst Island (p. 173); latitude 76° 52′ N at the inlet near Whale Sound (p. 191); latitude 77° N for the squadron's farthest north (p. 194). Also stated but not used as ship positions: 73° 30′ N as the latitude of Upernavik (p. 50) and 60° N / 76° N as the latitudes of Hudson's Strait and Jones's Sound (p. 197).
- **Read from the printed text (Snow)**: 59° 19′ N, 13° 42′ W (13 June); 64° 41′ N, 53° 34′ W (8 July); 75° 16′ N, 60° 30′ W (24 July); 75° 18′ 18″ N, 60° 21′ W (4 Aug); 67° 42′ N, 55° 42′ W (9 Sept). Also 74° 48′ N, 66° 13′ W for the *Erebus* and *Terror*'s last known position, 26 July 1845 (p. 3–4, not a *Prince Albert* position).
- **Computed from a stated bearing and distance**: Snow's 30 June position (95 miles from Cape Farewell) and his 20 July beset position (Devil's Thumb bearing S.E. by compass, about 30 miles); Snow's 17 Aug position (about 52 miles from Cape Hay).
- **Everything else in both tables is a modern coordinate for the place the author names, not a position either author recorded.** Each such cell carries the marker `[inferred from place name]`. Where the author names only a general area ("Melville Bay", "the West Water"), the coordinate is a rough centroid and is additionally hedged with `~`.
