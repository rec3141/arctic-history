# Belcher's Defence of the Abandonment of His Ships, August 1854

## Summary of Belcher's Argument

Belcher abandoned HMS Assistance and Pioneer in Wellington Channel on August 25, 1854, and ordered Captain Kellett to abandon HMS Resolute and Intrepid. His defence rests on five main points: (1) the ice was cemented permanently shut by antagonistic tides that would prevent southern drift; (2) the vessels faced inevitable destruction from ice pressure—they were trapped in a 40-foot-wide fissure in ice seven feet thick with no hope of escape; (3) the lateness of the season made a third winter impossible; (4) his Admiralty instructions explicitly authorized abandonment when he judged further operations impossible; and (5) his duty to the crews under his command, none of whom volunteered to remain, took precedence over saving ships that "were not portions of our Navy beyond this emblem" of the pendant.

---

## Belcher's Stated Reasons for Abandonment

### 1. Ice Conditions and Tidal Analysis (p. 220, August 1854)

Belcher explains his decision based on tidal analysis proving the ice would not drift south and could not be broken up. He observed antagonistic tidal forces at Cape Bowden where Arctic Ocean and Lancaster Sound currents met, creating compressions in Wellington Channel.

**Quote 1 (p. 221):** "Over an expanse of twenty-four miles these cracks had occurred in as many intervals; but now one solid barrier, cemented in many places by young ice, and where pools occurred on the floe, so hardly frozen as to bear both men and sledge, left but little hope of release until too late in the season to afford any prospect of reaching Beechey Island this year."

### 2. Impossible Escape and Ship Danger (p. 221, August 1854)

The ships were locked in a fissure with only 40 feet of tidal play and already suffering dangerous nips that fractured their docks.

**Quote 2 (p. 221):** "such however was the thickness of the surrounding ice (seven feet), that any decided nip left no hope of saving the vessels: it was too stubborn to bend or break."

### 3. The Final Decision Weighing All Factors (p. 221-222, August 1854)

**Quote 3 (p. 221-222):** "Weighing fully the chances, and the apparent impossibility of any disruption of the ice, without a similar gale to that of the 18th of August last year—that period also having passed; the danger if it did recur; the lateness of the season, with unmistakable symptoms of early winter; and the difficulties which further hesitation might entail,—I decided that I should best fulfil my pledge to their Lordships, and my duty to my country, as well as to the crews under my command (for none volunteered to remain out), by withdrawing them, and proceeding to the 'North Star.'"

### 4. Rebuttal: No Hope of Southern Drift (p. 222, August 1854)

Belcher defends against the theory that ships might drift south, citing evidence from the Breadalbane and his own tidal observations.

**Quote 4 (p. 222):** "Confident I am that those vessels would cease to float before the autumn gales conclude; and from what I saw, and with the habit of judging on all such matters, as this narrative will evince, much more sanguinely than those associated with me, no hope of their southern drift can be entertained,—and for these reasons:—we know of no southern drift; we have never experienced northern gales; we do possess facts to prove the reverse; portions of the 'Breadalbane' reached our Winter Quarters, and that without a gale: that disaster, bearing date the 21st of August, occurring three days after the gale which disturbed the ice of this Channel."

### 5. Emotional Impact on the Crews (p. 223, August 1854)

**Quote 5 (p. 223):** "By those only who have devoted their time and interest even to the merest tub that swims, and has been constituted a home, can our feelings be appreciated. It becomes the child of our adoption, the only object in these remote regions on which we lavish all our affections!"

## Orders to Captain Kellett and His Response

### Belcher's Order to Abandon (April 21, 1854, p. 166)

Following Kellett's April 12 letter asking for explicit orders, Belcher sent definitive instructions:

**Quote 6 (p. 166):** "Having maturely considered the contents, I see no reason for altering my original decision in October last,—that the 'Resolute' or tender should be abandoned, or both. The only consideration now arises from the absence of travelling-parties. You are therefore hereby directed to withdraw from the 'Resolute,' the valuable stores, chronometers and instruments, officers and crew, and repair for further instructions to Beechey Island, leaving Commander M'Clintock to await the return of the absent sledge crews, when he will also abandon that vessel and repair to Beechey Island."

### Kellett's Initial Objection (April 12, 1854, p. 167)

Kellett wanted explicit orders: **Quote 7 (p. 167):** "Had you said to me, That, knowing their Lordships' views, and weighing my altered position from that you expected me to occupy at the time of your writing my orders, it was your direction that I should abandon (my ship) both vessels, all would have been well with me, and you would have had me with you."

### Belcher's Reasoning to Kellett (April 21, 1854, p. 167-168)

**Quote 8 (p. 167-168):** "This abandonment goes mightily against the grain. If we could save even 'Intrepid,' it would be something; but your distance off-shore precludes any movement before the 22nd of August, and that is too late for operations at Beechey Island. No! all must come; no volunteering will satisfy me! If new crews are sent from England to hold 'North Star', then a party could proceed back to extricate one or both."

## His Authority and the Admiralty Orders

Belcher explicitly cites his Admiralty instructions (p. 240):

"when you judged you thought all had been done which could be done for the attainment of these objects, you were not to linger out, but to bring your crews home; and further, 'when you judged you could do no more, would have the courage to act on your judgment, and to return home.'"

On his decision-making authority (p. 240): "Simply, then, I stood with a recorded guarantee before the country, that the issue of the search was vested in me, and that with me it was to terminate."

## Spring 1854 Sledge Journeys

### Commander Richards' Journey (April-July 1854)

**Dates and Coordinates (from pp. 44-52):**
- April 10: Sailed from Northumberland Sound
- April 16: Deception Group (15 miles WSW of Cape Lady Franklin)
- April 24: Cape Fortune, lat. 76° 26' N., long. 103° 33' W.
- April 29: Point Success, lat. 76° 32' N., long. 105° 4' W. (despatches deposited, 120 miles from ship)
- May 6: Northeast point of Melville Island, lat. 76° N., long. 106° 25' W.
- May 11: Continued northwest along Melville Island's north coast (does not extend beyond 76° 48' 30" N.), passed Bays of Promise and Plenty
- May 16: Remarkable cape ~600 feet high (later named in Kellett's honor)
- May 17: Met Lieutenant Hamilton from the Resolute
- May 28: Reached Cape Mudge
- May 31: Met Lieutenant Pirn at a tent
- June 3: Discovered the Resolute and Intrepid
- June 5: Arrived aboard Resolute (alone)
- June 7: Met Captain Kellett upon his return
- June 8: Departed Kellett; traveled east coast of Melville Island up Byam Martin Channel
- June 14: Point King, lat. 75° 56' N. (near Dr. Bradford's furthest)
- June 15: Killed fine buck at Point King
- July 10: Returned to Cape Lady Franklin (found tent and two men left by Osborn, "gone southerly")
- July 11-12: Returned to ship with despatches from Kellett
- Total: 96 days (56 days outward, 35 days return)

### Lieutenant Osborn's Spring 1854 Journey

Osborn was ordered to take the south-western route (p. 42 orders), separating from Richards as circumstances allowed. Belcher notes Richards found "the tent, and two men, left by Lieutenant Osborn, gone southerly" at Cape Lady Franklin on July 10. Osborn returned to the North Star by dog-sledge in June (p. 9315). Limited details of his specific route and coordinates are provided in Belcher's narrative compared to Richards' detailed account.

## On the Court-Martial Question

Belcher states (p. 240): "Nor am I entering now upon any defence, for the judicious termination of my command has never been called in question."

This suggests Belcher's decision to abandon the ships was not seriously challenged officially, though his narrative structure indicates he anticipated criticism and took care to document his reasoning.
