# Schwatka's search, 1878–1880: Gilder's *Schwatka's Search*

Working notes from one on-disk OCR text.

**Source.** W. H. Gilder, *Schwatka's Search: Sledging in the Arctic in Quest of the Franklin Records* (New York: Charles Scribner's Sons, 1881). Gilder was second in command. File: `db/history/_staging/schwatkassearchs00gild.txt` (Library of Congress copy, Internet Archive scan, 12,530 lines). Front matter (Introduction) is Roman-numbered, pp. vii–xii; the narrative runs pp. 1–283; the "Conclusion"/summary chapter pp. 284–290; an appendix on Inuit life and language to p. 307. There is an index at pp. 290–297 (persons and places).

**Page numbers.** All page numbers below were derived from the printed running heads in the scan and checked by hand at the points that matter. One trap: **p. 124 is a full-page plate ("Irving's Grave")**, so the text jumps from p. 123 straight to p. 125 — the Irving medal passage is on **p. 125**, not 123.

**Three corrections to the working assumptions this file was commissioned under:**
1. Gilder's route north **did not go by Chesterfield Inlet**, which appears only as a geographic reference. The overland route ran from Camp Daly up the Connery and Lorillard rivers, then over unmapped country to the newly discovered **Hayes River**, and down that to **Back's (Great Fish) River** at Cockburn Bay.
2. **Lake Franklin and Lake Garry are never mentioned in Gilder.**
3. The March 1880 return was to **Depot Island**, not to Camp Daly (which had been abandoned as a base a year earlier).

## F. What Schwatka found — and did not find

### Graves, skeletons and reburials

- **Two graves of white men** on the coast between Franklin Point and Collinson Inlet: the upper part of a skull lying outside one, and inside the stone pile the upper maxilla with two teeth and a piece of cheek-bone. "these were laid together for burial on our return." (**pp. 122–123**)
- **Crozier's landing camp**, about a mile and a half above the Cape Jane Franklin camp, found by Klutschak and Melms: several cooking stoves with copper kettles, clothing, blankets, canvas, iron and brass implements, and **an open grave** containing blue cloth (part of a heavy overcoat), canvas coarsely stitched "as though the body had been incased as if for burial at sea," gilt buttons, and the object-glass of a marine telescope. (**p. 123**)
- **The Irving grave**, last week of June 1879 (between the 17 June departure from Cape Herschel and the 30 June departure from Irving Bay; Gilder gives no calendar date). On a stone at the foot of the grave Klutschak found a grimy silver medal 2½ inches across — obverse *GEORGIUS IIII., D. G. BRITTANNIARUM REX, 1820*; reverse a laurel wreath inscribed *SECOND MATHEMATICAL PRIZE, ROYAL NAVAL COLLEGE*, enclosing *AWARDED TO JOHN IRVING. MIDSUMMER, 1830.* Under the head, a figured silk pocket-handkerchief, neatly folded. **Only the skull and a few other bones survived.** (**p. 125**)
- **Disposition of Irving.** The bones were gathered "to be brought away for interment where they may hereafter rest undisturbed" — a reburial on King William Land being futile, since the natives would reopen it (**p. 125**). A monument was raised over the grave on **13 July 1879** (**p. 151**). The remains "were sent to grateful relations in Scotland, and buried with due honor in a graveyard of Lieutenant Irving's native town" (**p. 287**). "Lieutenant Irving's remains were the only ones that could be sufficiently identified to warrant their removal. Had there been others we would have brought them away." (**p. 289**)
- **The Erebus Bay boat place** (south side), late July 1879: the wreck of a ship's boat strewn along the beach, prow and stern-post in good condition, the surviving clinkered boards 28 ft 6 in. long, "showing it to have been a very large boat"; **portions of four skeletons**, reburied the next day. In the summary Gilder notes: "At one place we could distinguish four right femurs." (**pp. 155–157, 284**)
- **An empty grave** about four miles below Point Le Vesconte, with a skull a quarter-mile off, containing only a brass buckle and a percussion cap (**p. 151**); and an officer's grave near Point Le Vesconte, dug below ground and torn open by natives, with wild flowers grown over the debris — reinterred and a monument erected, Schwatka picking a bouquet as a memento (**pp. 289–290**).
- **A grave opened by Esquimaux near Tallock Point**, built of small stones though large ones abounded — which Gilder reads as evidence of the burying party's failing strength (**p. 286**).
- **Starvation Cove.** Klutschak prosecuted the search in the snow-free season and built a monument over the remains, depositing a record. Before he arrived, Joe and some Netchilliks had found a small **pewter medal** (a Great Britain launch medal, 1843), pieces of blanket, and **a skull**: "This was all that could be seen at this memorable spot." (**p. 164**) Five miles inland from the cove the natives found that summer the skeleton of a white man never seen before; Klutschak erected a monument over it, the clothing showing "deceased was a sailor, not an officer" (**p. 210**).
- **The overall claim:** the expedition "buried the remains of every member of that fated party above ground, so that no longer the bleached bones of those unfortunate explorers whiten the coasts of King William Land and Adelaide Peninsula" (**p. 239**).

### The two boat places — a distinction that matters

1. **Starvation Cove / the boat place west of Richardson Point**, Adelaide Peninsula (Netchillik *Nu-oo-tar-ro*). Visited 1 June 1879 under snow, re-searched by Klutschak in summer. Seeuteetuar said the boat lay upside down on the beach with all the skeletons beneath it, "about five or more" (**p. 89**); Ogzeuckjeuwock, who was there as a boy, described it right side up with the tin case of books inside. **This is where the records were lost.**
2. **The Erebus Bay boat place**, King William Land — McClintock's boat, four skeletons, no records.

### Notable relics recovered

- The **Irving medal** (silver, Royal Naval College Second Mathematical Prize, 1830) — pp. 125, 287
- The **pewter *Great Britain* launch medal** from Starvation Cove — pp. 164, 287
- **The sled** on which the Erebus Bay boat had been carried, bought from the Netchilliks — since cut down, it had been "seven feet longer than at present" (**p. 94**); the Introduction lists "the prow of the boat seen by Sir Leopold McClintock in Erebus Bay, the sled on which it had been transported, and the drag-rope by which the sled was drawn" (**p. xii**)
- **McClintock's holograph copy of the Crozier record**, found by Toolooah's wife in the ruins of Hobson's cairn — pp. 126–129. *This is the only paper Schwatka recovered, and it is McClintock's, not Franklin's.*
- Two sheet-iron Franklin cooking stoves with copper kettles; sledge harness stencilled **"T 11"** (*Terror*); the Greenhithe stone jug; a brush cut "H. Wilts" (given as "H. Wilkes" in the Introduction, p. xii — an internal discrepancy); a blanket stocking; wire-gauze snow-goggles bought from Adlekok (**pp. 102, 126, xii**)
- From an Inuit cache below Cape Maria Louisa, found by Owanork: a wooden barrel-shaped canteen marked **"NO. 3 / G. B."** under the Queen's broad arrow, cask staves, a small keg, a powder can, red **"GOLDNER'S PATENT"** meat cans, a narrow-bladed axe, bottles stamped "BRISTOL GLASS-WORKS" (**p. 148**)
- A pine board with the initials **"L. F." in brass tacks**, picked up on the west coast of Adelaide Peninsula near where the ship was said to have sunk — "may serve to identify that vessel" (**p. 104**)
- From the Erebus Bay boat: buttons, fish lines, copper and iron bolts and rivets, a sled drag-rope, sheet lead, shot, bullets, wire cartridges, clothing, broken medicine bottles, the charger of a powder-flask, an iron lantern (**p. 156**)
- The whole collection "was offered to the British Admiralty, and, having been gratefully accepted, were added to the relics already deposited at the Museum in Greenwich Hospital, and at the United Service Institution in London" (**p. xii**)

### **No written records were recovered**

Nothing of Franklin's own writing was found. Schwatka's dig for McClintock's 1859 deposit also failed: "though we dug a deep trench four feet wide from the centre of the cairn, due north, for a distance of twenty feet, nothing was found" (**p. 129**). The tall cairns near Cape Felix "had no records in them" (**p. 110**), and Schwatka's seven-foot pillar cairn inland from Cape Felix was taken down "without meeting with any record or mark whatever" (**p. 134**).

The whole premise of the expedition — Capt. Barry's story that a chief "had left these papers behind him in a cairn, where, among other things, some silver spoons had since been found" (**p. 4**) — was demolished at Marble Island in January 1879: "In this crucible of fact the famous spoon melted. So far as Captain Barry and his clews were concerned, we had come on a fool's errand" (**p. 39**); "There being no cairn, as a matter of course there was no guide to conduct us to it" (**p. 40**).

The expedition's claimed result was therefore *negative certainty*:

> "It was the first expedition which established beyond a doubt the loss of the Franklin records. McClintock recorded an opinion that they had perished: Schwatka recorded it as a fact."
> — `p. xi`

> "The most important direct result of the labors of the expedition will undoubtedly be considered the establishing of the loss of the Franklin records at the boat place in Starvation Cove… Next in importance to their recovery must be considered the knowledge of their irrecoverable loss."
> — `p. 240`

> "Had we known at this time what we learned a few days later, the place would have had an additional interest as the spot where the records of the expedition, which had been brought thus far with infinite toil and care, had been irrecoverably lost."
> — `p. 89`

### The Inuit testimony about the papers

**Ikinnelikpatolok**, on the ship frozen in near Grant Point (15 May 1879):
> "They also saw books on board, and left them there. They only took knives, forks, spoons, and pans; the other things they had no use for."
> — `p. 79`

**Peowat**, on the cairn he opened on Montreal Island:
> "…was found to contain a pocket-knife, a pair of scissors, and some fish-hooks, which he took away. He saw no papers anywhere about it."
> — `p. 80`

**Seeuteetuar**, on the Starvation Cove ground (1 June 1879):
> "He had also seen books and papers scattered around among the rocks along the shore and back from the beach."
> — `p. 84`

**Ahlangyah**, on the tent full of dead at the head of Terror Bay:
> "There were knives, forks, spoons, watches, many books, clothing, blankets, and such things. The books were not taken notice of."
> — `p. 92`

**Ogzeuckjeuwock**, who was about twelve when he stood at the Starvation Cove boat — the single most important passage in the book:
> "…he said he saw books at the boat place in a tin case, about two feet long and a foot square, which was fastened, and they broke it open. The case was full. Written and printed books were shown him, and he said they were like the printed ones."
> — `p. 106`

> "Some of the books were taken home for the children to play with, and finally torn and lost, and others lay around among the rocks until carried away by the wind and lost or buried beneath the sand."
> — `p. 108`

**Ockarnawole**, who had walked the Victory Point ground five years before:
> "About Victory Point found a grave, with a skeleton, clothes, and a jack-knife with one blade broken. Saw no books."
> — `p. 110`

Gilder's own inference:
> "It is also a very natural deduction that the books that were found in a sealed or locked tin case, which had to be broken open by the natives, were the more important records of the expedition, and in charge of the chief surviving officers…"
> — `p. 109`

> "The records of these observations were in all probability contained in the tin box which Ogzeuckjeuwock speaks of as having been found and lost beyond recovery."
> — `p. 110`

## H. The record-length sledge journey

Gilder states the claim twice, in nearly the same words.

**Introduction, `p. xi`:**
> "It made the largest sledge journey on record, having been absent from its base of supplies for eleven months and twenty days, and having traversed 2,819 geographical, or 3,251 statute miles."

**Narrative, chapter "Famine," `p. 238`:**
> "During the year that we were absent from the verge of civilization… we had travelled 2,819 geographical, or 3,251 statute miles, most of which was entirely over unexplored territory, constituting the longest sledge journey ever made, both as to time and distance… Our sledge journey stands conspicuous as the only one ever made through the entire course of an Arctic winter."

So: **11 months 20 days; 2,819 geographical / 3,251 statute miles** — from Camp Daly, 1 April 1879, to Depot Island, 4 March 1880 (which is 11 months 3 days; Gilder's figure evidently runs to the end of the Depot Island season).

Supporting superlatives, **p. 239**: "the lowest temperature ever experienced by white men in the field, recording one observation of −71 degrees Fahrenheit, sixteen days whose average was 100 degrees below the freezing-point, and twenty-seven which registered below −60 degrees Fahrenheit, during most of which the party travelled."

Game score, **p. 59**: "the unparalleled score of 522 reindeer, besides musk oxen, polar bears and seals… from the time we left Camp Daly until our return."

## Coordinates: what is stated and what is inferred

**Printed in Gilder** (these are all of them):
- **59°54′ N, 60°45′ W** — Labrador Sea, 19 July 1878 (p. 7)
- **63°51′ N, 90°26′15″ W** — Camp Daly (p. 15)
- **65°45′ N** — noon observation, 21 April 1879, in the interior (p. 65); no longitude given
- **67°32′42″ N** — moon culmination, Wilmot Bay inlet, night of 20 November 1879 (p. 207); no longitude given
- **69°38′ N, 98°41′ W** and **69°37′42″ N, 98°41′ W** — the Crozier record's own figures for the Victory Point landing place, as transcribed by Gilder from McClintock's copy (pp. 127–128); **70°05′ N, 98°23′ W** for where the *Erebus* and *Terror* were beset — again quoted from the record, not observed by Schwatka
- "five degrees west longitude" fragment at p. 106 is OCR noise, not a position

**Everything else in the table above is inferred from the place name**: New York, Nottingham/Salisbury Islands, Whale Point, Depot Island, Marble Island, the Connery/Lorillard/Hayes rivers, Cockburn Bay, Montreal Island, Richardson Point, Starvation Cove, the Pfeffer River, Seaforth Point, Cape Herschel, Washington Bay, Erebus Bay, Franklin Point, Collinson Inlet, Cape Jane Franklin/Irving Bay, Cape Felix, Terror Bay, Cape Crozier, Gladman Point, Sherman Inlet, the Dangerous Rapids, Mount Meadowbank and New Bedford.
