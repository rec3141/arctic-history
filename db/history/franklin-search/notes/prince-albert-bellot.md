## B. The sledge journey of spring 1852, and the discovery of Bellot Strait

**Shape of the journey.** Two preliminary trips laid depots at Fury Beach: a midwinter one by moonlight (5–8 January 1852, Kennedy, Bellot and three men) and a February one. The extended journey proper began when Kennedy left the ship at Batty Bay on **25 February 1852**; Bellot turned back after the first day to bring up a second party and rejoined at Fury Beach on 7 March. Fourteen hands were then at Fury Beach.

- **Departure (final):** 9 a.m., **29 March 1852**, from Fury Beach. Fourteen men set out; eight formed a fatigue party as far as Brentford Bay and turned back on 6 April.
- **The travelling six:** W. Kennedy, Lieut. Bellot, John Smith, Andrew Irvine, Richard Webb, William Adamson — "with five Esquimaux dogs" (K p. 154).
- **Return:** 5 a.m., **30 May 1852**, to the ship in Batty Bay.
- **Distance and duration claimed:** *"after an absence of ninety-seven days, during which we had accomplished a journey of about 1100 miles"* (K p. 152). Counted from the 25 February start this is ~95 days; Kennedy's figure of 97 is as printed. Bellot describes it as four months, February to June (B p. 106).
- **Route:** Fury Beach → south along the west shore of Prince Regent Inlet → Cresswell Bay and Cape Garry (1 April) → Brentford Bay (5 April) → **through Bellot Strait westward (7 April)** → due west across the ice of the "western sea" (Franklin Strait / Peel Sound), 8–10 April → across "Prince of Wales Land" and Arrowsmith's Plains due west to about 100° W → north → Ommanney Bay (24 April) → east to Browne's Bay of Peel Sound (27 April) → north up the coast to **Cape Walker (4 May)** → east across to Limestone Island (7 May) → along the north shore of North Somerset by Cape Rennel, Garnier Bay and Cape McClintock → Whaler Point (15 May) → Batty Bay (30 May). No trace of Franklin anywhere.

### The discovery of Bellot Strait

On **5 April 1852** the party reached Brentford Bay (Sir John Ross's name) and camped on Brown's Island; a column of vapour to the west betrayed open water. On **6 April** two parties examined the bay — Kennedy and Adamson north, Bellot and Smith south; both found a passage leading west. On **7 April** they travelled the northern channel from end to end, about twenty miles with its windings, and from a hill at the western end saw a broad channel running N.N.E.–S.S.W.

> "…until its great extent convinced us that we had fallen upon a western sea or channel, and that the passage we had just gone through was in reality a strait, leading out of Prince Regent Inlet. It appears on the map of our discoveries as Bellot Strait — a just tribute to the important services rendered to our Expedition by Lieutenant Bellot."
> — K p. 132

Also named on this stretch: **Levesque Island** for the land forming the strait's southern shore ("in accordance with the wishes of Lady Franklin"), **Cape Bird** retained for the hill at the west end (Sir James Ross's name), and **Cape Hodgkin** for the headland on the south side of the western entrance, after Dr. Thomas Hodgkin (K pp. 132–133).

### Mainland vs. island — the significance, and what Kennedy got wrong

Bellot Strait separates **Somerset Island** to the north from the **Boothia Peninsula** to the south. Boothia is part of the North American continent, so the land immediately south of the strait's narrows — **Zenith Point on Murchison Promontory (≈71°58′ N, 94°15′ W)** — is the **northernmost point of continental North America**. The strait is therefore the true dividing line between continent and archipelago on this meridian, and it also proves North Somerset to be an island.

Kennedy did not see it this way. He named the southern shore "Levesque **Island**" — i.e. he took the land south of his new strait for an island, not the tip of the continent. Nor did he resolve the geography to the west: he crossed the ice of Peel Sound / Franklin Strait believing he had crossed a channel, walked over land he took for "Prince of Wales Land," and concluded — wrongly — that North Somerset and Prince of Wales Land were joined and that "Franklin could not have passed south of Cape Walker, since the land extends there where formerly it was supposed that the sea existed" (Bellot, B p. 108). The names Kennedy scattered over the "new land" were mostly expunged from his own chart afterwards when Ommanney's prior discoveries became known (K p. 154 n.).

The strait's real significance was demonstrated later: McClintock's *Fox* wintered at its eastern end in 1858–59, and it was from Bellot Strait that the sledge parties set out that found the Victory Point record.

## C. Bellot's death, 18 August 1853

Bellot did not die on this voyage. He went north again the following year. Refused a French expedition of his own, he wrote to the Minister of Marine on 31 March 1853 asking leave to embark in the *Phoenix* (Capt. E. A. Inglefield), left for London almost at once, and **was aboard at Woolwich on 10 May 1853** (B pp. 71–72). The *Phoenix*'s special and urgent business was carrying Admiralty despatches to Sir Edward Belcher, then somewhere up Wellington Channel.

- **Where:** Erebus and Terror Bay (Beechey Island) → up the east shore of **Wellington Channel**, past Cape Innis and Cape Bowden.
- **Circumstances:** Inglefield had left the *Phoenix* two days earlier to look for Capt. Pullen; Pullen reappeared; Bellot, knowing the despatches were urgent, would not wait for Inglefield's return. He conferred with Pullen and **set out on 12 August 1853** with the quarter-master of the *North Star* and three seamen, a sledge and an india-rubber canoe.
- **13–14 August:** camped three miles from Cape Innis, then on detached blocks of ice three miles from Cape Bowden. On the night of the 14th, crossing a four-foot cleft, then trying to land three miles off shore in the canoe against a violent SE gale. Harvey (quarter-master) and Madden got ashore and rigged a pass-rope; on the fourth trip the floe began to drive off shore. Bellot shouted to let go the rope. The floe carried away Bellot and two seamen, **William Johnson and David Hook**.
- **18 August 1853, about 8.15 a.m.:** Bellot lashed up his books, went to see how the ice was driving, and was gone in four minutes. His stick was found across a crack five fathoms wide.

The Memoirs print the depositions of the survivors.

> "I then went to the top of a hill to watch them," says Madden, in his deposition, "and saw them swept away from land towards mid-channel. I watched from that spot for six hours, but lost sight of them in two. When they passed out of sight, the men were standing near the sledge, M. Bellot on the top of the hummock. They seemed to be on a very solid piece of ice."
> — B p. 78

> "M. Bellot," he says, "sat for half an hour in conversation with us, talking on the danger of our position. I told him I was not afraid… He replied, 'I know they were: and when the Lord protects us not a hair of our heads shall be touched.'"
> — Johnson's deposition, B pp. 78–79

> "He said, 'About a quarter past eight a.m.' (Thursday the 18th), and then lashed up his books, and said he would go and see how the ice was driving. He had only been gone about four minutes… on returning to our shelter saw his stick on the opposite side of a crack, about five fathoms wide, and the ice all breaking up. I then called out, 'Mr. Bellot!' but no answer."
> — Johnson's deposition, B p. 79

Hook deposed that when someone suggested it would be more prudent to keep to the middle of the channel, Bellot answered that Pullen's orders were to keep along the coast to the right, within about two miles of it — which the biographer takes as the making of the man: "a slave to duty, sacrificing his own safety to it" (B p. 80).

## D. Short verbatim quotes

**Lady Franklin fitting the ship out**

> "The 'Prince Albert,' originally destined, I believe, for the fruit trade, to and from the Azores, had been purchased by Lady Franklin from Messrs. White and Co. of Cowes, and sent to Aberdeen to undergo additional strengthening and the alteration of her rig under the hands of Mr. Duthie, the shipbuilder, for an Arctic voyage."
> — Kennedy, `p. 36`

> "There, in our little cabin with her estimable neice, sat the truly feminine yet heroic spirit who presided over our gallant little enterprise… If ever three English cheers were given with the heart's best feelings of a British sailor, they were given, when stepping over the vessel's side, our noble patroness waved us her last adieu and God's blessing on our voyage."
> — Kennedy, on the sailing from Stromness, `p. 39`

> "…my admiration of that unwearied and unexampled devotion with which she has sacrificed her health, her strength, and all her worldly means, to the search for her distinguished husband, and her gallant friends and countrymen."
> — Kennedy, Preface, `p. ix`

**Temperance and Hudson's Bay Company method**

> "I have, in conclusion, the satisfaction to remark, that although our crew suffered somewhat from scurvy, they have all returned to a man in comparative health, which I attribute in a great measure to the strictly teetotal principles on which the expedition was carried out, and the consequent harmony and good conduct of the men throughout."
> — Kennedy to the Admiralty, 9 October 1852, `p. 182`

> "We were all teetotallers, that is to say, we had not on board either wine, beer, or spirituous liquors; and I do not hesitate to ascribe in great part to this wise measure the good conduct so steadily maintained by our crew, and the harmony that never ceased to reign in spite of the privations…"
> — Bellot, `p. 93`

> "We adopted, in fact, for our travels the manner of life and the customs of the Esquimaux and the Indians… Garments of skin, and mocassins or sealskin boots, formed our accoutrement; pemmican our only food; sledges, with or without dogs, our means of transport… and a hut constructed of snow our shelter for the night."
> — Bellot, `p. 105`

> "During our absence of three months, we ever slept in snow houses, having dispensed with tents."
> — Kennedy to the Admiralty, `p. 181`

**Dogs and snow-houses**

> "These faithful creatures were perfect treasures to us throughout the journey. They were all suffering like ourselves from snow-blindness, but did not in the least relax their exertions on this account. The Esquimaux dog is in fact the camel of these northern deserts — the faithful attendant of man, and the sharer of his labours and privations."
> — Kennedy, `p. 139`

> "Other layers follow in the same way, until you have gradually a dome-shaped structure rising before you, out of which you have only to cut a small hole for a door, to find yourself within a very light, comfortable-looking bee-hive on a large scale, in which you can bid defiance to wind and weather."
> — Kennedy on building a snow-house, `p. 79`

**The result of the voyage**

> "Though every search was made in all parts we have visited, we have found no record or trace of the proceedings of Sir John Franklin's expedition."
> — Kennedy to the Admiralty, `p. 183`
