# Sutherland's *Journal of a Voyage in Baffin's Bay and Barrow Straits* — Penny's side of the 1850–51 season

Working notes drawn from two on-disk OCR texts:

- **Sutherland** = Peter C. Sutherland, M.D., *Journal of a Voyage in Baffin's Bay and Barrow Straits, in the Years 1850–1851, Performed by H.M. Ships "Lady Franklin" and "Sophia," under the Command of Mr. William Penny, in Search of the Missing Crews of H.M. Ships Erebus and Terror* (London: Longman, Brown, Green, and Longmans, 1852), 2 vols. Sutherland was surgeon of the brig *Sophia*.
  - Vol. I: `db/history/franklin-search/files/franklin-search-sutherland-v1.txt` (Chapters I–XV; departure from Aberdeen, 13 April 1850, to the short excursions of February–March 1851).
  - Vol. II: `db/history/franklin-search/files/franklin-search-sutherland-v2.txt` (Chapters XVI–XXI, covering spring/summer 1851 sledging and the passage home; then a long Appendix reprinting the travelling parties' official reports and Penny's own despatches to the Admiralty, roughly pp. i–cxxx of a separate Appendix pagination).

Every fact and quotation below is tagged **V1** or **V2** with the **printed** page number preserved in the running heads (e.g. "291", or "CXV" for the Roman-numbered Appendix). OCR is raw and rough; obvious typesetting/OCR corruptions are flagged in `[brackets]` rather than silently corrected. Names are given as printed, including OCR quirks (e.g. "Ornmanney" for Ommanney, "Uppernavik," "Esquimaux").

## A note on coordinates in the tables below

Sutherland gives comparatively few running noon positions for the ships themselves — most of his stated latitudes/longitudes attach to a **place** (a headland, an island, a point he has just named) rather than to a daily "ship's position" entry. Accordingly, in the tables:

- Coordinates printed **without** a marker, or marked **(stated)**, are read directly from the text — either an explicit "lat. …, long. …" or a meridian/artificial-horizon altitude Sutherland or Penny gives in the narrative.
- Coordinates marked **`[inferred from place name]`** are *not* in the source. They are modern approximate coordinates for the place named, supplied only so the table can be plotted, and are not claims about where the ship herself lay that day.
- A handful of dates carry **no** coordinate at all where neither the text nor a nameable place gives one to infer from; these are left blank rather than guessed.

### B.2 Where Sutherland's account differs from Sherard Osborn's

(Cf. `austin-penny-1850-51.md`, Section C, drawn from Osborn's *Stray Leaves*.) Osborn was not present for any of this — he arrived later via the grounded *Pioneer* — and his account (Osborn p. 85) telescopes the whole of Penny's discoveries into one continuous sentence covering "26–27 Aug": "the embankment of a house with carpenter and armourer's working-places, washing-tubs, coal-bags, pieces of old clothing, rope, and, lastly, the graves of three of the crew… placing it beyond all doubt." Sutherland, who was there, separates the story into two distinct finds on two different days and two different places:

1. **26 August**, north of Cape Spencer (not on Beechey Island): a single stone tent-ring/look-out camp with soup tins, an MS book, and a September-1844 newspaper — found by **Penny personally**, with Stewart, Goodsir, and Petersen.
2. **The next day (undated in the OCR, but bracketed between "August 26th" and "August 28th," and therefore almost certainly 27 August)**, on Beechey Island itself, in Union Bay: the meat-tin cairn, the storehouse/workshop site, and **the three graves** — found by a party **under Mr. Stewart's orders**, not Penny's, with Penny arriving on the scene only after word was sent to him.

Other differences worth noting:

- **Sutherland was not part of the grave-finding party.** He explicitly identifies himself only with the *later*, separate boat party that Stewart led to Cape Ricketts and Gascoyne Inlet that same day ("of which I happened to be one," p. 308) — a search that found no further traces and is a different expedition from the one that found the graves.
- **Sutherland gives no text for the grave epitaphs.** He refers the reader to an illustration — "On the opposite page will be seen a rude representation of the monuments, with their respective epitaphs" (p. 304) — and the OCR of that facing page (305) captures no epitaph text at all, only the page-number/running-head, confirming it was a plate. Osborn, by contrast, typesets all three inscriptions in full (Osborn p. 91).
- **Sutherland's copy of Ommanney's Cape Riley record is fuller** than the version Snow copied (as quoted in the existing Osborn/Snow notes): Sutherland's text (pp. 308–309) includes the detail that a provisions depot was left at Whaler Point, Port Leopold, that *Assistance* and *Intrepid* were detached from Austin's squadron off Wolstenholme Island on 15 August, and that Ommanney intended to proceed on to Cape Hotham and Cape Walker — details not present in the shorter text Snow transcribed.
- **The chain of transmission of the news differs slightly in dating.** Sutherland has an American boat bringing word of the Cape Riley/Beechey traces to Penny's ships on **25 August**; the existing Osborn-based notes place the Americans informing Penny about a day earlier, "~24 Aug."
- Sutherland's own text never prints an explicit "August 27th" heading for the day the graves were found — this is a gap in the OCR/original that should be flagged rather than silently resolved, though the date can be inferred with reasonable confidence from what precedes and follows it (and from Austin's/Osborn's independently dated arrival at Union Bay the night of 27 August, per `austin-penny-1850-51.md`).

### B.3 Verbatim: Ommanney's Cape Riley record, as Sutherland's boat party found and copied it (V1, pp. 308–309)

> "H. M. Arctic Searching Expedition.
> "This is to certify, that Captain Omnianney [sic, Ommanney], with the officers of H. M. ships 'Assistance' and 'Intrepid,' landed at Cape Riley on the 23rd of August, 1850, where were found traces of an encampment; and collected the remains of materials, which evidently prove that some party belonging to H. ## C. Winter 1850–51 at Assistance Bay, Cornwallis Island

### C.1 Securing and freeing the ships

- **Arrival/anchoring:** c. **11–12 September 1850** (see A.2 above). By **16 September** "the ice in Assistance Bay was daily increasing in strength, and the 'Lady Franklin' was in great measure frozen in, but the 'Sophia' was still free to a certain extent" (V1 p. 368). The bay was named "Assistance Bay… in consequence of [H.M.S. *Assistance*'s] priority of discovery" of a cairn there (V1 p. 364).
- Commitment to wintering was gradual and reluctant: "The question of going out of harbour (for Assistance Bay **we feared** would be our winter quarters), was receiving great attention" (V1 p. 368) — phrasing that shows the decision was not yet final in mid-September.
- **Freed:** the ships left winter quarters on **12 August 1851**, "exactly eleven months after we had dropped anchor in it" (V2 p. 307). Sawing a canal through the harbour ice had begun before Penny's return from the Wellington Channel boat expedition (V2 pp. 260–261), and by Penny's own official report the ships "continued ice-bound till the 10th of August," even though "had our parties returned in sufficient time to refit… we would have been at liberty on the 15th of July" (V2 Appendix p. CXVIII).

### C.2 Housing over and storehouses

- Late September/October 1850: royal masts struck, top-gallant masts shortened, running rigging readied (V1 p. 356).
- The ships were "by this time almost completely banked up with snow, and a gangway of the same material, with two parapet walls sloped gradually from the door in the awning to the surface of the ice," and the dogs housed in "a little snow-house at the ship's bow" — this is dated to shortly before **November 1850**, with "the ice in the harbour… upwards of two feet thick," having been "ten or eleven inches" on **26 September** and increasing "at the rate of half an inch per day" (V1 pp. 418–419).
- "After the banking up had been brought to a close, and our decks were covered with a layer of snow over half-inch boards, with felt underneath, the ice in our beds again melted" (V1 pp. 422–423) — i.e. banking-up was essentially finished by early November.

### C.3 Health and the sick list

- **No deaths occurred among Penny's own crews this winter** (confirmed both in the narrative and in Penny's own official despatch — see below). The one death recorded near the expedition this season was **George Malcolm, captain of the hold of H.M.S. *Resolute*** (Austin's squadron, not Penny's), who died of a severe frost-bite contracted "between the 21st and 23rd" of April 1851 during the spring sledging and was buried "on the east side of Griffith's Island in the frozen shingle" (V2 pp. 101–102).
- **17 September 1850**: "With the exception of one of the crew of the 'Sophia' who had to be exempted for a few days from duty while suffering from *furunculus*, there had not been a single instance of disease or ill[ness]" (V1 p. 370, continuing).
- **22 December 1850** (shortest day): "The health of our crews, up to this time, was remarkably good… The slight rheumatic complaints, which some of the crew spoke of when their beds were a little wet, disappeared" (V1 p. 445).
- **13 January 1851**: temperature down to −39° (Pastorelli's spirit-of-wine thermometer, "as solid as a leaden bullet"); "We never felt the slightest inconvenience from the cold, and frost-bites were unheard of" (V1 p. 451).
- Penny's own official report to the Admiralty of 8 September 1851 makes the point explicitly and proudly: **"one fact will speak for itself, viz. that there has not been one single case of sickness in either the 'Lady Franklin' or 'Sophia.'… While on this subject, I cannot but make mention in terms of praise of Messrs. Sutherland, Goodsir, and Stuart,— their exertions alike to instruct and amuse the men greatly contributing to the happy issue"** (V2 Appendix p. CXIV). The Admiralty's reply of 22 September 1851, signed W. A. B. Hamilton, concurs: "My Lords are further of opinion, that great credit is due to you and Captain Stewart… for the state of health maintained on board your respective ships" (V2 Appendix p. CXXI).
- By contrast, the neighbouring **Felix** (Sir John Ross) suffered a real scurvy outbreak: "Symptoms of scurvy broke out on board the 'Felix,' and threw the crew of that ship into a panic… The appearance of scurvy, so early as four months and a half after leaving home, certainly implied that the provisions which had been in use were exceedingly faulty" (V1 pp. 415–416). By late November, "Eight cases of scurvy had occurred by this time" (V1 p. 439). By early March 1851 (V1 p. 499, verified directly): "There was one person, however, of whom scurvy had taken a severe hold: all that could be done for him proved of little value, since the supplies of preserved vegetables and other antiscorbutics, which had been granted by Captain Penny, were discontinued by the request of Sir John Ross, who said that, now as his crew was quite well, he desired not to continue a burden to our expedition. He was still forced to receive supplies of sugar." The same page records that the coming spring's division of labour had by then been settled: "It was finally arranged that the Wellington Channel should be searched by our expedition, while Captain Austin's was to do all the rest; and the 'Felix' was left in the background, because it was not believed that she possessed the means to accomplish anything with safety or satisfaction."
- Penny's ships supplied the *Felix* with carrots, fresh potatoes, and porter throughout the winter: "the preserved carrots, with which our expedition was so munificently supplied by Mr. Hogarth, were highly prized by the crew of the 'Felix'… The man who was affected sensibly with the disease was allowed some porter, which was sent over once or twice a week for his special use from the 'Lady Franklin'" (V1 p. 417).

### C.4 Routine

- Formal parade/exercise: "The system of parading the crews of both vessels for two hours on the capacious deck of the 'Lady Franklin,' and marching them round and round, keeping step with the music of fife and drum, was found of essential service in preventing this evil [indigestion from want of exercise]" (V1 p. 453, verified directly).
- **Theatricals**: opened **4 November 1850** — "the Arctic Academy was opened under the favourable auspices of our commander; and the boards of the Royal Cornwallis Theatre were graced by a company of actors… Mr. Goodsir and Mr. Stuart, the medical officers of the 'Lady Franklin,' were the directors of the performances, and Walter Craig, one of the seamen of the same ship, was the stage-manager… performers were selected from time to time out of the respective crews of the three ships in Assistance Bay… Saturday night was generally chosen for these festivities… Sir John Ross and Commander Phillips were generally present" (V1 pp. 426–428). A favourite recurring song was "Shon McNab" (V1 p. 429).
- **Schools and libraries** (V1 p. 432, verified directly): "About eighty volumes were sent on board the 'Sophia,' and upwards of twice that number of books on board the 'Lady Franklin,' by the government, for the use of their crews during winter… it was fortunate we were permitted to change them from the one ship to the other, as soon as they were read." Lady Franklin herself had sent "some very useful works on the Arctic Regions… in one of the ships under the command of Captain Austin, which we received about the end of June, when we first had communication with that Expedition" — i.e. these particular books reached them in Melville Bay in June 1850, not during the winter itself. There was also "a small collection of religious books from the library of the Mariners' Chapel, Aberdeen" (V1 p. 432).
- **Christmas**: "The Christmas festivities passed away cheerfully… the crews of the three ships dined on Christmas Day on board the 'Lady Franklin.' The between-decks of that ship was cleared up, where the theatrical performances were conducted, and sufficient accommodation was made for fifty persons to sit down to dinner" (V1 p. 446, entry dated "December 30th"). Separately, at the December-1850 end of Ch. XIV: "A masquerade and a ball on the last night of December excited great attention, and afforded an unusual amount of amusement to the crews of the three ships, among whom nothing but the most praiseworthy harmony [prevailed]" (V1 pp. 455–456, verified directly).
- **Shortest day, 22 December 1850**: "The shortest day at length arrived. In the morning it was very close… The weather was exceedingly mild, for there was very little wind, and the temperature had risen from −25° to −4°… Those who had been attempting to read the brevier type found it too small" (V1 pp. 444–445).
- Hunting, fishing through the ice on Kate Austin Lake (salmon caught with hook and line through 2-ft-thick ice), snow-balling, and moonlit walks were routine amusements (V1 pp. 436, 471, and passim).

### C.5 Dealings with the other vessels wintering nearby

Nine vessels in all wintered within reach that season: Penny's *Lady Franklin* and *Sophia*; Austin's squadron (*Resolute*, *Assistance*, *Pioneer*, *Intrepid*) at Griffith's Island; and Sir John Ross's *Felix*, close by Penny's ships at Assistance Bay (the American *Advance*/*Rescue* had already sailed for home). Dealings recorded:

- **3 October 1850** (V1 pp. 391–393, verified directly): after several failed attempts, Penny and Petersen finally drive the dog-sledge across to Austin's squadron; they return four hours later "accompanied by a party from H.M.S. 'Assistance,' of three men and two officers," Lieut. Mecham and Mr. Ede (assistant surgeon), en route to Barlow Inlet — bringing the news that the American Expedition had already left for home.
- **19 October 1850 (Trafalgar Day)**: "our friends, the gallant Sir John Ross, Commander Phillips, and Dr. Porteus of the 'Felix,' met us at dinner on board the 'Lady Franklin,' we had a hearty dish of fresh salmon. Sir John said that he would endeavour to get a large one, which he himself should carry to Captain Austin, at Griffith's Island, although the distance was at least twenty miles" (V1 p. 415).
- **November 1850**: "The Esquimaux on board the 'Felix' disappeared" one night, was lost in a storm for hours (temperature −10° to −3°), reached Griffith's Island, "was dismissed by Captain Austin," and made his own way back to the *Felix* the same day — see Section F, Adam Beck (V1 pp. 424–425).
- Continuous medical/provision support to the *Felix* through her scurvy outbreak (Section C.3 above).
- **28 February 1851** (V1 p. 494, verified directly): "Captain Penny made a promise to Sir John Ross to let him have the dogs and sledge, accompanied by Mr. Petersen, to drive him over to Griffith's Island, that he might be present at a general meeting of the officers of the squadron under the command of Captain Austin, in which they were to consider fully the preparations for travelling, and the time for starting. Sir John wished to impress them with the importance of not leaving their ships before the second week in May, for before that time they might expect lower temperatures than −25°, at which he says human blood freezes."
- Throughout spring 1851, "frequent communication has been held with Captain Austin's expedition, which has wintered in the strait between Griffith's and Cornwallis Islands, and arrangements were made with reference to the different routes to be taken in the coming travelling" — Penny's own words, official despatch of 12 April 1851 (V2 Appendix p. CXIV).

## D. Spring and summer 1851 — every sledge and boat journey

### D.1 Departure

- **17 April 1851**: after some days of stormy weather, "The whole of our crews — men and officers — assembled on board the 'Lady Franklin' by Captain Penny's orders; and after prayers were read by him, and a few words were addressed to us with respect to our duties, we were ready to set out. There was not a drooping spirit among us" (V1/V2 — V2 pp. 38–39). Six sledges set out, "No. 1. advancing, of which Captain Stewart had special charge; and No. 6., of which Mr. Reid had the charge, bringing up the rear" (V2 p. 39). "The whole crew of the 'Felix' accompanied us… in the three hearty cheers, and one more for good luck, with which they parted from us… Sir John Ross did not accompany us out of the bay: there is, however, an excuse for him at the advanced age of seventy-four. I shall never forget the venerable appearance of his grey locks, as he waved his hat" (V2 p. 40). "Captain Penny and Mr. Petersen were to leave the ships in a day or two" (V2 p. 41).
- The Appendix preserves Penny's own words to the assembled crews, recorded by Sutherland: **"He had no doubt we would overcome every difficulty, and brave every trial and privation attending it; and, whether or not we should be made, through Providence, the instruments for relieving our absent countrymen, or discovering their unhappy fate, he felt convinced, when our labours were over, we should possess the sweetest reward man can have, namely, 'minds conscious of having done our duty.'"** (V2 Appendix p. CXI — this appendix report gives 17 April as a Thursday and describes the same launch).
- Penny's own official despatch of 8 September 1851 gives the schedule precisely: "on the 17th April six sledges, with forty-one officers and men, started from the ships, under the command of Captain Stewart of the 'Sophia'… I started from the ships on the 18th with the dog-sledges, accompanied by Mr. Petersen, and at noon on the 18th I joined the sledges" (V2 Appendix p. CXV).

### D.3 Penny's own journey to the head of Wellington Channel, May 1851 (V2, Ch. XVIII, pp. 122–136)

Day by day, as Penny narrates it in the first person:

- **9 May**, 6 a.m.: Penny, Petersen, and the seaman Thompson set out by dog-sledge; reach the advance depot (45 miles out) by early afternoon; push on to 51 miles from the ships by 11 p.m. Temperature −3° to −10° (pp. 122–123).
- **10 May**: passes Marshall and Goodsir's party; names **Point Petersen** ("to whose services the expedition which I commanded is very much indebted," p. 124); names **Kaine [Kane] Bay** "after Dr. Kaine, a highly intelligent medical officer in the American Expedition"; both he and Petersen snow-blind; camp called **Snow-blind Bay** (p. 124).
- **11 May**: "The Wellington Channel extends due north, to **latitude 75° 22′**, to the above-mentioned headland, which was named **Cape de Haven**, out of compliment to the Commander of the American Expedition" (p. 125).
- **12 May (Monday)**: from a 400-ft hill beyond Cape de Haven, sees land trending N.E. and N.W. with a gap between — "there might be openings out of this newly-discovered sea." Names the viewpoint **Point Decision**; names **Helen Haven** and **Point Phillips**, the latter "out of compliment to… Commander Phillips, R.N., who accompanied Sir John Ross in the 'Felix'" (pp. 125–126).
- **14 May (Wed)**: bear chase; travels N.W. by N. 25–30 miles; names **Hamilton Island** (after Capt. W. A. B. Hamilton, Secretary of the Admiralty) sighted (p. 127).
- **15 May**: reaches Hamilton Island; names **Cape Washington** (its S.E. point, after Capt. Washington, R.N.) and the **South Channel** (pp. 127–128).
- **16 May**: at Cape Washington, "obtained a meridian altitude, which gave **latitude 75° 44′**; but as my eyes were at this time very much inflamed, I could not depend upon its correctness within a mile or two" (p. 129). Rounds the island; names **Cape Scoresby** and **Haddo Bay**; open water found; reaches **Point Surprise** — "to our surprise a strait, and nothing but clear water, opened out before us. The tide seemed to be going at a very rapid rate, I should say not less than four knots" (p. 130). Names **Cape Benjamin Smith**, **Cape Crozier** (after Crozier, "second in command of the missing expedition"), **Cape Fitzjames** (after "the third in command"), an island named for **Sir Francis T. Baring** (First Lord of the Admiralty), and, seeing land to the north/north-east, names it **Prince Albert Land**, "as the first idea of there being land in this direction occurred on the 26th August, 1850, on board H.M.S. 'Assistance,' and also on board the 'Sophia'" (pp. 130–131). Also names **Sir John Barrow's Monument** for a distant range (p. 132). Penny's own exclamation on passing Point Surprise: **"No one will ever reach Sir J. Franklin; here we are, and no traces are to be found"** (p. 132).
- **17 May**: meridian altitude at Point Surprise gives the latitude "four miles farther north, about 76°, that of our position being **75° 55′ 43″**" (p. 132). Twenty-five miles of open water seen toward Baring Island and beyond Cape Crozier, with perhaps twenty-five more beyond that. Forced to turn back for want of dog provisions: **"No alternative remained but to return to the ships, and see if, by any means, a boat could be got into the open water, which was so unexpectedly discovered"** (p. 133).
- **18–20 May**: hard retreat; a worn-out dog shot for food (the others refuse to eat her); reach the depot at Point Separation 2 a.m. on the 20th; reach the ships at midnight, 20 May (pp. 133–136).

### D.4 Penny's boat expedition, June–July 1851 (V2, Ch. XVIII, pp. 136–186)

- Carpenter and sailmaker of the *Sophia* fit a large sledge to carry the six-oared whale boat, starting **26 May**; provisions for forty (later revised to fifty) days prepared (pp. 136–137).
- **4 June**: the boat/sledge party, 15 persons under **Mr. Manson**, leaves the ships (p. 139).
- **6 June**: Mr. J. Stuart returns from a 260-mile, 31-day journey to Cape Hurd and Beechey Island with no new traces, then re-equips and rejoins the boat party (p. 140).
- **11 June**: Penny himself joins the boat party at Cape Hotham with a new, properly sized sledge (fitted with the help of Sir John Ross's carpenter, lent for the purpose) (pp. 141–143).
- **12 June**: meets Sutherland's returning party at the lower depot — Sutherland had parted with Stewart on 28 May at 220 statute miles from the ships, "highest latitude he had attained was 76° 25′" (p. 143).
- **17–20 June**: reaches open water near Hamilton Island/Point Surprise; launches the boat; names **Cape Graham Gore** (after the first lieutenant of the *Erebus*) and a point after **Le Visconte**, second lieutenant of the *Erebus* (pp. 157–158); names **Cape Reid** (after the ice-master of the *Erebus*) and **Cape Blanky** (after the ice-master of the *Terror*, who had earlier spent four years missing with Ross in the *Victory* voyage) on Baring Island (p. 158); names **Dundas Island** and **Margaret Island** (p. 159).
- **22 June**: names **Disappointment Bay**, "from the circumstance that we had to remain so long in it, while nothing but open water could be seen around Baring Island" (p. 151); meridian altitude 75°16′20″ (p. 152).
- **5 September [1850, retrospective]**: Penny recalls that from Cape Spencer (750 ft) he had already seen and signalled open water up Wellington Channel to Captain Austin the previous autumn — "I hoisted a flag to direct the attention of Captain Austin to the water… I put my glass into the hands of Mr. Stewart… and Mr. Goodsir, who also said they could discern it very plainly" (p. 153).
- **5 July**: a fresh piece of English elm and driftwood picked up by William Bruce and Penny on the beach — later sent home and examined by Sir John Richardson (see below) (p. 164).
- **11 July**: altitude at Point Surprise gives latitude "about **76° 4′**," a four-mile discrepancy from the 17 May reading, which Penny notes "is to be regretted" (p. 167).
- **14–16 July**: reaches Margaret and Dundas Islands; erects cairns; discovers **North Channel**; names **Cape Liddell** (first lieutenant, *Terror*) and **Cape Collins** (second master, *Erebus*) (pp. 168–170).
- **18–20 July**: reaches Baring Island; erects a cairn; names **Leiper Lake** and **Driftwood Bay**; on 20 July, with only a week's provisions left and the passage closing with drifting ice, decides to turn back: "That the missing ships had gone beyond our reach, I had no doubt" (pp. 174–179).
- **19–21 July**: reaches Cape Becher, finds **Captain Stewart's farthest** cairn there; ascends further, opens **Sir Robert H. Inglis Bay**; names **Cape Sir J. Franklin** ("a few miles beyond latitude 77° and longitude 100°") and **Cape Sophia** ("after the niece of Sir John Franklin"); names **John Barrow Island**, **Parker Island**, and **Cape Lady Franklin** ("does not appear to extend so far north as 77°, and its longitude is about 102°"), with an inlet named for **Henry Goodsir** (pp. 176–179).
- **21–22 July**: forced to abandon the boat at **Abandon Bay** on Cornwallis Island; names **Lady Hamilton Bay** and **Cape Austin**, "out of compliment to the commander of the Expedition with which we had to co-operate" (pp. 181–182); fords the ice-cold Stuart Bay stream, names **Eleanor Lake** and **Laura Lakes** (after daughters of Captain Hamilton) (pp. 182–184).
- **23–25 July**: reaches the depot at Cape de Haven (latitude by artificial horizon 75°22′), continues on foot and by a boat Stewart had thoughtfully left; **reaches the ships at 10 p.m., 25 July 1851** (pp. 183–186). John Leiper, second mate, had been walking on bare soles; he "died in March [1852], six months after the return of the Expedition… His disease was an affection of the lungs" (p. 186, footnote).

Penny's own summary schedule (official despatch) gives his total for the season as **60 days, 932 miles** travelled (V2 Appendix, "Schedule" table, p. [figures partly garbled in OCR] c. CXX).

## E. The quarrel with Captain Austin

Sutherland's account does not stage a single dramatic confrontation so much as a drawn-out, increasingly constrained negotiation, in which Penny's dependence on Austin's steam power is explicit from early on:

- Before the two commanders had even met to discuss it, Sutherland notes the structural problem: **"To be conducted safely and satisfactorily, steam-power would require to become an essential element; and this we did not possess, but Captain Austin did; and before it could be made available, that officer would require to enter deeply into consultation with Mr. Penny, upon the expediency of still prosecuting the search"** (V2 p. 260).
- Austin's own mind was made up before Penny's return from Wellington Channel: **"Captain Austin's frequently expressed opinion was, that nothing of which he then knew… could induce us or any of us to remain out a second winter… Mr. Penny, whose resources for carrying on the search were completely exhausted, and depended entirely upon Captain Austin and his steamers, as far as a continuation of the search was concerned, had to adapt and mould his opinions to those of that gallant officer already expressed"** (V2 pp. 260–261).
- On his return, walking the beach with Stewart, "Captain Penny appeared to me to be in a sad dilemma… **he would neither be responsible for bringing the search to a close, nor for jeopardizing the lives of upwards of two hundred and twenty men, by leading them up the Wellington Channel in the very footsteps, as he believed, of the unfortunate Franklin**… he looked forward to an early meeting with Captain Austin, and said that after he had given an outline of what had come under his observations in the Victoria Channel, **he should at once propose a continuation of the search by means of one of the steamers and the 'Sophia' through the Wellington Channel, so soon as the ice in that channel should open"** (V2 pp. 301–302, "August" [11th, from context]).
- **11 August 1851, afternoon**: "Captain Austin's squadron came steaming into the harbour, and dropped anchor. The commander of the 'Sophia' was immediately sent on board the 'Resolute' to wait upon Captain Austin with letters from Mr. Penny; and in a short time [Penny] also went on board… to enter into a deliberate consultation" (V2 pp. 303–304). The piece of English elm found on the boat expedition was examined by the ships' carpenters and by Austin, but "The piece of elm failed; and from the absence of deposited records, all that Mr. Penny could or did say, failed to convince him of even the faintest probability that the missing ships had taken that route" (V2 pp. 304–305). **Austin's own stated opinion: "had he done exactly what Mr. Penny's Expedition had done, and were he placed in the position which Mr. Penny occupied, he should not hesitate to conclude at once that the search for the missing ships need not be prosecuted to the north-west of their winter quarters at Beechey Island"** (V2 p. 305).
- The outcome, recorded by Sutherland from his own journal at the time: **"After the heads of the Expeditions had considered matters fully, we were given to understand that little remained to be done but proceed to England. Captain Austin was satisfied that the missing Expedition need not be searched for to the due west or north-west; and Mr. Penny, uncertain whether they had proceeded up the channel, could hold out no hopes of our being able to accomplish anything deserving the almost inevitable risks of a second winter."** (V2 pp. 305–306).
- The closing note, learned only the next morning, was Penny's own written reply to Austin: **"Mr. Penny, in reply to frequent solicitations from Captain Austin with respect to the further prosecution of the search, had granted a note, stating that he had accomplished all that could be necessary within the bare limits of the Wellington Channel, and adding a question, 'What more can be done?'"** (V2 p. 306).
- Sutherland's editorial gloss makes clear he read this as effectively Penny's own capitulation, forced by circumstance rather than conviction: earlier the same section states plainly, of Penny's position, "It has been remarked elsewhere, that **without Captain Austin's co-operation our Expedition, dried up in its resources, could effect nothing by remaining out a second winter**. This… deprived us of that freedom of action which would have enabled us to hold out strongly in defence of our opinions" (V2 pp. 304–305).
- **Penny's own official despatch** (8 September 1851) states the outcome far more tersely and without mentioning any steamer proposal by name: "On the 11th of August, Captain Austin's ships entered our harbour… His parties had penetrated as far as ships could hope to do, yet like our own, they were unsuccessful in finding the least trace of the missing expedition. In fact, none had been found, such as would warrant the risk of a second winter; and **my orders being such as left no alternative, I determined on immediately returning to England**, if no instructions to the contrary should be met with" (V2 Appendix p. CXVIII). This official framing — orders leaving "no alternative" — is noticeably flatter than Sutherland's personal account of Penny's inner conflict and his half-made proposal for the steamer.
- The ships left winter quarters the next day, **12 August 1851** (Section C.1/A.5 above).

## F. Named Inuit and Greenlandic people

| Name | What Sutherland says |

CXXI). |
| **Adam Beck** | Sir John Ross's Greenlandic interpreter aboard the *Felix*. Got lost overnight near Griffith's Island in a November 1850 storm (temperature −10° to −3°), reached the squadron, was "dismissed by Captain Austin," and found his own way back to the *Felix* the same day (V1 pp. 424–425). Remained opinionated through the spring 1851 search, insisting Franklin's ships would be found "somewhere about or north of Wolstenholme Sound, where, he said, his ships' cables are still to be found," and seeming "to sneer at the disappointed travellers" (V2 pp. 196–197). CX). |
| **Enoolooapike** | Not part of this expedition, but discussed by Sutherland at length as background: "a native brought to Aberdeen by Captain Penny in 1839," on an earlier whaling voyage of the *St. Andrew*; he "remained a whole winter, and received a little education," gave Sir Francis Beaufort a map of a large inlet, and was landed the following year on "the Island of Keimooksooke" — the voyage that led Penny to rename the inlet **Hogarth Sound** (Tenudiakweek/"Whale and Rapid Tide Sound" to the Esquimaux) (V2 pp. 229–230). Also described in the Introduction: "This was the first Esquimaux from the British possessions within the Arctic circle that trod on British soil… his benefactor fulfilled the solemn promise he had made to the uncivilised but affectionate mother of this home-sick adventurer, for Enoolooapike was restored to her, loaded with presents, after an absence of somewhat less than a year" (V1 p. xviii). |
| Two unnamed Esquimaux who declined to join at Uppernavik | One said "that the strength of our ships, which we trusted to, would no more stand violent pressure among the ice than his kyak, and on that account he declined accompanying us." Another: "that after going far away from the settlements, surrounded on all sides by ice, where there would be no seals, our provisions would be exhausted, and what then should we do?" (V1 p. 105). |
| Three unnamed "Arctic Highlanders" at Cape York (V1 pp. 248–250, verified directly) | Brought aboard by boat off Cape York while working clear of Melville Bay: "It soon returned, bringing off three Esquimaux, poor wretched creatures, whose parents, I suppose, Sir John Ross had designated the Arctic Highlanders." Mr. Petersen "conversed with them in their native tongue with the greatest ease," though "the excitement and ecstasy they were in prevented a satisfactory conversation" — questions about the missing ships "appeared to them to be quite meaningless, as they had often seen English ships and white men." Mr. Stewart recognised one of them from the whaler *Joseph Green* the previous season. Presents (needles, knives, empty meat canisters, wood, iron hoops) were given before they were put ashore. Petersen told one woman his wife was partly of her kindred, "her delight seemed to know no bounds"; she pointed to the girl in the hood of her coat. |
| Sir John Ross's unnamed Esquimaux at Cape York | Brought story of a massacre that Petersen later disproved; also the individual who went missing in the November 1850 storm may be this same man or a different Esquimaux aboard the *Felix* — the text is not perfectly consistent about whether "the Esquimaux on board the 'Felix'" (V1 p. 424) is Adam Beck himself or a second man; both names appear in close proximity in different chapters and should not be silently merged. |
| Two Esquimaux and a Dane sought (but only Petersen obtained) at Uppernavik | "Two Esquimaux and a Dane would prove very useful to our Expedition, from the experience which they have in travelling over ice with dogs and sledges" — none of the Esquimaux would go; only Petersen accepted (V1 pp. 104–105). |

## Appendix: items flagged for further verification

Most facts and every direct quotation in this note were read and checked line-by-line against the two OCR files directly while writing it (Sections B, C.1–C.3, D.1, D.3, D.4, E, F, and all of Section G). A first reconnaissance pass over the bulk of the outbound-voyage and other-sledge-party material was assisted by automated reading, and every item drawn from that pass and then kept in this note was subsequently spot-checked and, where quoted verbatim, re-verified against the source text and its page number (see the corrected citations for Berry Island, the parade routine, the schools/library passage, the 3 October and 28 February Griffith's-Island exchanges, the Christmas/masquerade passages, the March 1851 *Felix* scurvy case, and the Cape York "Arctic Highlanders" scene — all now cited to a page checked directly). Two items remain as reported by the reconnaissance pass without a second direct check in this file: (1) the day/mile figures in D.2 for Mr. Goodsir's and Mr. J. Stuart's second-journey legs, which are corroborated in outline by Penny's official despatch (V2 Appendix pp. CXV–CXIX) but not verified sentence-by-sentence against the Ch. XVII narrative; and (2) the exact per-officer day/mile entries in Penny's "Schedule" table (V2 Appendix, c. p. CXX), where the OCR renders most of the numeral columns as garbled digits — the total given for Penny himself, "60 days, 932 miles," is legible in the OCR and is used above, but the table as a whole should not be trusted for any figure not double-checked against a clean copy.
