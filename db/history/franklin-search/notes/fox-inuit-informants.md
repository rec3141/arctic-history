# Named Inuit People in McClintock's "Voyage of the Fox" (1859)

## Greenlanders Shipped as Crew

### Anton Christian
- Role: Interpreter (on crew list line 136)
- Origin: Greenland
- Status on voyage: Served as interpreter and crew member
- Notes: Mentioned alongside Samuel Emanuel as two Greenland Esquimaux on the Fox

### Samuel Emanuel
- Role: Quartermaster/Ship's Steward (line 137)
- Origin: Greenland
- Died: June 14, 1859 (Scurvy) 
- Notes: Listed on crew manifest as one of the two Greenland Esquimaux

## Carl Petersen (Interpreter)
- Full name: Carl Petersen
- Nationality: Danish
- Role: Esquimaux interpreter
- Background: 
  - Previously served as interpreter in expeditions of Captain Penny and Dr. Kane (p. 9)
  - Landed in Denmark only 6 days before joining McClintock (p. 9)
  - After absence of a year from his family
  - Secured through Sir Roderick Murchison and the electric telegraph (p. 9)
- Later: "Since his return to Copenhagen, Petersen has been worthily honored" (footnote)
- Service notes: "Performing immense service" as interpreter throughout voyage

## Named Inuit People Encountered

### Ooblooria (also known as Oo-na-lee)
- Who: Old man, Boothian (from Boothia Felix/Cape Victoria area)
- Met: Cape Victoria, February-March 1859, and again ~April 29, 1859 at latitude 70.5°N
- Background: Had been employed by Sir James Ross as guide on HMS Victory
  - Knew Sir James Ross by Esquimaux name "Agglugga"
  - "All the old people recollected the visit of the 'Victory'" (p. 232-233)
- What he said/did:
  - Made rough sketch of coast-line with spear upon snow, indicated "eight journeys to where the ship sank, pointing in the direction of Cape Felix" (p. 231)
  - April encounter: "Old Oo-na-lee laid his hands on Petersen's shoulders to measure their width, and said, 'He is fatter now'" (p. 249)
  - Told McClintock about ship with three masts crushed by ice west of King William's Island: "all the people landed safely" (p. 233)
  - Provided information about second ship forced on shore at "Oot-loo-lik" (p. 251)
  - When questioned about depot: Petersen interpreted "Oo-na-lee's parting information, and told me how he described that the breech of the revolver turned round" (p. 253)
  - "was well acquainted with the coast-line up to Bellot Strait, and had names for the different headlands, although he had never been so far north" (p. 254)

### Oo-na-lee's Wife
- Who: One of the Inuit women at Cape Victoria, March 1859
- What she did: "She was the woman who plucked out an infant by its arm from inside her dress, and exposed it regardless of —30° and a fresh wind" (p. 254)

### Young man who sold the knife
- Who: Netsilik/Boothian native (name not given in text)
- Met: April 1859, at ~70.5°N latitude
- What he said: 
  - Sold knife to McClintock with indistinct markings (p. 250-251)
  - "the man told us it had been picked up on the shore near where a ship lay stranded; that it was then about the length of his arm, but his countryman who picked it up broke it into lengths to make knives" (p. 250-251)
  - "gave us by the young man who sold the knife" - "The most of this information was given us by the young man who sold the knife" (p. 251)
  - Reported body found on board stranded ship: "the body of a man was found on board the ship; that he must have been a very large man, and had long teeth" (p. 252)
  - About timing of ship destruction: "it was in the fall of the year — that is, August or September — when the ships were destroyed; that all the white people went away to the 'large river,' taking a boat or boats with them, and that in the following winter their bones were found there" (p. 252)

## Meeting Locations and Dates

### Cape Victoria (Boothia coast)
- Date: February-March 1859 (specifically March 3-4)
- Population: "entire village population arrived, amounting to about forty-five souls, from aged people to infants in arms" (p. 232)
- McClintock's note: "beyond this I would not go, much as they wished to lead us on" (p. 229)
- Location: "western shores of Boothia" (Chapter title, p. 213)

### Second meeting at ~70.5°N latitude
- Date: April 29-30, 1859
- Location: "On 29th April, in latitude 70½° N., we met two families of natives, comprising twelve individuals; their snow huts were upon the ice three-quarters of a mile off shore, and their occupation was seal-hunting" (p. 249)
- Same people: "They were the same people with whom I had communicated at Cape Victoria in February" (p. 249)

### Near Neitchillee (Netsilik villages)
- Reference: The Inuit "said, were residing at the island of Amitoke, ten days' journey distant from here. Can this Amitoke be Matty Island?" (p. 252)
- Direction: "returning to Neitchillee, hunting seals by the way" (p. 252)
- Those met at Cape Victoria "had already gone there" by spring (p. 252)


## Additional Named Inuit People Encountered

### Noo-luk
- Who: Man from Pond's Bay/Igloolik area
- Age: About 45 years old
- When: August 1858
- Location: Pond's Bay/Eclipse Sound area, Baffin Island
- What he said/did:
  - "Noo-luk explained that seven or eight days' journey to the southward there are two wrecks a short day's journey apart" (p. 163)
  - Described height at time of wrecks by showing how tall he was as a child at that time

### A-ra-neet
- Who: Man from Pond's Bay area
- When: August 1858
- What he said:
  - "The man A-ra-neet said he visited these wrecks five winters ago" (p. 163)
  - (About wrecks referred to as Dexterity and Aurora, wrecked in August 1821)

### Ow-wang-noot
- Who: Old man from Pond's Bay area
- When: August 1858
- What he did:
  - "The old man, Ow-wang-noot, drew the coast-line northwards from Cape Graham Moore to Navy Board Inlet, and pointed out the position of the northern wreck a few miles east of Cape Hay" (p. 163)

### Unnamed Woman at De Ros Islet (Pond's Bay area)
- Who: Woman, age at least 45 years
- When: August 1858
- Location: De Ros Islet, Pond's Bay area
- What she said:
  - About Igloolik ship: "Yes, a ship stopped there all one winter; but it is a long time ago" (p. 154)
  - "one of the crew died, and was buried there, and his name was Al-lah or El-leh" (corresponding to Mr. Elder, ice-mate who died in Parry's 1822-3 expedition)
  - Remembered names of several whaling captains
  - "She now told us of another wreck upon the coast, but many days' journey to the south of Pond's Bay; it came there before her first child was born" (p. 154)

### Unnamed Woman at King William's Island (May 1859)
- Who: Old woman 
- When: May 7, 1859
- Location: Inhabited village on King William's Island, latitude ~68°N
- Last visit to wreck: Winter of 1857-8
- What she said (verbatim, interpreted by Petersen):
  - "She said many of the white men dropped by the way as they went to the Great River; that some were buried and some were not; they did not themselves witness this, but discovered their bodies during the winter following." (p. 262-263, 65 words)
  - "There had been many books they said, but all have long ago been destroyed by the weather" (p. 262)
  - "the ship was forced on shore in the fall of the year by the ice" (p. 262)

### Unnamed Old Man and Old Woman at Point Booth (May 10, 1859)
- Who: Elderly couple at single snow hut
- When: May 10, 1859
- Location: Point Booth, King William's Island area
- What they said:
  - "Kammik toomee" (We are friends) - repeated when frightened (p. 265)
  - "They purchased it from their countrymen" (about wood source) (p. 265)
  - "Yes" to question about knowing Great River, but said "it was a long way off" (p. 265)
  - "Yes" about natives at Great River (p. 265)
  - Denied knowledge of white people dying on their shores (p. 265)

### Unnamed Young Man at Point Booth (May 10, 1859)
- Who: "A fine young man" who came out of the hut
- When: May 10, 1859
- Location: Point Booth, King William's Island area
- What he did: Appeared from hut but gave no information (p. 265)

### General Natives at Kaparok-to-lik (August 1858)
- Population: 25 souls (9 men, rest women and children)
- Location: Summer village in semicircular bay with glacier
- What they did/said:
  - Women and children: "held up their arms in the air and shouted 'Pilletay' (give me), incessantly" (p. 158)
  - Men: "more quiet and dignified, yet lost no opportunity... to repeat 'Pilletay' in a beseeching tone of voice" (p. 158)
  - Sledges made from wreck materials (oak planks, cask staves) (p. 159)

### General Natives at Cape Victoria (March 1859)
- Population: "entire village population arrived, amounting to about forty-five souls, from aged people to infants in arms" (p. 232)
- From "native village" (chapter reference p. 146)
- Women: "arrant thieves" but "good-humoured and friendly" (p. 234)
  - "decidedly plain; in fact, this term would have been flattering to most of them" (p. 234-235)
  - Tattooed lines upon cheeks and chins
- Men: "stout, hearty fellows" (p. 235)
  - Hair "cropped short, with the exception of one long, straggling lock hanging down on each side of the face" (p. 264)

### General Natives at King William's Island (May 7, 1859)
- Population: "thirty or forty natives of King William's Island" in "ten or twelve huts"
- What they said/did:
  - "did not think any of them had ever seen white people alive before, but they evidently knew us to be friends" (p. 260)
  - "All of them evinced extreme delight at seeing us" (by Petersen's interpretation, likely from previous context) 
  - General message when man was frightened: "Kammik toomee" (We are friends) (p. 261)
  - One man: tried to retain saw, "holding it behind his back, and presenting his knife in exchange" (p. 261)
  - Repeatedly tapped McClintock "gently on the breast, repeating the words 'Kammik toomee' (We are friends)" (p. 261)
  - "They were most obliging and peaceably disposed, but could not resist the temptation to steal" (p. 261)
  - About ship masts and fire: "they spoke to each other about fire, from which Petersen thought they had burnt the masts through close to the deck in order to get them down" (p. 262)
  - "They seemed to have but little intercourse with other communities" (p. 263)
  - "one man even asked Petersen if he had seen his brother, who lived in Boothia, not having heard of him since last summer" (p. 263)
  - Appearance: "finer race than those who inhabit North Greenland, or Pond's Inlet" with short-cropped hair and tattooed women (p. 263-264)

## Specific Quotes About Franklin's Ships and Dead

### About the first ship (crushed at sea, west of King William's Island)
- From old man at Cape Victoria (March 4, 1859, p. 233):
  "a ship having three masts had been crushed by the ice out in the sea to the west of King William's Island, but that all the people landed safely; he was not one of those who were eye-witnesses of it"

### About the second ship (stranded at Oot-loo-lik)
- From Oo-na-lee and young man at ~70.5°N (April 29-30, 1859, p. 251):
  "two ships had been seen by the natives of King William's Island; one of them was seen to sink in deep water, and nothing was obtained from her... but the other was forced on shore by the ice, where they suppose she still remains, but is much broken. From this ship they have obtained most of their wood"

### About white men dying on journey to Great Fish River
- From unnamed old woman at King William's Island (May 7, 1859, p. 262-263):
  "She said many of the white men dropped by the way as they went to the Great River; that some were buried and some were not; they did not themselves witness this, but discovered their bodies during the winter following." (Quoted above, 65 words)

### About "many books" at wreck
- From King William's Island natives (May 7, 1859, p. 262):
  "There had been many books they said, but all have long ago been destroyed by the weather"

### About timing of ship destruction
- From King William's Island natives (May 7, 1859, p. 252):
  "it was in the fall of the year — that is, August or September — when the ships were destroyed; that all the white people went away to the 'large river,' taking a boat or boats with them, and that in the following winter their bones were found there"

## Meetings and Coordinates

### Cape Victoria, Boothia (February-March 1859)
- First meeting: "Beyond this I would not go, much as they wished to lead us on" (p. 229)
- Point reached: Cape Victoria on Boothia coast
- Date: March 3-4, 1859
- Population encountered: ~45 souls

### At latitude 70.5°N (April 29-30, 1859) 
- Date: April 29, 1859, "in latitude 70½° N."
- Details: "we met two families of natives, comprising twelve individuals; their snow huts were upon the ice three-quarters of a mile off shore, and their occupation was seal-hunting" (p. 249)
- Same people as at Cape Victoria

### Neitchillee (Netsilik villages)
- Referenced: Multiple mentions that natives "said, were residing at the island of Amitoke... returning to Neitchillee, hunting seals by the way" (p. 252)
- The Cape Victoria natives "had already gone there" by spring (p. 252)

### Kaparok-to-lik (August 1858)
- Unnamed summer village of 25 souls
- Description: "seven tents... upon a low flat strip of land" in "a small semicircular bay" with 800-900 ft cliffs
- Notable features: glacier 150-200 ft high in valley behind
- Location: appears to be in Arctic but exact coordinates not given by McClintock

### Pond's Bay / De Ros Islet (August 1858)
- Area: North Baffin Island
- Natives winter at "Green Point, a mile or two within the northern entrance of Pond's Bay" (p. 163)
- Summer migration to Kaparoktolik and then S.W. for reindeer hunting

### Matty Island and vicinity (May 4-5, 1859)
- Southwest point of Matty Island: "deserted village of nearly twenty snow huts, besides several others, within a few miles upon either side of it" (p. 257)
- Abandoned "only within a fortnight or three weeks" of encounter
- Nearby unnamed island "Owut-ta" (named by Esquimaux) (p. 258)
- "Another snow-village very recently abandoned, the sledge tracks plainly showing that the inhabitants had gone to the E.N.E., which is straight for Neitchillee" (p. 258)

### King William's Island inhabited village (May 7, 1859)
- Date: May 7, 1859
- Population: 30-40 natives in 10-12 snow huts
- Crossing from Matty Island "towards the King William Island shore, we continued our march southward until midnight, when we had the good fortune to arrive at an inhabited snow village" (p. 260)
- Latitude: Not explicitly stated by McClintock

### Point Booth / Point Ogle area (May 10, 1859)
- Single snow hut at Point Booth: elderly couple and young man (p. 264-265)
- "Half a mile further on we found seven or eight deserted snow huts" (p. 266)
- Crossed Point Ogle on May 12 (p. 266)

## Carl Petersen's Critical Role

Petersen's interpretation of native statements was essential throughout. Key translations he provided:
- Explained that natives do not like alluding to the dead (p. 230)
- Interpreted Oo-na-lee's statement about boat being "crushed by the ice" (p. 230)
- Questioned old woman about white men's death journey and obtained information (p. 262)
- Thought natives had burnt masts through to deck (p. 262)
- Interpreted Oo-na-lee's parting information about revolver mechanism (p. 253)
- Noted dialect differences: "the dialect being strange to Petersen" (p. 263)
