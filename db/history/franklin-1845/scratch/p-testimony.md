Everything known about the Franklin expedition after 26 April 1848 comes from three kinds of evidence: bones and objects found on the ground; the two wrecks found in 2014 and 2016; and the accounts of Inuit families who saw the ships, boarded them, met the men walking, and found the dead. The third came first, was the most detailed, and was disbelieved the longest.

## Rae, 1854

In April 1854 [[person:John Rae]] was surveying the west shore of Boothia for the Hudson's Bay Company. At [[place:Kugaaruk]] (Pelly Bay) he met Inuit, and from one of them learned "that a party of 'white men' (Kablounans) had perished from want of food some distance to the westward, and not far beyond a large river, containing many falls and rapids." Rae's published report does not name the man. He is generally identified as [[person:In-nook-poo-zhee-jook]], who told the same story to Charles Francis Hall a decade later, though the identification is later scholarship and not Rae's own statement.

Over the following months Rae gathered more and bought relics: silver forks and spoons with officers' crests and initials, pieces of watches, telescopes, guns. He wrote the report at Repulse Bay on 29 July 1854.

> In the spring, four winters past (spring 1850), a party of "white men," amounting to about forty, were seen travelling southward over the ice, and dragging a boat with them, by some Esquimaux, who were killing seals near the north shore of King William's Land... their ship, or ships, had been crushed by ice, and... they were now going to where they expected to find deer to shoot.

> From the mutilated state of many of the corpses and the contents of the kettles, it is evident that our wretched countrymen had been driven to the last resource — cannibalism — as a means of prolonging existence.

Rae was scrupulous about what he had and had not seen: "None of the Esquimaux with whom I conversed had seen the 'whites,' nor had they ever been at the place where the bodies were found, but had their information from those who had been there."

## What England did with it

It attacked him. Charles Dickens wrote two pieces in *Household Words* arguing that the testimony of "a gross handful of uncivilised people" could not be trusted against the character of British sailors, and that Inuit had more likely murdered the survivors themselves. Lady Franklin campaigned against Rae. He got the Admiralty's £10,000 reward and almost nothing else: no knighthood, no naval command, a lasting reputation as the man who slandered the dead.

Every substantive part of Rae's report has since been confirmed. Cut marks consistent with defleshing have been found on Franklin remains from several sites, including, in 2024, on a jaw from [[place:Erebus Bay]] identified by DNA as [[person:James Fitzjames]]. The mutilated corpses were real. The Inuit witnesses were telling the truth, and the men who called them liars were not there.

## Hall, 1864-1869

[[person:Charles Francis Hall]] lived among Inuit in the Repulse Bay region for five years, learned to travel as they did, and questioned witnesses at length with [[person:Tookoolito]] and [[person:Ebierbing]] interpreting. He was credulous in places and he says himself he sometimes doubted his informants' accuracy, but the record he brought back is the fullest body of Franklin testimony that exists. It is printed in [[source:nourse1879narrative]].

In April 1866 an elder, [[person:Kok-lee-arng-nun]], and his wife told Hall they had often been aboard the ships and had known both Too-loo-ark and Aglooka — Franklin and Crozier — by their different ways of shaking hands. Too-loo-ark's was warm and repeated; Aglooka's was short and jerky. After the first summer and first winter they saw no more of Too-loo-ark, and Aglooka was the ish-e-mut-ta, the boss. That matches the written record's date for Franklin's death exactly, and it was given by people who had no access to the written record.

They also described the loss of one ship:

> the ship on board of which they had often seen Too-loo-ark was overwhelmed with heavy ice in the spring of the year. While the ice was slowly crushing it, the men all worked for their lives in getting out provisions; but, before they could save much, the ice turned the vessel down on its side, crushing the masts and breaking a hole in her bottom and so overwhelming her that she sank at once... Several men at work in her could not get out in time, and were carried down with her and drowned. "On this account Ag-loo-ka's company had died of starvation, for they had not time to get the provisions out of her."

The other ship was different. She was found "near Ook-goo-lik... in complete order, having three masts and four boats hanging at the davits — whale-ship like." The people were afraid of her for a long time. When they finally went aboard they found nobody alive and a very large dead man in a locked cabin, and they took knives, forks, spoons, pans, guns and boxes and left the books.

[[person:In-nook-poo-zhee-jook]] guided Hall on King William Island in May 1869 and drew him a numbered sketch map of the whole region, marking where one of the ships sank at Ook-joo-lik and where he himself had found two boats with dead men in them. Then, in an igloo with McClintock's printed chart spread out, he put his finger on the place: "very near O'Reilly Island, a little eastward of the north end of said island, between it and Wilmot and Crampton Bay." Hall wrote down the position as lat. 68° 30' N., long. 99° W., and dated the boarding to early in the spring of 1849.

## Schwatka's party, 1879

Ten years later [[person:Frederick Schwatka]] took a sledge party across the same ground and questioned witnesses again. At an Ookjoolik camp they found [[person:Ikinnelikpatolok]], about sixty-five or seventy, who as a boy had shaken hands with Sir George Back's party on the Great Fish River. The next white man he saw was dead in a bunk aboard a big ship frozen in the ice near an island about five miles due west of Grant Point. His people walked three miles out over smooth ice to reach her.

> They did not know how to get inside by the doors, and cut a hole in the side of the ship, on a level with the ice, so that when the ice broke up during the following summer the ship filled and sunk.

That is the sinking of HMS *Erebus*, described by a man who watched it happen, printed in [[source:gilder1881schwatkas]] in 1881, and treated for the next 133 years as an anecdote.

## The trouble with reading testimony as navigation

None of this is a survey. Informants were describing events they had seen as children or heard from parents, through interpreters, to men holding charts drawn on assumptions the informants had no reason to share. Names of places moved; the same story reaches us under more than one informant's name. Distances given in days' travel do not convert cleanly into miles. [[artifact:franklin-1845-002|The drift track]] in this layer draws the movement the testimony implies, and it is labelled as testimony, with the one openly inferred leg marked as such.

But the shape held. Two ships. One crushed and sunk quickly with men aboard. One carried south, deserted, boarded and eventually flooded through a hole cut in her side near an island off Adelaide Peninsula. That is what the Inuit said. It is where the wrecks were found: [[page:franklin-1845-the-wrecks]].
