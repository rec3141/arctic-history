One sheet of paper. That is the entire written output of the Franklin expedition after July 1845 — one printed Admiralty form, filled in twice by different hands eleven months apart, recovered from a cairn at [[place:Victory Point]] on the north-west coast of King William Island on 6 May 1859 by Lieutenant [[person:William Hobson]] of the *Fox*. It is now in the National Maritime Museum at Greenwich, and [[source:mcclintock1859voyage]] printed it in facsimile.

## The form

It was a standard Admiralty blank, printed in six languages, asking whoever found it to forward it to the Secretary of the Admiralty and, in the other languages, to do the same. The Navy scattered thousands of them in bottles and cairns. The irony of the printed instruction is hard to miss: the one that mattered sat in a cairn for eleven years while search ships passed within a hundred miles of it.

## Gore's entry, 28 May 1847

> 28 of May 1847. H. M. ships 'Erebus' and 'Terror' wintered in the ice in lat. 70° 05' N., long. 98° 23' W. Having wintered in 1846-7 at Beechey Island, in lat. 74° 43' 28" N., long. 91° 39' 15" W., after having ascended Wellington Channel to lat. 77°, and returned by the west side of Cornwallis Island. Sir John Franklin commanding the expedition. All well. Party consisting of 2 officers and 6 men left the ships on Monday 24th May, 1847. Gm. Gore, Lieut. Chas. F. Des Vœux, Mate.

[[artifact:franklin-1845-011|Full text and pin]]. Eighty-one words that carry the whole first half of the voyage.

## Fitzjames's and Crozier's entry, 25 April 1848

Written round the margins of the same sheet, upside down and along the edges:

> April 25, 1848. — H. M. ships 'Terror' and 'Erebus' were deserted on the 22nd April, 5 leagues N.N.W. of this, having been beset since 12th September, 1846. The officers and crews, consisting of 105 souls, under the command of Captain F. R. M. Crozier, landed here in lat. 69° 37' 42" N., long. 98° 41' W. Sir John Franklin died on the 11th June, 1847; and the total loss by deaths in the expedition has been to this date 9 officers and 15 men.

Signed by [[person:Francis Crozier]] as "Captain and Senior Officer" and by [[person:James Fitzjames]] as "Captain H.M.S. Erebus", with one more line in Crozier's hand: "and start (on) to-morrow, 26th, for Back's Fish River." A further marginal note records that the paper had been moved four miles from where Gore originally left it, and calls him "the late Commander Gore". [[artifact:franklin-1845-012|Full text and pin]].

McClintock's judgement stands up: "A sad tale was never told in fewer words."

## The four things it gets wrong, or seems to

**The Beechey date.** Gore writes that the ships wintered at Beechey "in 1846-7". They wintered there in 1845-6. McClintock caught it at once — the dates at the top and bottom of the sheet make it impossible — and it is a slip of the pen, nothing more. It is a useful warning about the document all the same: the man writing "All well" got a basic fact wrong.

**The landing position.** The record says the party landed "here" at lat. 69° 37' 42" N., long. 98° 41' W. Victory Point on a modern chart is near 69° 58' N, 98° 33' W — more than twenty miles further north. The record is a naval position taken with instruments by experienced officers, so this is not a rounding error. Possible explanations: a copying slip in the latitude, an observation taken elsewhere and written up later, or an error in the survey the ships were working from. This layer pins both quote artifacts at Victory Point, where the paper was found, and not at the latitude the paper states. That is a choice, and it is flagged here so a later reader can undo it.

**The nine unaccounted for.** McClintock did the arithmetic on the spot: 138 in the original complement, minus 24 dead, leaves 114, but only 105 landed, "so that 9 individuals are unaccounted for." The gap is in his complement figure, not in the record. The muster gives 134 men sailing from Greenhithe and five sent home from Greenland in the transport, leaving 129. 129 minus 24 is 105 exactly. The record's arithmetic is sound; the famous discrepancy dissolves once the right starting number is used. Older accounts that treat the nine as missing men are following McClintock's slip.

**The officers.** Nine of roughly twenty-four officers dead against fifteen of about 105 ratings is a death rate three or four times higher among the officers, in a service where officers ate better. No explanation for that has ever been agreed on.

## What it does not say

It does not say what killed Franklin, or where he was buried, or what the expedition had achieved in 1847, or what condition the men were in, or what the plan was beyond "Back's Fish River". There is no list of the dead. There is no mention of the ice conditions, the state of the provisions, or whether anyone expected to be found. Everything the expedition might usefully have recorded in nineteen months of besetment either was never written or did not survive.

The rest of what is known comes from the people who lived there. See [[page:franklin-1845-inuit-testimony]].
