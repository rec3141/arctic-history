Both ships were found in the water Inuit witnesses had described, within two years of each other, 166 and 168 years after they were abandoned. Neither was found where the Victory Point record says they were left.

## HMS *Erebus*, 2 September 2014

Parks Canada had been surveying for the wrecks since 2008. The 2014 season, the Victoria Strait Expedition, put an unusual fleet in the water: the Coast Guard icebreaker *Sir Wilfrid Laurier*, HMCS *Kingston*, the Arctic Research Foundation's *Martin Bergmann*, the *One Ocean Voyager*, and Parks Canada's small research vessel *Investigator*, launched from the *Laurier*'s well deck each morning to tow side-scan sonar from seven in the morning until eight at night.

Ice closed the intended northern search area. The search moved south — into the water off Adelaide Peninsula that [[person:In-nook-poo-zhee-jook]] had pointed out on McClintock's chart in 1869 and that [[person:Ikinnelikpatolok]] had described to Schwatka's party in 1879. Then a Government of Nunavut archaeology team working ashore on a small island found an iron davit fitting from a Royal Navy ship, and a deck hawse plug. That fixed the area.

On the second survey line in the new area, the wreck came up on the screen whole. Ryan Harris, who had been looking at that screen since 2008, later said he would liken it to winning the Stanley Cup. A short ROV dive a few days later found two cannon lying off the stern. The find was announced in Ottawa on 9 September 2014 and the ship was confirmed as the *Erebus* on 1 October.

She lies in about 11 metres of water in [[place:Wilmot and Crampton Bay]], near [[place:O'Reilly Island]], upright, largely intact, but shallow enough that autumn storms and moving ice work at her every year. That is roughly where Hall wrote down, from In-nook-poo-zhee-jook's finger on the chart, "lat. 68° 30' N., long. 99° W."

## HMS *Terror*, 3 September 2016

After 2014 the search moved back north, on the reasonable logic that if one ship had gone south, the other was probably nearer where the crews left them, in northern Alexandra Strait.

On 3 September 2016 the *Martin Bergmann* was running from [[place:Uqsuqtuuq]] (Gjoa Haven) up to that northern area. [[person:Sammy Kogvik]], a lifelong resident of Gjoa Haven, had been aboard for a day. He mentioned to a crewmate that some years earlier, on a hunting trip, he had seen a large piece of wood standing out of the ice in [[place:Terror Bay]]. Similar accounts had circulated in Gjoa Haven for years. The crew turned into the bay and dropped a sonar.

They found a three-masted ship sitting upright on the bottom, about 100 kilometres from where the rest of the search team was surveying. An ROV showed intact crew quarters, a mess hall, a food store. Parks Canada's divers, delayed three days by weather, worked the site and on 18 September confirmed her as the *Terror* from the double-wheeled helm and the iron sheathing on the bow, checked against nineteenth-century ship drawings.

She is in deeper, sheltered water and is astonishingly well preserved — hatches closed, some window glass still in place. Which raises the question the archaeologists put first: not what she is, but why she is there, upright, tidy and 100 kilometres from where she was abandoned. She was not left at anchor. A small boat appears to have gone down with her.

## What this does to the story

The written record ends on 26 April 1848 with 105 men walking south. The wrecks say something the record cannot: at least one ship, and probably both, moved a long way after that, and did so under some kind of control. A ship does not drift 100 kilometres into a bay and settle upright with her hatches closed. This is exactly what Inuit testimony had said for a century and a half — that men returned to the ships, that at least one was sailed south, that Inuit boarded her at Utjulik and found a dead man in a locked cabin, and that she sank there after a hole was cut in her side. See [[page:franklin-1845-inuit-testimony]].

It also means the expedition's own men, or some of them, very likely completed the last unsailed link of the Northwest Passage — going south along the west side of King William Island in a ship — thirty years before Nordenskiöld and fifty-five before Amundsen. Hall said as much in 1869, and was ignored.

## Whose wrecks they are

The two sites together are the Wrecks of HMS Erebus and HMS Terror National Historic Site, the first national historic site in Nunavut cooperatively managed with Inuit. Britain transferred ownership of the wrecks and their contents to Canada and the Inuit Heritage Trust. Parks Canada runs an Inuit Guardians programme out of [[place:Uqsuqtuuq]], and the community holds the annual Umiyaqtutt Festival marking the finds. Diving seasons since 2015 have recovered well over a thousand objects from the *Erebus*, worked from vessels including [[artifact:franklin-1845-008|the barge *Qiniqtiryuaq*]]; the site is deteriorating, and the recovery is partly a race with the weather.

[[person:Louie Kamookak]] of Gjoa Haven, who spent more than thirty years collecting elders' accounts and matching them to the written record and to place names, did not live to see the *Terror* fully surveyed; he died in 2018. Parks Canada's own account of the discoveries says plainly that Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit, together with western science, is what found the ships.

They are also graves. At least one man was still aboard the *Erebus* when Inuit boarded her, and the seabed around both wrecks is an archaeological site under a Superintendent's order. Vessels working these waters — this one included — are surveying a burial place.
