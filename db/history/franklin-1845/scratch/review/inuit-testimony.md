# What the Inuit witnesses said, and what was done with it

Everything known about the Franklin expedition after 26 April 1848 comes from three kinds of evidence: bones and objects found on the ground; the two wrecks found in 2014 and 2016; and the accounts of Inuit families who saw the ships, boarded them, met the men walking and found the dead. The third came first, was the most detailed, and was disbelieved the longest.

## Rae, 1854

In April 1854 [[person:John Rae]] was surveying the west shore of Boothia for the Hudson's Bay Company. At [[place:Kugaaruk]] (Pelly Bay) he met Inuit, and from one of them learned "that a party of 'white men' (Kablounans) had perished from want of food some distance to the westward, and not far beyond a large river, containing many falls and rapids." Rae's published report does not name the man; he is generally identified as [[person:In-nook-poo-zhee-jook]], who told the same story to [[person:Charles Francis Hall]] a decade later, though that identification is later scholarship and not Rae's own. Over the following months Rae gathered more and bought relics: silver forks and spoons with officers' crests, pieces of watches, telescopes, guns. [[artifact:franklin-1845-007|The *Illustrated London News* engraved them]] when they reached the Admiralty that November. He wrote his report at [[place:Naujaat|Repulse Bay]] on 29 July 1854.

> In the spring, four winters past (spring 1850), a party of "white men," amounting to about forty, were seen travelling southward over the ice, and dragging a boat with them, by some Esquimaux, who were killing seals near the north shore of King William's Land... their ship, or ships, had been crushed by ice, and... they were now going to where they expected to find deer to shoot.

> From the mutilated state of many of the corpses and the contents of the kettles, it is evident that our wretched countrymen had been driven to the last resource — cannibalism — as a means of prolonging existence.

[[artifact:franklin-1845-014|The first]] and [[artifact:franklin-1845-013|the second]] are pinned where they belong. Rae was scrupulous about what he had and had not seen: "None of the Esquimaux with whom I conversed had seen the 'whites,' nor had they ever been at the place where the bodies were found, but had their information from those who had been there."

## What England did with it

It attacked him. [[person:Charles Dickens]] wrote a two-part answer in his magazine *Household Words* on 2 and 9 December 1854, at [[person:Jane Franklin|Lady Franklin]]'s request. His case was not that Rae had misread the evidence but that the witnesses could not be believed at all. He set "the vague babble of savages" against the character of picked English seamen, wrote that "we believe every savage to be in his heart covetous, treacherous and cruel", and offered the alternative that "no man can, with any show of reason, undertake to affirm that this sad remnant of Franklin's gallant band were not set upon and slain by the Esquimaux themselves." That is [[source:dickens1854lost]], and it worked. The Admiralty's reward for ascertaining the fate of the expedition went to Rae and his party, and little else did: no knighthood, no naval command, and a lasting reputation as the man who slandered the dead.

Every substantive part of Rae's report has since been confirmed. Cut marks made by metal blades have been found on Franklin remains from several sites, most fully at NgLj-2 in [[place:Erebus Bay]], and in 2024 a tooth from that coast was identified by DNA as [[person:James Fitzjames]], captain of [[vessel:HMS Erebus|the *Erebus*]]. The mutilated corpses were real. The Inuit witnesses were telling the truth, and the men who called them liars were not there. See [[page:franklin-1845-death-march]].

## Hall, 1864-1869

Charles Francis Hall lived among Inuit in the Repulse Bay region for five years, learned to travel as they did, and questioned witnesses at length with [[person:Tookoolito]] and [[person:Ebierbing]] interpreting. He was credulous in places, and says himself he sometimes doubted his informants, but the record he brought back is the fullest body of Franklin testimony that exists: [[source:nourse1879narrative]], [[artifact:franklin-1845-010|full text here]].

In April 1866 an elder, [[person:Kok-lee-arng-nun]], and his wife told Hall they had often been aboard the ships and had known both Too-loo-ark and Ag-loo-ka — [[person:John Franklin|Franklin]] and [[person:Francis Crozier|Crozier]] — by their different ways of shaking hands. Too-loo-ark took a man's hand and gave it a few warm and friendly shakes; Ag-loo-ka's was short and jerky. After the first summer and first winter they saw no more of Too-loo-ark, and Ag-loo-ka was the ish-e-mut-ta, the boss. If their acquaintance began when the ships were beset in September 1846, that puts Too-loo-ark's disappearance in the summer of 1847, which is where the written record puts Franklin's death — 11 June 1847. The couple had no access to the written record; it had not been found.

They also described the loss of one ship:

> the ship on board of which they had often seen Too-loo-ark was overwhelmed with heavy ice in the spring of the year... the ice turned the vessel down on its side, crushing the masts and breaking a hole in her bottom and so overwhelming her that she sank at once... Several men at work in her could not get out in time, and were carried down with her and drowned. "On this account Ag-loo-ka's company had died of starvation, for they had not time to get the provisions out of her."

[[artifact:franklin-1845-015|That passage is pinned at the place of the interview.]] The other ship was different. She was seen near Ook-goo-lik "in complete order, having three masts and four boats hanging at the davits — whale-ship like." The Inuit feared to go aboard for a long time; when they did they found no one, and took what they wanted.

In-nook-poo-zhee-jook guided Hall on King William Island in the spring of 1869 and told him more. On 4 May, in an igloo with the interpreters present and McClintock's printed chart spread out, Hall asked him where the ship had sunk, and he put his finger on the place: [[artifact:franklin-1845-016|"very near O'Reilly Island, a little eastward of the north end of said island, between it and Wilmot and Crampton Bay."]] The men of the place had gone aboard her, he said, and to get into the cabin they knocked a hole through the door because it was locked; inside they found a dead man whose body was very large and heavy, his teeth very long, and it took five men to lift him. They left him where he lay. Writing to Henry Grinnell that July, Hall set the position down as lat. 68° 30' N., long. 99° W., and dated the boarding to early in the spring of 1849.

## Schwatka's party, 1879

Ten years later [[person:Frederick Schwatka]] took a sledge party across the same ground and questioned witnesses again. [[artifact:franklin-1845-035|At an Ookjoolik camp]] they found [[person:Ikinnelikpatolok]], about sixty-five or seventy, who as a boy had shaken hands with Sir George Back's party on Back's River. The next white man he saw was dead in a bunk aboard a big ship frozen in the ice near an island about five miles due west of Grant Point. His people walked three miles out over smooth ice to reach her.

> They did not know how to get inside by the doors, and cut a hole in the side of the ship, on a level with the ice, so that when the ice broke up during the following summer the ship filled and sunk.

They found knives, forks, spoons, pans, cups and plates aboard and took what they could use; they saw books and left them. [[artifact:franklin-1845-017|That is the sinking of HMS *Erebus*]], described by a man who watched it, printed in [[source:gilder1881schwatkas]] in 1881 and treated for the next 133 years as an anecdote. Gilder's book also prints [[artifact:franklin-1845-037|the council with the Netsilingmiut]] at which much of the testimony was taken down, and [[artifact:franklin-1845-036|the woman who came to the camp to speak for her people]]; [[artifact:franklin-1845-053|the whole scanned volume is here]].

## The trouble with reading testimony as navigation

None of this is a survey. Informants were describing events they had seen as children or heard from parents, through interpreters, to men holding charts drawn on assumptions they had no reason to share. Place names moved; the same story reaches us under more than one informant's name; distances given in days' travel do not convert cleanly into miles. [[artifact:franklin-1845-002|The drift track]] on the map draws the movement the testimony implies, labelled as testimony, with its one inferred leg marked.

But the shape held. Two ships. One crushed and sunk quickly with men aboard. One carried south, deserted, boarded, and eventually flooded through a hole cut in her side near an island off the Adelaide Peninsula. That is what the Inuit said. It is where the wrecks were found: [[page:franklin-1845-the-wrecks]].
