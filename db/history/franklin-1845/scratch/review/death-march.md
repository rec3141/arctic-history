# The death march down King William Island

On 26 April 1848, 105 men started south from [[place:Victory Point]] to walk to the Back River. By [[person:Francis Leopold McClintock|McClintock]]'s reckoning of the distances it is about two hundred miles of coast and sea ice to the river's mouth, and then roughly a thousand miles up the river and across country to the nearest Hudson's Bay Company post. They were hauling boats on sledges. None of them made it.

What follows is not a narrative, because no narrative survives. It is a list of the places where things were found, in the order they lie along the coast.

## The evidence, going south

**Victory Point.** [[person:William Hobson]] found the cairn and [[artifact:franklin-1845-012|the record]] on 6 May 1859, and around it a scatter of gear thrown down "as if these men, aware that they were retreating for their lives, had there abandoned everything which they considered superfluous."

**[[place:Erebus Bay]], on the west coast**, which [[artifact:franklin-1845-039|Schwatka's party crossed on the ice in 1879]]. [[artifact:franklin-1845-022|A 28-foot boat on a heavy oak sledge]], with two skeletons in her, five watches, twenty-six pieces of officers' silver, five or six books, and for provisions a little tea and nearly forty pounds of chocolate. Two double-barrelled guns stood muzzle upwards against her side, one barrel of each loaded and cocked. The sledge was pointing north-east, back towards the ships. In 1992 an unrecorded Franklin site, NgLj-2, was found in the same bay and excavated the next year: nearly four hundred human bones and bone fragments, a minimum of eleven men, with cut marks made by metal blades on about a quarter of them. [[source:keenleyside1997final|Anne Keenleyside, Margaret Bertulli and Henry Fricke]] published the analysis in *Arctic* in 1997, and [[artifact:franklin-1845-055|it is here]]. In 2024 a tooth from remains recovered on this coast in 1993 was matched by Y-chromosome DNA to a living descendant of one of the expedition's senior officers, at a genetic distance of one. [[person:Douglas R. Stenton]], Stephen Fratpietro and Robert Park identified the man as Captain [[person:James Fitzjames]] of [[vessel:HMS Erebus|the *Erebus*]], who died less than 80 km from where the ships were deserted; [[artifact:franklin-1845-054|their paper is here]].

**Near [[place:Cape John Herschel|Cape Herschel]], on the south coast.** McClintock, walking the other way shortly after midnight on 25 May 1859, came on a young man lying face down on a gravel ridge near the beach, in the dress of a steward or officer's servant, with a clothes brush and a horn pocket comb beside him. Twenty years later [[person:Frederick Schwatka]]'s party built [[artifact:franklin-1845-038|snow houses on the same cape]] while searching the ground again.

**Todd Islands, off the south coast.** [[person:In-nook-poo-zhee-jook]] guided [[person:Charles Francis Hall]] there on 11 May 1869 to the place where five men were said to be. Deep snow defeated the search; Hall found part of a human thigh bone that appeared to have been fractured not long before. A witness named Poo-yet-ta said the bodies had not been buried when he first saw them but were lying close together, each fully dressed and unmutilated, with a jack-knife in one man's pocket and cans of meat beside them.

**Near the mouth of the Peffer River, on the mainland shore.** The next day Hall's attendants dug through the snow and found one unburied skeleton. He raised an American flag half-mast over it, built a stone monument five feet high, and fired a salute. The one entire skeleton Hall carried home came from this coast; it went on to England and was identified, by the plug of a tooth, as Lieutenant Le Vesconte of [[vessel:HMS Erebus|the *Erebus*]].

**[[place:Starvation Cove]], on the Adelaide Peninsula near Richardson Point.** The furthest point any of them reached. Inuit found a boat and skeletons here. Schwatka's party reached the place in 1879. [[person:Ebierbing]] and a party of Netsilingmiut searched it first and found, in a pile of stones, a small pewter medal struck for the launch of the steamer *Great Britain* in 1843, and among the seaweed some pieces of blanket and a skull — "all that could be seen at this memorable spot". [[person:Heinrich Klutschak]], the party's artist and surveyor, followed and built a monument over what remained; [[artifact:franklin-1845-045|his own camp]] and [[artifact:franklin-1845-023|Schwatka]] are among the plates he drew for the book. Five miles inland, Inuit found another skeleton that summer, a sailor by his clothing, because the party had promised rewards for anything new and kept their promises.

An old woman named Toolooah — not [[person:Toolooah|the dog driver of that name]] who worked for Schwatka, but an elder who shared it — had been with the party who found the boat and the skeletons at Starvation Cove, and she "confirmed the testimony previously obtained in every essential particular."

Starvation Cove is on the mainland. They got across. They did not get up the river, and [[artifact:franklin-1845-046|the Back River]], [[artifact:franklin-1845-047|its rapids]] and [[artifact:franklin-1845-048|what a march in that cold looks like]] are in Schwatka's book to show what they would have faced.

## The records

Schwatka's party concluded that the expedition's papers had been carried to the boat place at Starvation Cove and destroyed there. William Gilder put it plainly in [[source:gilder1881schwatkas]]: the most important direct result of the expedition's labours was "the establishing of the loss of the Franklin records at the boat place in Starvation Cove... Next in importance to their recovery must be considered the knowledge of their irrecoverable loss."

Inuit accounts describe children given papers to play with, and paper scattered by the wind. This is not vandalism and should not be read as such: a bundle of Admiralty paper found beside dead strangers is not obviously a document to anyone who has no reason to think so, and no one has ever suggested Inuit had reason to.

## Cannibalism

[[person:John Rae]] reported in 1854, from Inuit testimony, that "from the mutilated state of many of the corpses and the contents of the kettles, it is evident that our wretched countrymen had been driven to the last resource — cannibalism — as a means of prolonging existence." He was disbelieved and vilified for it. [[artifact:franklin-1845-013|The sentence is here.]]

Physical anthropology has confirmed it since the 1990s. Ninety-two of the bones at NgLj-2 carry cut marks, from 2 to 27 millimetres long, on clavicles, scapulae, humeri, radii, pelvic bones, ribs, vertebrae, femora, tibiae and the small bones of the hands and feet. Under a scanning electron microscope they show the straight edges, V-shaped section and high depth-to-width ratio of cuts made by metal blades, not by animal teeth or stone tools, and they cluster round the joints — the pattern left by taking flesh off a body. Three long bones had also been broken open to the marrow cavity, though weathering made it impossible to say how soon after death. The 2024 identification puts a name and a rank to it.

Reported factually, this is what it is: the last stage of a starvation from which nobody was coming to help. It says nothing about the character of the men involved, which is roughly what Rae said in 1854 and what his critics refused to hear.

## What is still unknown

Whether the party stayed together after Victory Point or split. Whether some turned back to the ships — the sledge at Erebus Bay was pointing north-east, and the position of the [[page:franklin-1845-the-wrecks|wrecks]] suggests at least one ship was later remanned and sailed south. How long the last men survived: [[artifact:franklin-1845-014|Rae's informants described about forty men still walking in the spring of 1850]], two years after the record, and the accounts of [[person:Francis Crozier]] as Aglooka walking towards Back's River with one companion cannot be dated at all.

Some of this may yet be answered from the wrecks. Most of it will not.
