# The Franklin expedition, 1845-1848

The Franklin expedition was the largest and best-equipped polar expedition Britain ever sent out, and the one that vanished most completely. Two ships, 129 men after the last discharges, three years of provisions, and a single sheet of paper to show for all of it. Everything else we know about what happened after the summer of 1845 comes from bones, abandoned gear, two wrecks, and the accounts of the Inuit families who lived on and around King William Island and watched it happen.

## The ships and the men

[[vessel:HMS Erebus]] and [[vessel:HMS Terror]] were bomb vessels, built to take the recoil of heavy mortars and therefore built very strong. Both had been to the Antarctic with [[person:James Clark Ross]]. For 1845 they were fitted with railway locomotive engines driving screw propellers, steam heating, iron plating on the bows, and provisions for three years. [[person:John Franklin|Sir John Franklin]], 59 years old and a former Lieutenant-Governor of Van Diemen's Land, commanded from [[vessel:HMS Erebus|the *Erebus*]], with [[person:James Fitzjames]] as his captain; [[person:Francis Crozier|Francis Rawdon Moira Crozier]], who had already served in the Antarctic and under Parry, commanded [[vessel:HMS Terror|the *Terror*]].

134 men left the United Kingdom. Five were sent home from Greenland — four in the transport *Barretto Junior* and one in HMS *Rattler* — leaving 129 who entered the ice. That figure is not a modern reconstruction: [[person:Francis Leopold McClintock]] printed it in 1859 on the Admiralty's own authority, in the conclusion to [[source:mcclintock1859voyage]].

Fourteen of the officers sat for [[artifact:franklin-1845-003|daguerreotype portraits]] at Greenhithe in May 1845, in the week before sailing. The plates survive, and so do [[artifact:franklin-1845-004|the engravings the *Illustrated London News* cut from them]] six years later, when the men in them had been dead for three years and nobody in England knew it.

## What the ships did

[[artifact:franklin-1845-001|The reconstructed route]] leaves [[place:Greenhithe]] on 19 May 1845, calls at [[place:Stromness]] in Orkney, and reaches the [[place:Whalefish Islands]] in Disko Bay in early July, where the stores are taken out of the transport and the last letters home are written. In the last week of July the whalers *Prince of Wales* and *Enterprise* spoke to the ships in [[place:Qimusseriarsuaq|Melville Bay]], waiting for the middle ice to open; the meeting is usually dated 26 July 1845, though some accounts put it two days later. That is the last time any European sees them.

Everything after that is known from one document, found in a cairn at [[place:Victory Point]] on King William Island in May 1859. Its first entry, written by Lieutenant [[person:Graham Gore]] and Mate [[person:Charles Des Voeux]] on 28 May 1847, is almost cheerful: the ships had gone up [[place:Wellington Channel]] to latitude 77 north, come back down the west side of [[place:Cornwallis Island]], wintered at [[place:Beechey Island]], and were now wintering in the ice off King William Island. [[artifact:franklin-1845-011|"All well."]]

The second entry, written round the margins of the same sheet eleven months later, is one of the bleakest documents in the history of exploration. Sir John Franklin died on 11 June 1847. Twenty-four men were dead. The ships had been beset since 12 September 1846 and were deserted on 22 April 1848. The 105 survivors were starting for Back's Fish River. [[artifact:franklin-1845-012|The record itself]] is on this map, transcribed from the facsimile in McClintock's narrative.

None of them reached the river.

The expeditions that went looking for these ships — Austin, Belcher, Kennedy, Rae, McClintock, and the western squadrons — belong to [[topic:franklin-search]] and [[topic:mcclure-investigator]], not here. These pages are about the expedition itself and what became of it.

## Why the disagreements matter

Almost nothing about the second half of this story is settled, and the pages say so where it is true. The record gives a landing position, 69 degrees 37 minutes 42 seconds north, 98 degrees 41 minutes west, that does not agree with Victory Point on a modern chart. The nine men McClintock at first thought the record left unaccounted for were an error in his own complement figure, and he corrected it himself before the same book went to press. The record's statement that the ships wintered at Beechey "in 1846-7" is simply a slip for 1845-6, as McClintock noticed at once. Inuit testimony describes at least one ship remanned and sailed south after the abandonment, which the record can neither confirm nor deny. Where the sources disagree, these pages report the disagreement rather than choosing.

## Why it still matters here

Two points. First: the expedition's failure was read in Britain for a century and a half as a mystery, when the essential facts had been given plainly to John Rae in 1854 and to Charles Francis Hall in 1866-69 by Inuit witnesses, and were disbelieved because of who was speaking. The wrecks were found where that testimony said they were. That is a lesson about evidence, not only about Franklin.

Second: this is not distant history in Nunavut. The wreck sites lie in the waters of the Kitikmeot, and [[place:Uqsuqtuuq]] (Gjoa Haven) is the community nearest to both. [[person:Louie Kamookak]] of Uqsuqtuuq spent decades collecting the oral accounts that helped narrow the search area. The two sites are now a national historic site managed by Parks Canada together with Inuit — the Inuit Heritage Trust and the Nattilik Heritage Society, with support from the Kitikmeot Inuit Association — and what is recovered from the wrecks is jointly owned by Parks Canada and the Inuit Heritage Trust. Ships working these waters, this one included, are surveying seabed that is also an archaeological site and a burial place.
