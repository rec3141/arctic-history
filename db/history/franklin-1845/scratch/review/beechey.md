# Beechey Island: the first winter and the three graves

[[place:Beechey Island]] is not really an island. It is a limestone block joined to the south-west corner of [[place:Devon Island]] by a gravel isthmus, with cliffs on three sides and a sheltered bay on the fourth. It is the one place on earth where you can still stand in a spot the Franklin expedition demonstrably occupied and know roughly what they did there, because they left the ground covered in evidence and three of them in it.

## The first winter, 1845-46

[[person:Graham Gore|Gore]]'s record of 28 May 1847 gives the fact in one line: the ships wintered at Beechey Island in lat. 74 deg 43' 28" N., long. 91 deg 39' 15" W., "after having ascended Wellington Channel to lat. 77 deg., and returned by the west side of Cornwallis Island."

That single sentence covers an extraordinary first season. From [[place:Qimusseriarsuaq|Melville Bay]] in late July 1845 the ships crossed to [[place:Lancaster Sound]], turned north up [[place:Wellington Channel]], sailed a hundred and fifty miles up it to latitude 77 — as far as any ship would get for another eight years — found the way blocked or thought better of it, came back down the far side of [[place:Cornwallis Island]] by a channel not then on any chart, and secured for the winter at Beechey some time in September. [[person:Francis Leopold McClintock|McClintock]], reading the record twelve years later, wrote: "Seldom has such an amount of success been accorded to an Arctic navigator in a single season."

Gore's record misdates that winter as 1846-7. It was 1845-6. McClintock spotted the error immediately: the dates at the top and bottom of the sheet prove it.

## The three graves

Three men died there. [[person:John Torrington]], leading stoker of [[vessel:HMS Terror|the *Terror*]], on 1 January 1846, aged 20. [[person:John Hartnell]], able seaman of [[vessel:HMS Erebus|the *Erebus*]], on 4 January, aged 25. [[person:William Braine]], private of the Royal Marines, on 3 April, aged 32. Their shipmates cut headboards, carved the names, ages, ships and dates on them, and set them at the head of three graves in the gravel above the beach. [[artifact:franklin-1845-005|Elisha Kent Kane sketched the three boards]] at the searchers' first sight of the place in the summer of 1850, and [[artifact:franklin-1845-006|they still stand]] — replicas now, with the originals conserved in Ottawa. A fourth grave beside them holds Thomas Morgan of [[vessel:HMS Investigator]], who died at Beechey in 1854 after crossing the ice from Mercy Bay; the last man buried here belongs to [[topic:mcclure-investigator|the search]], not to the expedition.

Three deaths in a first polar winter out of 129 men is not, by the standards of the period, alarming. Nobody reading those boards in 1850 thought they explained a catastrophe.

## What Ommanney found, and what he did not

On 23 August 1850 a cairn on the summit of Beechey Island caught the eye of HMS *Assistance*. Captain [[person:Erasmus Ommanney]] landed and found the expedition's winter quarters: the graves, the foundations of a storehouse and a forge, a carpenter's and an armourer's working place, sledge tracks, scraps of clothing, and a famous heap of empty preserved-meat tins, stacked and filled with gravel. Within days ten search vessels were anchored off the island.

They found no written record. Not in the cairn, not anywhere. This is the strangest fact about Beechey Island and no one has explained it. A naval expedition wintering at a known landfall would normally leave a document saying where it meant to go next. Either they left one and it was not found, or it was taken, or Franklin did not leave one. Because of that absence, the searchers of 1850-51 had to guess Franklin's route, and most of them guessed north up Wellington Channel — the direction he had already tried and abandoned. The search went the wrong way for years. That story belongs to [[topic:franklin-search]].

## The exhumations

In 1984 and 1986 Owen Beattie's team from the University of Alberta exhumed all three men from the permafrost — Torrington in 1984, Hartnell and Braine in 1986. The bodies were nearly intact; the photographs of Torrington's face are among the most widely reproduced images in northern history and are, it should be said, photographs of a 20-year-old stoker's corpse, taken without any consent he could have given.

The autopsies found pneumonia and tuberculosis, and high lead levels in bone and hair. For twenty years that supported a lead-poisoning explanation for the whole disaster, usually blamed on the solder of the tinned provisions. It has not held up. In 2018 [[artifact:franklin-1845-056|Treena Swanston and her colleagues]] imaged the microscopic structure of Franklin bone with a synchrotron beam and asked when the lead went in. The men who survived longest were not the more heavily leaded; the bone laid down in their last months was not the most leaded; and against a comparison group of Royal Navy remains from Antigua, the Franklin samples are not unusual for the period. Their conclusion, in [[source:swanston2018franklin]], is that the exposure was long-term and largely predated the voyage, and that lead did not play a pivotal role in the loss of the expedition. Sources disagree here and the disagreement is worth naming: [[source:keenleyside1997final|Keenleyside, Bertulli and Fricke]], working on the bones from [[place:Erebus Bay]] in 1997, still argued that lead poisoning contributed. Tuberculosis, pneumonia and eventually starvation and scurvy are enough on their own. This page treats the single-cause lead version as superseded rather than proved.

## Standing there now

Beechey Island is a protected historic site, and cruise ships and research vessels call there in the open-water season. The artefacts on the beach are protected and visitors are asked to keep to the marked ground. What makes the place worth the stop is not the graves but the ordinary domestic wreckage around them: the tin heap, the forge, the place where somebody's workbench stood. It is the only surviving trace of the expedition still doing what it was built to do, three years before everything went wrong.
