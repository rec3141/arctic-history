# The Victory Point record

One sheet of paper. That is the entire written output of the Franklin expedition after July 1845 — one printed Admiralty blank, filled in twice by different hands eleven months apart, recovered from a cairn at [[place:Victory Point]] on the north-west coast of King William Island on 6 May 1859 by Lieutenant [[person:William Hobson]] of [[vessel:Fox|the *Fox*]]. It is now at Royal Museums Greenwich, catalogue number AAA2069, and [[source:mcclintock1859voyage]] printed it in facsimile.

## The form

It was one of the printed forms, in McClintock's words, "usually supplied to discovery ships for the purpose of being enclosed in bottles and thrown overboard at sea, in order to ascertain the set of the currents, blanks being left for the date and position". Whoever found one was asked to forward it to the Secretary of the Admiralty with a note of time and place — a request printed on the sheet in six different languages. The Navy scattered thousands of them. The irony of the printed instruction is hard to miss: the one that mattered sat in a cairn for eleven years while search ships passed within a hundred miles of it.

## Gore's entry, 28 May 1847

> 28 of May 1847. H. M. ships 'Erebus' and 'Terror' wintered in the ice in lat. 70° 05' N., long. 98° 23' W. Having wintered in 1846-7 at Beechey Island, in lat. 74° 43' 28" N., long. 91° 39' 15" W., after having ascended Wellington Channel to lat. 77°, and returned by the west side of Cornwallis Island. Sir John Franklin commanding the expedition. All well. Party consisting of 2 officers and 6 men left the ships on Monday 24th May, 1847. Gm. Gore, Lieut. Chas. F. Des Vœux, Mate.

[[artifact:franklin-1845-011|Full text and pin]]. Fewer than a hundred words, and they carry the whole first half of the voyage: [[place:Beechey Island]], [[place:Wellington Channel]], [[place:Cornwallis Island]], and the ice off King William Island.

## Fitzjames's and Crozier's entry, 25 April 1848

Written round the margins of the same sheet:

> April 25, 1848. — H. M. ships 'Terror' and 'Erebus' were deserted on the 22nd April, 5 leagues N.N.W. of this, having been beset since 12th September, 1846. The officers and crews, consisting of 105 souls, under the command of Captain F. R. M. Crozier, landed here in lat. 69° 37' 42" N., long. 98° 41' W. Sir John Franklin died on the 11th June, 1847; and the total loss by deaths in the expedition has been to this date 9 officers and 15 men.

Signed by [[person:Francis Crozier]] as "Captain and Senior Officer" and by [[person:James Fitzjames]] as "Captain H.M.S. Erebus", with one more line in Crozier's hand: "and start (on) to-morrow, 26th, for Back's Fish River." [[person:Francis Leopold McClintock|McClintock]] judged the marginal writing to be Fitzjames's, except the last line, which was Crozier's own. A further marginal note records that the paper had been moved to where it was found — the site of Sir James Ross's pillar — from a spot four miles to the northward where Gore had originally deposited it, and calls him "the late Commander Gore". "This little word *late*", McClintock wrote, "shows us that he too, within the twelvemonth, had passed away." [[artifact:franklin-1845-012|Full text and pin]].

McClintock's judgement stands up: "A sad tale was never told in fewer words."

## The four things it gets wrong, or seems to

**The Beechey date.** [[person:Graham Gore|Gore]] writes that the ships wintered at Beechey "in 1846-7". They wintered there in 1845-6. McClintock caught it at once — the dates at the top and bottom of the sheet make it impossible — and it is a slip of the pen, nothing more. It is a useful warning about the document all the same: the man writing "All well" got a basic fact wrong.

**The landing position.** The record says the party landed "here" at lat. 69° 37' 42" N., long. 98° 41' W. Victory Point on a modern chart is near 69° 58' N, 98° 33' W — more than twenty miles further north. The record is a naval position taken with instruments by experienced officers, so this is not a rounding error. Possible explanations: a copying slip in the latitude, an observation taken elsewhere and written up later, or an error in the survey the ships were working from. Both quote artifacts are pinned at Victory Point, where the paper was found, rather than at the latitude the paper states. That choice is noted here so a later reader can undo it.

**The nine unaccounted for.** McClintock did the arithmetic on the spot: 138 in the original complement, minus 24 dead, leaves 114, but only 105 landed, "so that 9 individuals are unaccounted for." He then corrected himself before the same book went to press. In its conclusion he states, "on the authority of the Admiralty, that only one hundred and thirty-four individuals left the United Kingdom, and of these five men subsequently returned: one by H.M.S. 'Rattler,' and four by the transport 'Barretto Junior;' so that only one hundred and twenty-nine — the exact number mentioned in the record — actually entered the ice." 129 minus 24 is 105 exactly. The record's arithmetic is sound, the gap was in the received complement figure of 138, and the man who found the discrepancy is also the man who closed it. Later accounts that treat the nine as missing men are reading only page 288 and not page 350.

**The officers.** Nine of roughly twenty-four officers dead against fifteen of about a hundred and five ratings is a death rate two and a half times higher among the officers, in a service where officers ate better. No explanation for that has ever been agreed on.

## What it does not say

It does not say what killed Franklin, or where he was buried, or what the expedition had achieved in 1847, or what condition the men were in, or what the plan was beyond "Back's Fish River". There is no list of the dead. There is no mention of the ice conditions, the state of the provisions, or whether anyone expected to be found. Everything the expedition might usefully have recorded in nineteen months of besetment either was never written or did not survive.
