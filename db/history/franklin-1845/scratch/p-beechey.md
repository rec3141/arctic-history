[[place:Beechey Island]] is not really an island. It is a limestone block joined to the south-west corner of Devon Island by a gravel isthmus, with cliffs on three sides and a sheltered bay on the fourth. It is the one place on earth where you can still stand in a spot the Franklin expedition demonstrably occupied and know roughly what they did there, because they left the ground covered in evidence and three of them in it.

## The first winter, 1845-46

Gore's record of 28 May 1847 gives the fact in one line: the ships wintered at Beechey Island in lat. 74 deg 43' 28" N., long. 91 deg 39' 15" W., "after having ascended Wellington Channel to lat. 77 deg., and returned by the west side of Cornwallis Island."

That single sentence covers an extraordinary first season. From Melville Bay in late July 1845 the ships crossed Baffin Bay, entered Lancaster Sound, turned north up [[place:Wellington Channel]], sailed roughly 150 miles up it to latitude 77 — as far as any ship would get for another eight years — found the way blocked or thought better of it, came back down the far side of Cornwallis Island through a channel not on any chart, and secured for the winter at Beechey some time in September. As McClintock wrote, "Seldom has such an amount of success been accorded to an Arctic navigator in a single season."

Gore's record misdates that winter as 1846-7. It was 1845-6. McClintock spotted the error immediately: the dates at the top and bottom of the sheet prove it.

## The three graves

Three men died there. [[person:John Torrington]], leading stoker of the *Terror*, on 1 January 1846, aged 20. [[person:John Hartnell]], able seaman of the *Erebus*, on 4 January, aged 25. [[person:William Braine]], private of the Royal Marines, on 3 April, aged 32. Their shipmates cut headboards, carved the names, ages, ships and dates on them, and set them at the head of three graves in the gravel above the beach. [[artifact:franklin-1845-006|The headboards are still there]], replaced now by replicas with the originals conserved, and so is a fourth, for a man of the search fleet who died in 1854.

Three deaths in a first Arctic winter out of 129 men is not, by the standards of the period, alarming. Nobody reading those boards in 1850 thought they explained a catastrophe.

## What Ommanney found, and what he did not

On 23 August 1850 a cairn on the summit of Beechey Island caught the eye of HMS *Assistance*. Captain Erasmus Ommanney landed and found the expedition's winter quarters: the graves, the foundations of a storehouse and a forge, a carpenter's and an armourer's working place, sledge tracks, scraps of clothing, and a famous heap of several hundred empty preserved-meat tins, stacked and filled with gravel. Within days ten search vessels were anchored off the island.

They found no written record. Not in the cairn, not anywhere. This is the strangest fact about Beechey Island and no one has explained it. A naval expedition wintering at a known landfall would normally leave a document saying where it meant to go next. Either they left one and it was not found, or it was taken, or Franklin did not leave one. Because of that absence, the searchers of 1850-51 had to guess Franklin's route, and most of them guessed north up Wellington Channel — the direction he had already tried and abandoned. The search went the wrong way for years. That story belongs to [[topic:franklin-search]].

## The exhumations

In 1984 and 1986 Owen Beattie's team from the University of Alberta exhumed all three men from the permafrost. The bodies were nearly intact; the photographs of Torrington's face are among the most widely reproduced images in Arctic history and are, it should be said, photographs of a 20-year-old stoker's corpse, taken without any consent he could have given.

The autopsies found pneumonia and tuberculosis, and high lead levels in bone and hair. For twenty years that supported a lead-poisoning explanation for the whole disaster, usually blamed on the solder of the tinned provisions. It has not held up well. Later work on the isotopes suggests much of the lead was acquired before the men ever sailed, from the ordinary lead exposure of Victorian Britain, and comparisons with contemporary Royal Navy crews who did not die show similar burdens. Tuberculosis, pneumonia and eventually starvation and scurvy are enough on their own. Lead may have contributed; it is no longer a sufficient cause, and this page treats the single-cause version as superseded rather than proved.

## Standing there now

Beechey Island is a Territorial Historic Site, and cruise ships and research vessels call there in the open-water season. The graves are fenced, the artefacts on the beach are protected, and visitors are asked to keep to the marked ground. What makes the place worth the stop is not the graves but the ordinary domestic wreckage around them: the tin heap, the forge, the place where somebody's workbench stood. It is the only surviving trace of the expedition still doing what it was built to do, three years before everything went wrong.
