The Franklin expedition is the largest and best-equipped Arctic expedition Britain ever sent out, and the one that vanished most completely. Two ships, 129 men after the last discharges, three years of provisions, and a single sheet of paper to show for all of it. Everything else we know about what happened after the summer of 1845 comes from bones, abandoned gear, and the accounts of the Inuit families who lived on and around King William Island and watched it happen.

## The ships and the men

HMS *Erebus* and HMS *Terror* were bomb vessels, built to take the recoil of heavy mortars and therefore built very strong. Both had been to the Antarctic with James Clark Ross. For 1845 they were fitted with railway locomotive engines driving screw propellers, steam heating, iron plating on the bows, and provisions for three years. Sir John Franklin, 59 years old and a former Lieutenant-Governor of Van Diemen's Land, commanded from the *Erebus*, with [[person:James Fitzjames]] as his captain; Francis Rawdon Moira Crozier, who had already served in the Antarctic and under Parry, commanded the *Terror*.

134 men sailed. Five were sent home from Greenland, leaving 129.

## What the ships did

[[artifact:franklin-1845-001|The reconstructed route]] leaves [[place:Greenhithe]] on 19 May 1845, calls at Stromness in Orkney, and reaches the Whalefish Islands in Disko Bay in early July, where the stores are taken out of the transport and the last letters home are written. On 26 July 1845 the whalers *Prince of Wales* and *Enterprise* speak to the ships in Melville Bay, waiting for a passage across Baffin Bay. That is the last time any European sees them.

Everything after that is known from one document, found in a cairn at [[place:Victory Point]] on King William Island in May 1859. Its first entry, written by Lieutenant Graham Gore and Mate Charles Des Voeux on 28 May 1847, is almost cheerful: the ships had gone up Wellington Channel to latitude 77 north, come back down the west side of Cornwallis Island, wintered at [[place:Beechey Island]], and were now wintering in the ice off King William Island. [[artifact:franklin-1845-011|"All well."]]

The second entry, scrawled round the margins of the same sheet eleven months later, is one of the bleakest documents in the history of exploration. Sir John Franklin died on 11 June 1847. Twenty-four men were dead. The ships had been beset since 12 September 1846 and were deserted on 22 April 1848. The 105 survivors were starting for Back's Fish River. [[artifact:franklin-1845-012|The record itself]] is on this map, transcribed from the facsimile in [[source:mcclintock1859voyage]].

None of them reached the river.

## Why the disagreements matter

Almost nothing about the second half of this story is settled, and the pages say so where it is true. The Victory Point record gives a landing position, 69 degrees 37 minutes 42 seconds north, 98 degrees 41 minutes west, that does not agree with Victory Point on a modern chart. The nine men McClintock thought it left unaccounted for turn out to be an error in his complement figure, not in the record. Its statement that the ships wintered at Beechey "in 1846-7" is simply an error for 1845-6, as McClintock noticed at once. Inuit testimony describes at least one ship remanned and sailed south after the abandonment, which the record cannot confirm or deny. Where the sources disagree, this topic reports the disagreement rather than choosing.

## Why it still matters here

Two points. First: the expedition's failure was read in Britain for a century and a half as a mystery, when the essential facts had been given plainly to [[person:John Rae]] in 1854 and to [[person:Charles Francis Hall]] in 1866-69 by Inuit witnesses, and were disbelieved because of who was speaking. The wrecks were found where that testimony said they were. That is a lesson about evidence, not only about Franklin.

Second: this is not distant history in Nunavut. The wreck sites lie in the waters of the Kitikmeot, and [[place:Uqsuqtuuq]] (Gjoa Haven) is the community nearest to both. Louie Kamookak of Uqsuqtuuq spent decades collecting the oral accounts that helped narrow the search area, and the wrecks are now co-managed by Parks Canada and the Inuit Heritage Trust. Ships working these waters, this one included, are surveying seabed that is also an archaeological site and a burial place.
