In the summer of 1846 the ships left [[place:Beechey Island]] and went south. Nobody wrote down when they sailed, what the ice was like, or why they chose the route they did. All that survives is the destination they reached: on 12 September 1846 the *Erebus* and *Terror* were beset in the pack at latitude 70 degrees 5 minutes north, longitude 98 degrees 23 minutes west, about twelve miles off the north-west corner of King William Island. They never moved again under sail.

## The wrong side of the island

They had come down Peel Sound and Franklin Strait, which was the obvious route and, in a good ice year, the right one. What they ran into at the bottom of it was the stream of heavy multi-year ice that comes down M'Clintock Channel from the Beaufort Sea and piles against the north-west coast of King William Island. It is the worst ice in the archipelago and it does not reliably clear.

Franklin's charts showed King William Island as attached to Boothia, a peninsula rather than an island. If that were true, the west side was the only way through. It was not true. There is a channel east of the island — Rae Strait, unknown to Franklin because [[person:John Rae]] would not survey it until 1854 — that is shallow, awkward, and almost always free of the M'Clintock Channel ice. Roald Amundsen took the *Gjøa* through it in 1903 and wintered at what is now [[place:Uqsuqtuuq]] (Gjoa Haven), fifty-five years after the *Erebus* and *Terror* stopped moving ninety miles to the north-west. That comparison is made too easily: the *Gjøa* was a 47-ton fishing sloop with six men, not a 370-ton bomb vessel with sixty-odd. But the ice difference is real and it is most of the story.

## Nineteen months

Two winters, and the summer between them, stuck in a moving floe. We know almost nothing about it, which is itself the point: this is the longest stretch of the expedition and the emptiest part of the record.

What the record does say, on one sheet of paper:

- 24 May 1847: Lieutenant [[person:Graham Gore]] and Mate [[person:Charles Des Voeux]] leave the ships with six men, probably to survey the unknown coast between Point Victory and Cape Herschel and close the last gap in the Northwest Passage.
- 28 May 1847: they deposit [[artifact:franklin-1845-011|a record]] at Point Victory. "All well."
- 11 June 1847: Sir John Franklin dies. Fourteen days after "all well". No cause, no place of burial.
- By 25 April 1848: 9 officers and 15 men are dead, Gore among them.
- 22 April 1848: the ships are deserted, five leagues north-north-west of Victory Point.
- 25 April 1848: 105 survivors under [[person:Francis Crozier]] land at Victory Point and leave [[artifact:franklin-1845-012|the second record]].
- 26 April 1848: they start for Back's Fish River.

Nine officers dead out of about twenty-four is a startling proportion — much higher than among the ratings. Nobody has a settled explanation. Scurvy, tuberculosis, lead, botulism from the tinned meat, and simple bad luck have all been argued, and the honest answer is that we do not know.

## Why they left

McClintock, who knew what the ships carried, made the arithmetic plain: they were provisioned to July 1848. Two winters beset had already eaten the margin. Staying meant starving aboard; leaving meant dragging boats and sledges 250 miles to Back's Fish River and then a thousand miles up it to the nearest Hudson's Bay Company post. Crozier chose the march. McClintock thought it showed "the loftiest sense of duty", and whatever else it was, it was a decision made by men who could count.

Hobson found the ground round the Victory Point cairn strewn with clothing and gear of every kind, thrown down "as if these men, aware that they were retreating for their lives, had there abandoned everything which they considered superfluous."

It did not work. In May 1859 Hobson and McClintock followed the coast south and found what was left: a skeleton face-down in the shingle near Cape Herschel, and at [[place:Erebus Bay]] [[artifact:franklin-1845-022|a 28-foot ship's boat mounted on a heavy oak sledge]], hauled up on the beach with portions of two skeletons in her, five watches, two loaded and cocked guns standing against her side, twenty-six pieces of silver plate carrying the crests of Franklin and nine other officers, an astonishing quantity of clothing and tools — and for food, a little tea and forty pounds of chocolate. Hobson found no record, journal or memorandum of any kind. The boat was pointing back north-east, towards the ships.

## What is disputed

Almost everything after 26 April 1848. Whether all 105 marched together or the party split. Whether some returned to the ships and re-manned them — Inuit testimony says at least one ship was sailed south afterwards, and the position of the *Erebus* wreck, far south of where she was abandoned, is hard to explain otherwise. Whether the boat at Erebus Bay was going back for supplies or was a party that had given up. Whether the survivors lasted months or years: Inuit accounts collected by Rae describe about forty men still walking in the spring of 1850, two years after the record was written.

The one thing not in dispute is that none of the 105 reached help. See [[page:franklin-1845-inuit-testimony]] for what the people who were there said, and [[page:franklin-1845-victory-point-record]] for the document itself.
