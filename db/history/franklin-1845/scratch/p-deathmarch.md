On 26 April 1848, 105 men started south from [[place:Victory Point]] to walk to the Back River. It is about 250 miles of coast and sea ice to the river mouth and then roughly a thousand miles up the river and across country to the nearest Hudson's Bay Company post. They were hauling boats on sledges. None of them made it.

What follows is not a narrative, because no narrative survives. It is a list of the places where things were found, in the order they lie along the coast.

## The evidence, going south

**Victory Point.** [[person:William Hobson]] found the cairn and [[artifact:franklin-1845-012|the record]] on 6 May 1859, and around it a scatter of gear thrown down "as if these men, aware that they were retreating for their lives, had there abandoned everything which they considered superfluous."

**[[place:Erebus Bay]], on the west coast.** [[artifact:franklin-1845-022|A 28-foot boat on a heavy oak sledge]], with two skeletons in her, five watches, twenty-six pieces of officers' silver, five or six books, and for provisions a little tea and forty pounds of chocolate. Two double-barrelled guns stood muzzle-up against her side, one barrel of each loaded and cocked. She was pointing north-east, back towards the ships. Modern excavation of the same site has produced the remains of at least thirteen men, many bearing cut marks, and in 2024 a jaw identified by DNA as [[person:James Fitzjames]], captain of the *Erebus*.

**Near Cape Herschel, on the south coast.** McClintock, coming the other way at the end of May 1859, found a young man lying face down in the shingle in the dress of a ship's steward, with a clothes brush and a horn pocket comb beside him.

**Todd Islands, off the south coast.** [[person:In-nook-poo-zhee-jook]] guided [[person:Charles Francis Hall]] there in May 1869 to the place where five men were said to be. Deep snow defeated the search; Hall found part of a fractured human thigh bone. A witness named Poo-yet-ta said the bodies had not been buried when he first saw them but were lying close together, each fully dressed and unmutilated, with a jack-knife in one man's pocket and meat tins beside them.

**Near the mouth of the Peffer River, on the mainland.** Hall dug through the snow and found one unburied skeleton. He raised an American flag half-mast over it, built a five-foot stone monument, and fired a salute. He took the remains home; a tooth filling later identified them as Lieutenant Le Vesconte of the *Erebus*.

**[[place:Starvation Cove]], on Adelaide Peninsula near Richardson Point.** The furthest point any of them reached. Inuit found a boat and skeletons here. An elder named Toolooah, whom [[person:Frederick Schwatka]]'s party met in 1879, had been with the party who found them and "confirmed the testimony previously obtained in every essential particular." When Schwatka's men searched the spot they found a skull, some pieces of blanket in the seaweed, and a small pewter medal struck for the launch of the steamer *Great Britain* in 1843. They built a monument over what remained. Five miles inland, Inuit found another skeleton — a sailor by his clothing — during the summer of Schwatka's search, because the party had promised rewards for anything new and kept their promises.

Starvation Cove is on the mainland. They got across. They did not get up the river.

## The records

Schwatka's party concluded that the expedition's papers had been carried to the boat place at Starvation Cove and destroyed there. Gilder put it plainly: the most important result of the search was "the establishing of the loss of the Franklin records at the boat place in Starvation Cove... Next in importance to their recovery must be considered the knowledge of their irrecoverable loss."

Inuit accounts describe children given papers to play with, and paper scattered by the wind. This is not vandalism and should not be read as such: a bundle of Admiralty paper found beside dead strangers is not obviously a document to anyone who has no reason to think so, and no one has ever suggested Inuit had reason to.

## Cannibalism

[[person:John Rae]] reported in 1854, from Inuit testimony, that "from the mutilated state of many of the corpses and the contents of the kettles, it is evident that our wretched countrymen had been driven to the last resource — cannibalism — as a means of prolonging existence." He was disbelieved and vilified for it. [[artifact:franklin-1845-013|The sentence is here.]]

Physical anthropology has confirmed it repeatedly since the 1990s: cut marks made by metal blades on limb bones from Erebus Bay and other sites, consistent with the removal of flesh, and later evidence of pot-polishing on bone ends, which indicates the marrow was being boiled out — that is, end-stage starvation, not a single desperate act. The 2024 identification of Fitzjames puts a name and a rank to it.

Reported factually, this is what it is: the last stage of a starvation from which nobody was coming to help. It says nothing about the character of the men involved, which is roughly what Rae said in 1854 and what his critics refused to hear.

## What is still unknown

Whether the party stayed together after Victory Point or split. Whether some turned back to the ships — the boat at Erebus Bay was pointing north-east, and the position of the [[page:franklin-1845-the-wrecks|wrecks]] suggests at least one ship was later remanned and sailed south. How long the last men survived: Rae's informants described about forty men still walking in the spring of 1850, two years after the record, and the accounts of [[person:Francis Crozier]] as Aglooka walking towards Back's River with one companion cannot be dated at all.

Some of this may yet be answered from the wrecks. Most of it will not.
