# Four years at Etah

[[place:Etah]] in 1913 was, as [[person:Donald Baxter MacMillan]] describes it, five black
holes in a hillside at the mouth of [[place:Foulke Fiord]], at 78 degrees 20 minutes
north — the most northerly inhabited place in the world, seven hundred miles from the
Pole. The expedition put its house up beside the centuries-old stone dwellings of the
Inughuit rather than on the open shore, on the reasoning that the people who had lived
there for generations knew where the wind did not reach.

[[place:Borup Lodge]] was thirty-five feet square, double-walled with a four-inch air
space, boarded with tongue-and-groove and covered with Cabot quilt and rubberoid roofing,
with eight rooms on the ground floor. Banked to the eaves with drifted snow it looked like
[[artifact:crocker-land-1913-027|this]]. [[person:Jerome Lee Allen]] had the engine
assembled, the switchboard and house wired and the lights burning within twenty-one days
of the floor timbers going down. The wireless set never made contact with anybody, in four
years.

## The people the expedition depended on

The Smith Sound tribe numbered somewhere over two hundred people. MacMillan took a census
of every one of them four times and compiled a dictionary of about three thousand words of
their language. The expedition employed and travelled with a large fraction of the adult
men, and the ten thousand miles of sledge work it claims were mostly driven by them.

with their families and their dogs on 16 August 1913; [[person:Arklio]] and his wife

[[person:Esayoo]] and [[person:Ak-pood-a-shah-o]] and [[person:Ah-now-ka]] and
[[person:Koo-la-ting-wa]]; [[person:Piugaattoq]] and [[person:Ittukusuk]];
[[person:Ak-kom-mo-ding-wa]] and [[person:Ah-took-sung-wa]], found living at the old

The relationship was not equal and MacMillan does not pretend it was, but it was close.
"Do as the Eskimos do; dress as the Eskimos dress," he wrote, was a good adage to follow.
The expedition wore skin clothing, hunted with the tribe, ran its house on walrus and
seal, and had its dogs and its sledges built by them. Its doctor, [[person:Harrison J.
Hunt]], was called *angakkoq*, the big-medicine man, and treated the tribe for four years.
The counterweight is [[topic:greenland-inughuit]] and
[[page:inughuit-and-the-explorers]], which hold the other side of it.

## The work

The scientific record of the expedition is larger than its geography. Meteorological
observations were taken daily without a break at Borup Lodge and at a sub-station on
Starr Island run by [[person:Fitzhugh Green]] and Allen, which showed differences of
sixteen degrees over half a mile as air came down off the ice-cap. Tidal observations were
run at Etah, and MacMillan had already made a fortnight of them at
[[place:Fort Conger]] in 1909. A seismograph lent by Georgetown University ran through a
whole year. Thermometer Hill, eleven hundred feet up, was visited every Sunday. Alida Lake
was sounded along its length: greatest depth eighty-two and a half feet.

[[person:W. Elmer Ekblaw]] collected the geology and the botany — his appendix on the
vegetation around Borup Lodge lists dandelion, poppy, blue-grass, mushrooms as wide as
dinner plates, ferns, buttercups, bluebells, rhododendron and heather growing a thousand
miles inside the Arctic circle, and he counted eighteen species of flower within a minute's
walk of the door. [[person:Maurice Cole Tanquary]] took the insects. MacMillan wrote the
ornithology himself: it runs to a numbered list of species with breeding records —
red-throated loon breeding at 82 degrees 30 north on the shores of Grant Land, Mandt's
guillemot from Cape York at 76 north to Cape Union at 82 degrees 18. In one season they
gathered [[artifact:crocker-land-1913-052|four thousand eider eggs]] from a single island;
they searched the hills north of Etah for years for the eggs of the knot and eventually got
them ([[artifact:crocker-land-1913-053]]). The waterfall above the settlement, which
MacMillan called [[artifact:crocker-land-1913-041|the Niagara of North Greenland]], and the
grave of August Sonntag at [[artifact:crocker-land-1913-042|Port Foulke]] were photographed
too.

MacMillan exposed, developed and filed five thousand photographic negatives in fifteen
hundred days; his own numbered lists of them survive ([[source:macmillan1913photolists]]),
and what became of them is in [[page:crocker-land-the-photographs-and-the-science]].

## Winters

The sun went for the last time on 24 October and came back after a hundred and twenty-six
days. What filled the dark was work, hunting, visitors, and a good deal of deliberate
manufacture of occasion: a vaudeville show for the Inughuit on 19 December 1913 with Jot
Small performing mock surgery; Christmas dinners with the tribe fed afterwards until they
shouted enough; a Victrola with Caruso and Melba on it; fruit cakes sent from Kingston,
New York by [[person:M. J. Look]]. The Christmas dinner of 1914 was photographed -
[[artifact:crocker-land-1913-024|Hunt, Green, Ekblaw, Tanquary, Allen, Mene and Small round
the table]], with MacMillan behind the camera. The fourth Christmas, in 1916, had only two of the original seven still at
Etah — MacMillan and [[person:Jonathan C. Small]] — with
[[person:Edmund Otis Hovey]] and [[person:George Comer]], who had come north to fetch them
and been caught themselves. Hovey put an English sovereign under each plate. That table was photographed too:
[[artifact:crocker-land-1913-055]].

Things also went wrong in the ordinary way. Tau-ching-wa was burned nearly to the bone in
a petrol explosion and travelled the next winter in a cloth mask. Tanquary lost two toes.
Ekblaw froze his feet in March 1914 and again in December 1916. Kud-la died of what
MacMillan calls pneumonia. A child of Kood-la-tin-a's, playing on a sledge, hooked her
sealskin hood over the upstander and strangled. Two men were carried off on a broken pan
of ice. In January 1917 Megishoo, aged nine, swallowed a galvanised iron ring an inch
across while playing at being an *angakkoq*.

## What news reached them

Almost none. They sailed in July 1913, six weeks before the war they knew nothing about.
The first mail from home came with [[person:Edmund Otis Hovey|Doctor Hovey]] on 15
September 1915, more than two years out, and with it the news of the great world war,
which MacMillan says seemed remote and unrelated to life in a canvas tent on a Greenland
fiord. A sledge in January 1917 brought word that Kitchener had drowned, that von Moltke
was dead, that a German submarine had been to Baltimore, that the United States had bought
the Danish West Indies and that Denmark had been granted control of Greenland. That the
United States had entered the war MacMillan learned over the rail of the *Neptune* on 1
August 1917 ([[artifact:crocker-land-1913-015]]).
