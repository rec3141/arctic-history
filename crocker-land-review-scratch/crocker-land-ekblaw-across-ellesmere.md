# Ekblaw across Ellesmere and Grant Land, 1915

The longest journey the Crocker Land Expedition made was not the one it was sent for. In
the autumn of 1914 [[person:Donald Baxter MacMillan]] gave [[person:W. Elmer Ekblaw]] and
[[person:Maurice Cole Tanquary]] the exploration of Ellesmere and Grant Lands: from
[[place:Etah]] to [[place:Cape Sabine]]; across Ellesmere from [[place:Beitstad Fiord]] to
[[place:Bay Fiord]]; down Bay Fiord and [[place:Eureka Sound]] to the mouth of
[[place:Greely Fiord]]; up Greely Fiord and every fiord that ran out of it; across Grant
Land by [[place:Lake Hazen]] to [[place:Lady Franklin Bay]]; and home along the Greenland
coast. About twelve hundred miles, Ekblaw reckoned, and three months if nothing went
wrong. The point of it was to join the survey Lockwood and Brainard had made from Lady
Franklin Bay for the Greely expedition in 1883 to the survey Fosheim and Raanes had made
from Jones Sound for Sverdrup in 1900-02, and so close the last gap in the outline of
Ellesmere. Tanquary in the end went out on the relief ship and Ekblaw made the journey
without him.

The whole route, with Ekblaw's dates, is [[artifact:crocker-land-1913-003]].

## The men

Ekblaw took [[person:Esayoo]], an older man who had travelled for Peary, and
[[person:E-took-a-shoo]], whom he calls one of the strongest men in the Smith Sound tribe
and the fastest snow-house builder in it, for the whole distance.
[[person:Oobloyah]] and Okpuddyshao went as far as the divide of Ellesmere;
[[person:Arklio]] and Nukapingwa as far as Bay Fiord. It was Esayoo who changed the plan.
MacMillan had laid down the Beitstad Fiord crossing, which the 1914 parties had used;
Esayoo said go by Flagler Fiord. Ekblaw took his advice, and on Easter Sunday, 5 April
1915, drove up a valley between precipitous mountains all day without his caribou-skin
kooletah, killed the party's first musk-ox in the afternoon, and named the gateway
[[place:Sverdrup Pass]] ([[artifact:crocker-land-1913-010]]). It is the standard crossing
of Ellesmere to this day.

## Musk-oxen

What follows in Ekblaw's report is, by modern standards, a slaughter, and it is also the
best census of the Ellesmere musk-ox that exists for the period. Eleven killed in Bay
Fiord on 9 April; fourteen from a single herd a few days later; eleven more at Camp Hunt;
twenty-one at the mouth of Greely Fiord on 19 April; from the top of a hill he called
Mount Hovey he counted more than two hundred animals at once. Six at Tanquary Fiord.
Eleven at Discovery Bay. MacMillan's photograph of a herd on the uplands is
[[artifact:crocker-land-1913-031]]. The party lived on them and so did the dogs, and without
them the journey could not have been made at all — there was no other way to move a sledge over
that distance.

Scientists working Quttinirpaaq today have this as a baseline. So is the negative
observation: at [[place:Lake Hazen]] at the end of May there were no live musk-oxen at
all, only old skulls and skeletons where Peary's hunters, Esayoo among them, had killed
them years before.

## New country

North and west of Greely Fiord Ekblaw was on ground nobody had mapped. He entered a bay
sixteen miles deep with two arms on its east side and named it [[place:Borup Fiord]], for
George Borup, the friend of the expedition who had drowned in 1912. The land beyond it he
called Louise Homer Land, for the contralto. A fiord running thirty miles into the heart
of Grant Land became [[place:Tanquary Fiord]]. A cape became Cape James for the president
of the University of Illinois, a mountain Mount Bayley for his own teacher, and a range
the Osborn Mountains for the president of the American Museum. He crossed
[[place:Canon Fiord]] in eight hours where Fosheim and Raanes had needed a week.

He also found people had been there long before him. At the head of Canon Fiord he built
his cairn among the ruins of stone houses. On the shore at the mouth of the valley leading
to Lake Hazen he found ruined dwellings and wrote that he was sure the old Inuit route
across Grant Land ran up that fiord and then up the valley to the lake. In Tanquary Fiord
there were caches and fox traps. These are among the first records of the Ellesmere
archaeological sites that Peter Schledermann and others have since worked, and Ekblaw's
inference about the route has stood up.

At the head of Greely Fiord on 16 May he came to a lake fifteen miles long and two wide
between vertical cliffs 1,500 feet high — "one of the dreariest, loneliest, coldest spots
on this old globe of ours." It was the same day of the year that Lockwood and Brainard had
turned homeward from it thirty-two years before. He was carrying Lockwood's sketches:

> The head of the fjord had not changed one jot, one tittle ... every dark cliff, every
> patch of snow, every gully in the slopes, appeared unchanged.
> ([[artifact:crocker-land-1913-012]])

He corrected Lockwood too. The map showed a lake at the foot of Mount Arthur; there was
only a great plain.

## Fort Conger

On 30 May he came down Chandler Fiord to [[place:Fort Conger]], where Adolphus Greely's
party had lived for two years before walking south to die at Cape Sabine. Only the kitchen
of the headquarters house was still standing.

> We camped there one night, boiling our meat and tea on the old Army Range No. 1 which
> still stands in good condition, though rusty ... We made excellent coffee from some we
> found in a sealed tin in the old kitchen; strange to say, it retained its aroma fresh
> and strong. ([[artifact:crocker-land-1913-011]])

Musk-ox spoor lay through the rooms. The memorial tablet to C. W. Paul and J. J. Hand of
HMS *Discovery*, who died of scurvy on Nares's north Greenland sledge journey in 1876,
stood clear and undamaged. In one of the shanties Peary's Inughuit had built out of Fort
Conger's own timbers, Ekblaw found the records MacMillan had left in 1909, when he spent a
fortnight there taking tidal observations, and added a note of his own. He had expected to
meet a party from Etah at Lake Hazen, and another at Fort Conger. Nobody came to either.

## Home

Getting back across Kennedy Channel was the worst of it: north of Hans Island a pressure
ridge forty feet high and, beyond it, the most chaotic ice he had ever seen — three miles
in seven hours of utmost exertion. Then, crossing Lafayette Bay on 5 June, a rifle shot,
and half a mile off the little brown tent they all knew: [[person:Fitzhugh Green]] with
Oobloyah and Arklio, out laying caches for them. They came home together down the Greenland
coast past the [[place:Rensselaer Harbour|Rensselaer Harbour]] where Kane's brig had lain,
and reached Etah on 9 June with the whole village out to meet them.

Ekblaw's own natural history is in the book as well as in his reports: the two plates of the
knot's nest and eggs are signed "Photo by Ekblaw" ([[artifact:crocker-land-1913-053]]), and
the botanical appendix ends with [[artifact:crocker-land-1913-064|Arctic poppy and arnica]].

Ekblaw ended his report by giving [[artifact:crocker-land-1913-013|the lion's share of the
credit]] to Esayoo — "a thorough gentleman, a boon companion of the trail" — and due
credit to E-took-a-shoo. It is a rare thing to find in a 1918 expedition narrative, and it
was correct.
