# The Crocker Land Expedition, 1913-1917

In June 1906 Robert Peary stood on the summit of Cape Colgate, at the north end of
[[place:Axel Heiberg Island]], and looked northwest. In *Nearest the Pole* he wrote that
"it was with a thrill that my glasses revealed the faint white summits of a distant land"
([[artifact:crocker-land-1913-009|his own words]]). He named it Crocker Land, for George
Crocker of the Peary Arctic Club, who had paid for the ship. On the maps of the following
decade it appeared as a brown patch in the polar sea, about 120 miles off the cape, and it
was the last large unexplored blank left in the northern hemisphere.

The claim was believed at once. In 1907 Edwin Swift Balch printed Peary's journal for 24 and
28 June 1906 in the *Bulletin of the American Geographical Society* and used it against
Fridtjof Nansen, who had just written that
[[artifact:crocker-land-1913-067|there is "no known land in the north whose northern coasts are not now explored"]].
[[artifact:crocker-land-1913-065|"American geographers, at least, know,"]] Balch answered, that
there is land west of Axel Heiberg Land - and he put it on the map at 82 degrees 30 minutes
to 83 degrees 20 minutes north, 103 to 105 degrees west ([[source:balch1907crocker]]). That
is the exact ground MacMillan walked over in April 1914. Nansen was right and Balch was
wrong, and it took four years and a life to prove it. The expedition's own two route maps
are [[artifact:crocker-land-1913-022|the circumpolar relief map]] and
[[artifact:crocker-land-1913-023|its field-of-work map]].

Seven years later the American Museum of Natural History, with the American Geographical
Society and the University of Illinois, sent seven men to find it. The leader was
[[person:Donald Baxter MacMillan]], who had been with Peary in 1908-09. With him went
[[person:Fitzhugh Green]], an ensign of the United States Navy, as physicist and
astronomer; [[person:W. Elmer Ekblaw]] as geologist and botanist;
[[person:Maurice Cole Tanquary]] as zoologist; [[person:Harrison J. Hunt]] as surgeon;
[[person:Jerome Lee Allen]], chief electrician of the Navy, to run a wireless set that
never reached anyone; and [[person:Jonathan C. Small]], known to everyone as Jot, to cook
and to build. They expected to be away eighteen months. They were away four years.

## What actually happened

The expedition never got where it meant to go. The steamer *Diana* left the Brooklyn Navy-yard
([[artifact:crocker-land-1913-017|photographed loading]]) and Boston on 5 July 1913, went on the
rocks at Barge Point, Labrador, a little after midnight on 16 July and was condemned; the sealer *Erik* took the
party on from Battle Harbour, and after six days of butting the ice of
[[place:Smith Sound]] failed to reach the intended winter quarters at
[[place:Flagler Bay]] on the Ellesmere shore. On 26 August 1913 the stores were landed at
[[artifact:crocker-land-1913-018|Provision Point]] in [[place:Foulke Fiord]] instead
(the whole outward passage is [[artifact:crocker-land-1913-078]]), and the house that became
[[place:Borup Lodge]] went up at [[place:Etah]] — named for George Borup, the young man
of Peary's last expedition who drowned in 1912 and who would have come.

From that house four winters of work went out in every direction. The central journey
began on 12 February 1914 and is told in [[page:crocker-land-after-the-mirage]]: nineteen
men, fifteen sledges and 165 dogs, cut back by influenza and mumps, then by frozen feet
and desertion, until four men — MacMillan, Green, [[person:Piugaattoq]] and
[[person:Ittukusuk]] — went out from [[place:Cape Thomas Hubbard]] onto the moving pack.
On 21 April Green ran back to the igloo shouting [[artifact:crocker-land-1913-005|"We
have it!"]]; Piugaattoq looked at it and said it was *poo-jok*, mist. Three days later,
at 82 degrees 30 minutes north and 108 degrees 22 minutes west — thirty miles inside the
brown patch — there was
[[artifact:crocker-land-1913-006|nothing whatever in sight]]. Crocker Land does not exist.
On 29 April they found Peary's cairn on the third summit of the cape and MacMillan
photographed [[artifact:crocker-land-1913-037|the record, the cairn and the piece of flag]]
inside it. On the way home Green shot Piugaattoq. That killing, and what it meant to the Inughuit,
belongs to [[topic:greenland-inughuit]] and to [[page:inughuit-and-the-explorers]]; it is
not retold here.

The rest of the four years produced the work that lasted. In 1915 Ekblaw crossed Ellesmere
Island, ran the length of [[place:Eureka Sound]], mapped [[place:Greely Fiord]] and its
tributaries, named [[place:Borup Fiord]] and [[place:Tanquary Fiord]], and reached
[[place:Fort Conger]] — see [[page:crocker-land-ekblaw-across-ellesmere]]. In 1916
MacMillan sledged twelve hundred miles west to [[place:King Christian Island]] and
[[place:Cornwall Island]] — see [[page:crocker-land-king-christian-island]]. Three relief
ships were sent and two of them failed; the story of the *George B. Cluett*, the *Danmark*
and the *Neptune* is in [[page:crocker-land-the-ships-that-could-not-come]]. What life at
Etah was actually like, and who lived it, is in [[page:crocker-land-four-years-at-etah]]; the
five thousand negatives and the science are in
[[page:crocker-land-the-photographs-and-the-science]].

The *Neptune*, under [[person:Robert Bartlett]], took the last men off on 2 August 1917
and reached Sydney, Cape Breton, on the 24th. MacMillan's first question over the rail was
about the war ([[artifact:crocker-land-1913-015]]).

## Why it matters here

The ship is working in the Queen Elizabeth Islands, and much of the coastline on the chart
under her keel was first drawn by this expedition. Ekblaw's 1915 traverse
([[artifact:crocker-land-1913-003]]) joined Lockwood and Brainard's survey from Lady
Franklin Bay to Fosheim's and Raanes's from Jones Sound and closed the last gap in the
outline of Ellesmere; MacMillan's 1916 journey ([[artifact:crocker-land-1913-004]]) put
[[place:King Christian Island]] on the map as a separate island and settled that the
"Finlay Land" Sherard Osborn reported in 1853 was the same thing seen from the other side.
Both parties recorded musk-ox herds in Bay Fiord and Greely Fiord in numbers no one has
seen since, and the ruins of Inuit houses, caches and fox traps at the head of Tanquary
Fiord and along Greely Fiord — evidence of the old routes across Grant Land that is now a
century-old baseline for archaeologists working the same ground.

The expedition's science was not incidental. Meteorological observations were taken daily
without a break for four years, at Borup Lodge and at a sub-station on Starr Island;
tidal observations were run at Etah and at Fort Conger; a seismograph lent by Georgetown
University ran through a whole year; Alida Lake was sounded; MacMillan took a census of
the whole Smith Sound tribe four times and compiled a dictionary of three thousand words
of Inuktun. He exposed and developed five thousand photographic negatives, of which sixty-six were
printed in his book and are registered here as image artifacts.

And it matters because of who did the work. Nineteen men left Etah in February 1914 and
twelve of them were Inughuit. The expedition covered, by MacMillan's own reckoning, ten
thousand miles of sledge travel; the caption he wrote under one of his own plates reads
"Four of Our Faithful Natives Who Deserve the Credit for Our Ten Thousand Miles of
Sledge-work." Ekblaw, at the end of the report of the longest journey any of them made,
gave [[artifact:crocker-land-1913-013|the lion's share of the credit]] to
[[person:Esayoo]], who had chosen the route.

## The record

The narrative used throughout these pages is [[source:macmillan1918four]], MacMillan's
*Four Years in the White North* (Harper, 1918), whose seven appendices carry the field
reports of the other members — five of the seven are Ekblaw's. The full text is
[[artifact:crocker-land-1913-001|registered here]]. It is a partial record: it is
MacMillan's book, published while the war was still on, and on the death of Piugaattoq it
says very little. Beside it stand the manuscript journals the American Museum has digitised
- [[source:green1913journal]], [[source:allen1913journal]], [[source:hovey1915journal]],
[[source:hunt1913fieldnotebook]] and [[source:macmillan1913photolists]] - and the printed
notices of the expedition as it went out and came back:
[[source:science1912crocker]], [[source:ags1913crocker]], [[source:science1913crocker]] and
[[source:science1917crocker]].
