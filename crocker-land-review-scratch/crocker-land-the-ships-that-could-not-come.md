# The ships that could not come

The Crocker Land Expedition was provisioned for eighteen months and stayed four years,
because the sea would not let anybody in to get it. Three ships were sent. The first two
failed and wintered where they stuck; the third got through.

## 1914: nothing

No ship was sent in 1914; the expedition expected none. It expected one in 1915.

## 1915: the George B. Cluett

The American Museum of Natural History chartered a three-masted auxiliary schooner, the
*George B. Cluett*, under [[person:Charles Pickles]], with
[[person:Edmund Otis Hovey]] aboard as the Museum's representative and the old whaling
master [[person:George Comer]] as ice pilot. MacMillan's verdict on her, written
afterwards, is blunt: absolutely unfitted for Arctic work, handicapped by a late start and
delays down the Greenland coast, she attempted the crossing of ice-choked
[[place:Melville Bay]] with a disabled engine and not a single man aboard who knew that
water or that coast.

Her track, as far as MacMillan records it, is [[artifact:crocker-land-1913-079]].
She got as far as Umanak — [[place:North Star Bay]] — and stopped there. Hovey came on the
last hundred miles to [[place:Etah]] in the Danish trading station's power boat, with
[[person:Peter Freuchen]] and his men, and arrived on 15 September 1915. The first mail
in two years came with him, and a shout across the water: "How is it for going home?"

MacMillan would not go. [[person:Harrison J. Hunt]] was still in the north and all the
collections and equipment were at Etah; he wanted another year of work; [[person:Jonathan
C. Small]] said at once that he would stay too. Within a few minutes the boat sailed south
again.

That decision cost the expedition its second winter's ship as well, because the *Cluett*
never got home either. She was caught and frozen in at [[place:Parker Snow Bay]], about
ninety miles by dog team south of North Star Bay, with a crew who had no warm clothing and
little food. On 5 November 1915 [[person:Fitzhugh Green]] and Freuchen brought the news to
MacMillan on the trail. MacMillan reached her himself at seven in the evening on 26
December, having spent Christmas Eve at Umanak with Freuchen for company.

She broke out in July 1916 and sailed for home on the 29th — without Hovey, Comer, Hunt or
[[person:W. Elmer Ekblaw]], all four of whom refused to embark. They were certain the
Museum would send another ship, and Captain Pickles would not take the *Cluett* any
further north. [[person:Maurice Cole Tanquary]], [[person:Jerome Lee Allen]] and Green had
already gone south by dog team and mail boat in February 1916.

## 1916: the Danmark

The Museum did send another ship. The *Danmark*, out of Copenhagen under Captain Hanson,
was chartered to come up from South Greenland, take the expedition off at Etah and carry
it and its collections to Sydney, Cape Breton. She reached North Star Bay on 23 September
1916 — a month too late for the season — could not get beyond it, and went into winter
quarters. She was well found, provisioned for thirty men until 1 November 1917, but short
of coal. Her track is [[artifact:crocker-land-1913-080]]; she never moved between its two ends.

The news of her did not reach Etah until 7 December, a month late again, and when it came
nobody at Borup Lodge wanted to move to her. The house was banked with snow blocks, warm,
and stocked with food and fuel for a year. Ekblaw and Hunt, aboard the *Danmark* and
convinced she would never reach Etah and could not sail home on canvas alone, chose to
sledge out instead: they left North Star Bay on 15 December 1916 and crossed Melville Bay
in the dark of the year, ten days of it, reaching Cape Seddon at midnight at the end of
the month. Ekblaw wintered at South Upernavik with the factor Hans Kintrup-Jensen and got
home by way of Godhavn in 1917. Hovey went the same way in March 1917, driven by
[[person:Koo-la-ting-wa]], and reached New York in late August.

## 1917: the Neptune

By the summer of 1917 only MacMillan, Small, Hovey and Comer were left in the north, and
by August only MacMillan and Small at Etah. Everything was packed — more than two hundred
boxes — and they were waiting for the *Danmark*, expected on 1 August.

On 31 July [[person:In-ah-loo]] came to where MacMillan was filming the waterfall and
asked whether anyone had seen the ship. He said not yet and went on with his work. Then
the shout went up from the shore: *Oo-me-ark-suah!* — big ship. It was not the *Danmark*.
It was the *Neptune*, and the voice off her bridge was one MacMillan had known since 1908:

> "Is that you, Bob?" I yelled. "Of course! Who in hell do you think it is?" was the
> characteristic reply. ([[artifact:crocker-land-1913-015]])

[[person:Robert Bartlett]], who had commanded Peary's *Roosevelt* and walked out of the
[[topic:karluk-cae|Karluk]] disaster three years before, had the relief ship. MacMillan's
first question was about the war; the answer was that it was still on and that America had
joined the Allies.

Getting out was not quick, and it took twelve days. At [[place:Clarence Head]] there was a
solid mass and not a lead in sight; off the Cary Islands the *Neptune* lay locked for three
days with two iron plates torn clean off her bows, every steam pump working and a plank well
built in the forehold to bail from. Working back east, MacMillan and Bartlett landed on
[[place:Hakluyt Island]] on about 10 August - the island William Baffin named three hundred
years before, and which was struck off the chart along with the whole of Baffin Bay until it
was found again - and walked over old stone houses, tupik rings, storehouses and fox traps.
They got south by Cape Parry to within twenty-five miles of [[place:Cape York]], where
Bartlett drove into the pack on the 12th, made little, and fell back into the lee of the
cape; sixty years before, almost to the day, the *Fox* had been locked in Melville Bay for
252 days and drifted 1,194 miles. At noon on 13 August she swung on her heel and by five
that afternoon was in open water going south. She called at Godhavn on the 17th and picked
up Ekblaw, sighted Labrador on the morning of the 22nd, and was at Sydney, Cape Breton, on
24 August 1917, four years and seven weeks after the *Diana* left the Brooklyn Navy-yard.
The homeward track is [[artifact:crocker-land-1913-081]]; MacMillan's two photographs of the
ship, in Baffin Bay and at anchor in Etah harbour, are [[artifact:crocker-land-1913-060]],
and [[artifact:crocker-land-1913-061|the leave-taking at Etah]] is the last plate but four in
his book. At Godhavn he photographed [[artifact:crocker-land-1913-063|the whalers' lookout
and the hulk of the *Fox*]].

## Why it happened

None of this was bad luck alone. The eastern entrance to Smith Sound is guarded by
Melville Bay, which is navigable only from about June to August and only when the wind has
set the ice right; the whalers had known that for a century, and both the *Cluett* and the
*Danmark* were sent to cross it too late in the season with the wrong ship and without
anybody who had done it before. The expedition's own hardest journeys were made in
February and March by sledge for the same reason: in this country the winter is the season
when you can travel and the summer is the season when you are stuck. It is the inverse of
how the *Amundsen* works, and it is worth holding in mind when reading any of these
narratives.
