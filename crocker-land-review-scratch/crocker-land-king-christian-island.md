# To King Christian Island, 1916

Of everything the Crocker Land Expedition did, the journey of spring 1916 is the one that
runs closest to the water the *Amundsen* works. It took [[person:Donald Baxter MacMillan]]
and six Inughuit twelve hundred miles from [[place:Etah]] to
[[place:King Christian Island]] and [[place:Cornwall Island]] and back in eight weeks. The
route with its dated positions is [[artifact:crocker-land-1913-004]]; the expedition's own
map of the whole field of work is [[artifact:crocker-land-1913-023]].

## The question

There were two reports of land out west and nobody knew whether they were the same land.
Sherard Osborn, searching for Franklin, wrote in his journal on 29 April 1853 that from
the northwestern extremity of Bathurst Island he distinctly saw land about thirty miles to
the northwest; it was put on the charts as Finlay Land. On 27 April 1901 Isachsen and
Hassel of the Sverdrup expedition, sledging the south shore of
[[place:Ellef Ringnes Island]], made out King Christian Island. Geographers had joined the
two into a single land of some three thousand square miles, its south end at 76 degrees 53
minutes north and its north end at 77 degrees 50. MacMillan's orders were to encircle it,
map it and explore it. Nobody had landed on it.

## Out

He left Etah on 22 March 1916 with six men and light loads, and got across
[[place:Smith Sound]] in a day and a half — thirty miles on the 24th, camping in a maze of
bear tracks. [[person:Ak-kom-mo-ding-wa]], the oldest man in the party, was nearly carried
away when the ice-foot dropped at Cairn Point. They killed twelve seals in the Weyprecht
Islands south of [[place:Bache Peninsula]] and were over the divide of
[[place:Sverdrup Pass|Flagler Pass]] in nine hours on 28 March, past the cache Frederick
Cook had left there in 1908, and down into [[place:Bay Fiord]] over rock, sand and deep
snow. In the fiord they came on [[person:W. Elmer Ekblaw]]'s camp of the year before.

Rabies was in the dogs — *piblock-to* — and six were lost on the outward journey: one
tossed by musk-oxen, two clawed by bears, three shot. From the camp of 7 April MacMillan
could see the loom of North Cornwall on the western horizon, thirteen degrees east of
magnetic north. At Cape Southwest on Axel Heiberg on 10 April he had covered 345 miles at
seventeen a day and found bituminous coal in every river bed.

Then the crossing of the Sverdrup Islands. On 13 April a run of fifty and eight-tenths
miles in fifteen hours forty minutes brought a new land in sight that turned out to be a
narrow island, Cape Ludvig. On the 14th, in Hendriksen Sound, they ran down a bear and two
cubs after five miles and took both cubs alive; [[person:Noo-ka-ping-wa]] held one over
his head out of reach of the dogs and it set its teeth in his wrist. They camped that
night on a small island that was on no map, practically midway between the magnetic and
the geographic poles, where MacMillan noted that the compass variation should
theoretically be the full 180 degrees — north should be south and east west. On the 17th
they were on the south shore of Ellef Ringnes, 468 miles from Etah. On the 18th, at five
in the afternoon, the haze lifted off the ice and King Christian Island stood on the
horizon.

They were ashore on the 19th after six and a quarter hours: a low coast running back to
peaks of about two thousand feet. Wind and drift kept them there through the 20th and
21st on thirty-nine pounds of pemmican for the dogs. On the 22nd MacMillan built a cairn,
took his sights, and turned for home in a snowstorm within half an hour.

## Back by Cornwall Island

On Easter Sunday, 23 April, he left a cairn and a record on Ellef Ringnes with a tally of
the journey: thirteen bears, thirteen seals, sixteen hare, two ptarmigan and thirty
musk-oxen. Vilhjalmur Stefansson's [[topic:karluk-cae|Canadian Arctic Expedition]] party,
coming the other way out of the west, later crossed that trail and found the record; a
letter from Stefansson at Bay of Mercy on Banks Island, eight hundred miles off, reached
Borup Lodge by dog team in January 1917.

On 26 April, at a quarter past four, MacMillan stepped ashore on North Cornwall — the
first outsider to land there in sixty-four years. Sir Edward Belcher, hunting for Franklin,
had touched at two points on the south shore for a few hours on 30 August 1852; the
northern and eastern coasts and most of the southern had never been seen. MacMillan named
a headland twelve hundred feet high McLeod Head, for Captain Angus McLeod, and put a cairn
five feet high on it. On the 29th, working along the south shore by the Table and Exmouth
Islands, he found a new island two miles long and half a mile wide lying between his
sledges and the coast, and three more off the west and northwest of the island, and a
large fiord in the southwest with a high island in the middle of it.

## Home across a sound that had gone out

On 2 May, back at Cape Southwest, white wolves had raided the old camp; seventeen stubs of
matches left in the snow told MacMillan in the Inughuit's own shorthand that
[[person:Arklio]] and [[person:E-took-a-shoo]] had killed seventeen hare. MacMillan's picture of a team on this kind of
going is [[artifact:crocker-land-1913-030]]. On the 8th the
hills of Bay Fiord were crawling with Arctic hare and the party rested and played their
first game of whist. On the 10th and 11th they were lost twice in fog on the Ellesmere
glacier and dared not move for fear of going over the edge.

On 14 May they pitched the tent on the ice in front of Greely's starvation camp of 1884 at
[[place:Cape Sabine]]. MacMillan wrote that he had often wished one Etah hunter could have
crossed the sound to that camp of dying men; he might have saved the whole party. Then,
turning east:

> Heading east, we heard the sound of waves beating against the edge of the ice. Stretching
> north as far as the eye could see was open water. Smith Sound had broken up!
> ([[artifact:crocker-land-1913-014]])

They doubled back north by Victoria Head to find the pack still bridging the sound, picked
up a forty-pound bear cub on the way and lashed it to a sledge, and drove into Etah just
before midnight on 16 May. The cub, named Bowdoin, was tethered to a bench in the workshop
and wrecked the room within three minutes.

## What it added to the chart

King Christian Island and "Finlay Land" were settled as one island — and a much smaller
one than the three thousand square miles the geographers had allowed it. Cornwall Island
got a coastline. Several small islands went onto the map for the first time. It was the
last of the great dog-sledge reconnaissances of the Queen Elizabeth Islands before aerial
survey, and the coastlines MacMillan drew stood on Canadian charts until the aerial
photography of the late 1940s and 1950s corrected them.
