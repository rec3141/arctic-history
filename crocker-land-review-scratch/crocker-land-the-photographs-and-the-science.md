# The photographs, and the science that came home

The Crocker Land Expedition failed at the thing it was named for and succeeded at almost
everything else. Its most useful product is not a discovery but a record: four years of
unbroken observation from one point on the Greenland coast, and about five thousand
photographic negatives, which [[person:Donald Baxter MacMillan]] exposed, developed and
filed in fifteen hundred days.

## Five thousand negatives

The negatives were made on glass and developed at [[place:Borup Lodge]] through four
winters, with meltwater and a lamp. MacMillan numbered and listed them as he went, and his
own lists survive at the American Museum of Natural History and have been digitised
([[source:macmillan1913photolists]]). Sixty-six of them, and three maps, were printed in
[[source:macmillan1918four|*Four Years in the White North*]] in 1918, and every one of those
plates is registered here as an image artifact with a position, from the full-resolution
Internet Archive scan of the book: forty-nine artifacts, because several plates carry two or
three pictures.

They are worth looking at as data and not only as illustration. Four sorts stand out.

**Ice.** [[artifact:crocker-land-1913-034|Rafted pressure ice on the polar pack]],
[[artifact:crocker-land-1913-035|the rough ice at the farthest north]] and
[[artifact:crocker-land-1913-036|the fourth camp]] were made between 81 and 82 and a half
degrees north in April 1914, out on the moving floe northwest of
[[place:Cape Thomas Hubbard]]. Nobody has photographed that water from its surface since. The
smooth floes MacMillan crossed on the way out, and the wall of chaotic pressure ice that
stopped him, are now understood as the multi-year ice and ice islands of the Canada Basin,
shed from the Ellesmere ice shelves; he had no name for them and inferred a landmass to the
west instead.

**Refraction.** The plate MacMillan called
[[artifact:crocker-land-1913-043|eight exposures at twenty-minute intervals of midnight sun in
Smith Sound]] is an experiment, not a picture: eight exposures on one plate between 11 p.m.
and 1.20 a.m. at 78 degrees 20 minutes north, made to show the dip of the sun's track and how
it increases as one goes south. It belongs beside the mirage of 21 April 1914
([[artifact:crocker-land-1913-005]]), because both are about the same thing - what the
atmosphere over an ice surface does to light travelling near the horizon. Crocker Land was an
optical phenomenon, and this expedition is the reason that is known.

**Glaciers and coasts.** [[artifact:crocker-land-1913-021|Brother John's Glacier and Alida
Lake]] above [[place:Etah]], the [[artifact:crocker-land-1913-057|glacier a few miles north of
Cape York]] and [[artifact:crocker-land-1913-029|the face of the Beitstad Glacier]] are dated
photographs of ice fronts whose positions have been remeasured many times since. A glacier
photograph from 1914 with a known viewpoint is a data point.

**Sites.** [[artifact:crocker-land-1913-058|The remains of Greely's starvation hut at Camp
Sabine and Peary's hut of 1900-02]], [[artifact:crocker-land-1913-045|the Kane record and the
arrow carved on Fern Rock at Rensselaer Harbour]], and
[[artifact:crocker-land-1913-042|August Sonntag's grave and the winter quarters at Port
Foulke]] photograph nineteenth-century sites at a distance of thirty to sixty years, before
most of them were disturbed. So does the plate of
[[artifact:crocker-land-1913-063|the hulk of the *Fox* at Godhavn]], McClintock's Franklin
search ship in her last years as a coal hulk.

## The science

The geography is in [[page:crocker-land-ekblaw-across-ellesmere]] and
[[page:crocker-land-king-christian-island]]. The rest ran on quietly at Etah.

Meteorological observations were taken daily, without a break, for four years at Borup Lodge
and at a sub-station on Starr Island that [[person:Fitzhugh Green]] and
[[person:Jerome Lee Allen]] worked; they recorded differences of sixteen degrees over half a
mile as air came down off the ice-cap. Tidal observations were run at Etah, and MacMillan had
run a fortnight of them at [[place:Fort Conger]] in 1909. A seismograph lent by Georgetown
University ran a whole year. Thermometer Hill, eleven hundred feet up, was climbed every
Sunday. Alida Lake was sounded end to end: greatest depth eighty-two and a half feet. Green
did the magnetic and astronomical work, and it was his sextant sights that fixed the two
positions on the polar pack that settled the Crocker Land question.

[[person:W. Elmer Ekblaw]] collected the geology, the botany and the birds, and his work is
the part that has aged best. His musk-ox counts on Ellesmere in 1915 - eleven here, fourteen
there, more than two hundred animals in sight at once from Mount Hovey - are still the
earliest usable census for that country, and so is his negative observation that there were
no live musk-oxen left at [[place:Lake Hazen]], only skulls where Peary's hunters had been.
He identified the ruins of stone houses, caches and fox traps along Greely Fiord and at the
head of [[place:Tanquary Fiord]], and inferred from them the old Inuit route across Grant
Land; archaeologists working the same ground have since confirmed it.
[[person:Maurice Cole Tanquary]] took the insects. MacMillan wrote the ornithology himself,
with breeding records as far north as 82 degrees 30 minutes.

And he did four censuses of the whole Smith Sound tribe and compiled a dictionary of about
three thousand words of Inuktun - which is a record of a community, made by an outsider, in
the years just before the Danish administration reached it. What that community made of the
expedition is not in MacMillan's book; it is in [[topic:greenland-inughuit]] and
[[page:inughuit-and-the-explorers]].

## What is not here

The five thousand negatives are not all in the book, and most of them have never been
published. MacMillan's lists name them; the plates themselves are at the American Museum of
Natural History with the rest of the Crocker Land Expedition Collection, along with the
manuscript journals of [[source:green1913journal|Green]],
[[source:allen1913journal|Allen]], [[source:hovey1915journal|Hovey]] and
[[source:hunt1913fieldnotebook|Hunt]]. Hovey's is handwritten and its machine transcription
is unusable; a future run that wants the *George B. Cluett*'s outward passage in detail will
have to read the manuscript pages themselves.
