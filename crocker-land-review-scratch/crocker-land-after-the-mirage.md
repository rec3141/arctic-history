# The journey after Crocker Land, February to May 1914

The plan was simple and it was wrong. Cross [[place:Smith Sound]] in February while the
ice was hard, run west across Ellesmere Island to [[place:Eureka Sound]], go north to
[[place:Cape Thomas Hubbard]] at the top of [[place:Axel Heiberg Island]], and from there
walk out 120 miles over the polar pack to the land Peary had seen in 1906. The point they
were aiming at lay 483 statute miles from [[place:Etah]] in a straight line.

## Nineteen men, fifteen sledges, 165 dogs

The divisions went out one a day: [[person:Fitzhugh Green]] with
[[person:Ah-pellah]] and seven sledges at 500 pounds each on 7 February;
[[person:Maurice Cole Tanquary]] on the 8th; [[person:W. Elmer Ekblaw]] on the 9th;
[[person:Harrison J. Hunt]] on the 10th, with [[person:Tau-ching-wa]], who had been badly
burned in a petrol explosion at Etah and travelled in a cloth mask. MacMillan left last,
on 12 February, with [[person:Piugaattoq]] — he spells it Pee-a-wah-to — and
[[person:Minik Wallace]], the man the Museum had taken to New York as a child in 1897 and
who was now back among his own people. At [[place:Nerky]] the thermometer stood at
forty-eight below.

The whole thing came apart in [[place:Bache Peninsula|Hayes Sound]] in early March, when
influenza and mumps went through the divisions. Doctor Hunt slept three nights beside one
sick man before he could leave him; the party retreated ninety miles to Etah in two
marches. On 10 March four loaded teams started again.

On 20 March they were on the summit of the Beitstad Glacier at 4,750 feet, at fifty below;
MacMillan photographed the sledges going up its face and wrote under the picture that
[[artifact:crocker-land-1913-029|"we perspired freely at -50 degrees Fahrenheit"]].
Ekblaw's feet froze there — the ball of one blistered, the big toe swollen and waxy — and
he went back with [[person:Kai-o-ta]]. At the fiord below, Minik decided the work did not
suit him and turned for home, and Tau-ching-wa went with him without giving a reason.
Down [[place:Bay Fiord]] and up Eureka Sound the going was the best of the trip: on 28
March they made twenty-five miles and shot eleven hare; on the 29th they crossed 80
degrees north, a whole degree in two days. On 11 April they reached Cape Thomas Hubbard
and a wind-storm drove them into a dugout under the cliff. The full route, with every
dated position MacMillan gives, is [[artifact:crocker-land-1913-002]].

## Onto the pack

On 14 April [[person:Arklio]] and [[person:Noo-ka-ping-wa]] were sent home and four men
went out from the land: MacMillan, Green, Piugaattoq and [[person:Ittukusuk]] (MacMillan's
E-took-a-shoo). The first camp was fourteen miles out. The Inughuit did not like it: too
much water, the sun too high, it will not freeze, the ice is moving. On the 16th they
crossed a lead a hundred yards wide and tried to sound; the wire ran out to nearly two
thousand fathoms without finding bottom and then parted, taking the pick with it. By the
19th they were fifty-two miles offshore by dead reckoning and past the pressure area. The
three plates he made out there - [[artifact:crocker-land-1913-034|ice held up by pressure only]],
[[artifact:crocker-land-1913-036|the fourth camp]] and
[[artifact:crocker-land-1913-035|the rough ice and the last camp]] - are the only photographs
anyone has of that piece of sea.

Then, on 21 April:

> Green was no sooner out of the igloo than he came running back, calling in through the
> door, "We have it!" ... Great heavens! what a land! Hills, valleys, snow-capped peaks
> extending through at least one hundred and twenty degrees of the horizon.
> ([[artifact:crocker-land-1913-005]])

MacMillan turned to Piugaattoq and asked which point they should steer for. Piugaattoq
looked at it for a few minutes and said he thought it was *poo-jok* — mist. Ittukusuk said
"perhaps it is." Green was still sure it was land. As they went on, the country changed
shape with the swing of the sun, and at nightfall it was not there at all. That evening,
over tea and pemmican, MacMillan asked himself the question the whole expedition turned
on: could Peary have been mistaken? Piugaattoq's own words are held under
[[topic:greenland-inughuit]] as [[artifact:greenland-inughuit-028]].

On the morning of the 22nd, at thirty-one below in air clear as crystal, Green got a
latitude of 81 degrees 52 minutes and a longitude of 103 degrees 32 minutes, agreeing
almost exactly with the dead reckoning. They steered more northerly on the 23rd and 24th.
The fix on the 24th put them at 82 degrees 30 minutes north, 108 degrees 22 minutes west:

> We had not only reached the brown spot on the map, but we were thirty miles inland! You
> can imagine how earnestly we scanned every foot of that horizon - not a thing in sight,
> not even our almost constant traveling companion, the mirage.
> ([[artifact:crocker-land-1913-006]])

They went six miles further on the 25th. The long level floes ended in a sharp line
running east and west, and beyond it lay the roughest ice of the journey. Two days' food
remained for going on. They turned back.

MacMillan drew a conclusion from that line of rough ice which is worth recording because
it was reasonable and wrong: the smooth ice behind them, he argued, must have been
sheltered by some great body of land to the *west*, and he put its eastern edge at 120
degrees west and its northern edge at 82 north. There is no land there either. What his
smooth ice was is now understood as the old multi-year floes and ice islands of the
Canada Basin, shed from the Ellesmere ice shelves — a phenomenon nobody had a name for
in 1914.

## Peary's cairn

On 29 April, back within a mile of land, they saw a cairn on a low point. They had already
walked thirty miles that day. There are three summits to Cape Thomas Hubbard; they climbed
the first and found nothing, and then the next, breaking through a heavy crust at every
step. On the last was Peary's cairn, with a stick out of the top and, at its base, a
cocoa-tin holding a piece of the American flag and a record of nine words:
[[artifact:crocker-land-1913-007|"Peary, June 28, 1906."]] They put back a small silk
flag, a record of their own, and a copy of Peary's, and photographed all three things:
[[artifact:crocker-land-1913-037]]. The cape itself stands at 81 degrees 22 minutes north,
94 degrees 12 minutes west; the farthest point of 24 April lay 150 miles northwest of it.

Then they looked northwest from the exact spot Peary had looked from, on a day without a
cloud or a trace of mist.

> Yes, there it was! It could even be seen without a glass ... the whole resembling hills,
> valleys, and snow-capped peaks to such a degree that, had we not been out on the frozen
> sea for 150 miles, we would have staked our lives upon its reality. Our judgment then,
> as now, is that this was a mirage or loom of the sea ice.
> ([[artifact:crocker-land-1913-008]])

That is as clean an answer to a geographical question as the Arctic has produced. It cost
the life of a man.

## The return

Three days' food were left. MacMillan sent Green and Piugaattoq south to survey
twenty-five miles of unmapped Axel Heiberg coast - the men who made this journey possible
are in MacMillan's own group plate, [[artifact:crocker-land-1913-032]] - and went north himself with Ittukusuk to
get Sverdrup's farthest-north record from Cape Colgate. Green came back alone on 5 May,
six days later, having been given food for three. What he said, and what he had done, and
what the Inughuit made of it, is held under [[topic:greenland-inughuit]] and in
[[page:inughuit-and-the-explorers]]. No investigation was ever held.

The homeward road took a fortnight and a half. On 12 May they reached the head of Bay
Fiord after twelve and three-quarter hours on snowshoes — Green had lost his at Cape
Thomas Hubbard and came in an hour behind. On the 15th they were over the Beitstad Glacier
again, where Green overturned and smashed his sledge, with a white wolf following all day
and sitting on its haunches by the camp at night. On the 17th, at the entrance to Hayes
Sound, they found three sticks set in a line pointing inland to a cache of pemmican. On
the 20th, at Payer Harbour, there was a box of tinned peaches, pears and marmalade left by
Doctor Hunt, and then two galloping teams — [[person:Ak-pood-a-shah-o]] and
[[person:Oo-bloo-ya]], come across the sound with fresh dogs reeking of seal meat. They
were at Etah that night.

Near Cape Hatherton one of the dogs, a good one called Noo-ka-ping-wa after the man, had
covered his fourteen hundred miles, staggered from side to side and dropped.
MacMillan wrote that he seemed to say, well, I think you can make it without my help.
