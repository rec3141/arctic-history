The sledging season of 1876 was the point of the whole expedition, and it broke
it. Three main parties went out from the ships in April: [[person:Albert
Hastings Markham]] north over the ice, [[person:Pelham Aldrich]] west along the
Ellesmere coast, and [[person:Lewis Anthony Beaumont]] east along the north
coast of Greenland. All three came back with men dying.

## Markham's northern party

Fifteen men left the Alert at [[place:Floeberg Beach]] on 3 April 1876 with two
20-foot boats and three sledges — Marco Polo, Victoria and Hercules — and 71
days' provisions ([[artifact:nares-greely-022]]). Because no depots could be
laid on the moving pack, they had to carry everything from the start: about 240
pounds a man, which Markham himself later wrote should never be exceeded. The
daily ration was pemmican, biscuit, bacon, tea and rum. There was no lime
juice, and no fresh food.

The load meant double and often triple hauling: advance two sledges, walk back
empty, bring up the third. The ice was not a plain but a field of rubble and
old pressure ridges, and they made about two miles of northing on a good day.
By early May men were stiff and short of breath and their gums were bleeding.

At noon on 12 May they set up the artificial horizon on the floe, took a good
altitude, and got 83 deg 20 min 26 sec N — [[artifact:nares-greely-026|
exactly 399 and a half miles from the North Pole]]. It was the farthest north
then reached by anyone. They sang the Union Jack of Old England and the Grand
Palaeocrystic Sledging Chorus, drank a magnum of whisky sent by the Dean of
Dundee for the purpose, and turned round.

## The retreat

The return was the disaster. By 13 May invalids were being carried on the
sledges. Men who could still pull dragged the sick forward in short stages,
came back for the next load, and did the same ground three times over. On 7
June, with eleven of the fifteen unable to walk, [[person:Alfred Arthur Chase
Parr]] set out alone across some thirty miles of broken floe to fetch help from
the ship, and reached it the same day. He was too late for George Porter, who
died at ten minutes past noon on 8 June and was buried on the ice under a Union
Jack.

Beaumont's Greenland party fared no better. James Hand died on 3 June and
Charles Paul on 29 June, both carried on sledges to within sight of safety.
[[person:Hans Hendrik]]'s dog team and the fresh meat he brought in are given
credit in Nares's narrative for saving the rest. Aboard the Alert,
[[person:Niels Christian Petersen]] had both feet amputated after a March
journey and died on 14 May.

Four men dead, and by midsummer more than half the two ships' companies had
shown scorbutic symptoms. Nares broke off the expedition, got the Alert out of
the ice on 31 July 1876, and was home at [[place:Portsmouth]] on 2 November,
a year early.

## The argument about lime juice

A committee of three admirals and two doctors sat in London and reported on
7 May 1877 that it attributed "the early outbreak of scurvy in the spring
sledging parties of the Expedition to the absence of lime-juice from the sledge
dietaries."

Nares printed the sentence in his own narrative and then argued with it for
several pages. His defence was that no previous sledge party had carried lime
juice in April — it froze rock-hard and could not be issued — and that Rae,
M'Clintock, Richards, Parry, Franklin, McClure and a long list of others had
made far longer journeys without it and come back healthy. He sent lime juice
to the outer depots for use on the way home, in May and June, "which was as
early a date as any sledge party travelling in the Arctic regions had ever
consumed such a ration."

He was right about the precedent and wrong about the cause, and he half knew
it. His own conclusion is the honest part: that the successful sledge journeys
of the Franklin search "had induced a dangerous confidence in arctic men, and
that former travellers, without being aware of it, were in reality on the verge
of the same dire disease which attacked ourselves." What nobody in the room
knew was that the Navy's lime juice had itself become nearly useless. The
service had switched from Mediterranean lemons to West Indian limes, which
carry far less vitamin C, and the juice was pumped through copper piping and
stored, which destroyed most of what remained. The men were not being protected
by the ration they were arguing about.

The judgement that stuck in public was Nares's telegram from Valentia: the
polar sea was not navigable, and the route by Smith Sound was impracticable.
British Arctic effort essentially stopped for twenty years. The next people to
winter on that coast were Americans, and they went for a different reason —
see [[page:fort-conger-and-the-first-polar-year]].
