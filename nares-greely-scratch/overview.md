Between 1875 and 1884 two expeditions went as far north as ships and men could
be pushed through [[place:Nares Strait]], the long channel between Ellesmere
Island and Greenland. Both reached a farthest north. Both ended in scurvy,
starvation and dead men, and the second ended in a court of public opinion
that has never quite closed.

The two are one story because they used the same door. Smith Sound and the
channels above it — Kane Basin, [[place:Kennedy Channel]], Hall Basin,
[[place:Robeson Channel]] — form the only sea route that leads from Baffin Bay
towards the Arctic Ocean. [[person:Elisha Kent Kane]], [[person:Isaac Israel
Hayes]] and [[person:Charles Francis Hall]] had each tried it (see
[[topic:kane-hall-polaris]]); the British and then the Americans went through
behind them, wintered higher than anyone had, and found that the sea beyond was
not an open polar basin but a floor of very old, very thick ice.

## The British Arctic Expedition, 1875-1876

[[person:George Strong Nares]] took HMS Alert and HMS Discovery out of
[[place:Portsmouth]] on 29 May 1875 ([[artifact:nares-greely-001]]) with the
Admiralty's instructions to reach the North Pole. The Discovery wintered at
[[place:Discovery Harbour]] in [[place:Lady Franklin Bay]]. On 1 September 1875
a gale blew the pack off the Ellesmere shore and the Alert ran ten knots north
past [[place:Cape Sheridan]] into 82 deg 27 min N, higher than any ship had
ever wintered. The beach she lay against for the next eleven months Nares
named [[place:Floeberg Beach]] ([[artifact:nares-greely-002]]), for the
grounded masses of ancient ice that sheltered her.

In the spring [[person:Albert Hastings Markham]] and [[person:Alfred Arthur
Chase Parr]] dragged two boats and two sledges north over that ice and, on 12
May 1876, stood at 83 deg 20 min 26 sec N: the farthest north then reached
([[artifact:nares-greely-023]]). It cost them everything. Scurvy was already in
the party; by early June eleven of fifteen men could not walk, Parr had to make
a solo march of some thirty miles to the ship for help, and George Porter died
on the floe. Two more men died in [[person:Lewis Anthony Beaumont]]'s party on
the north Greenland coast, and the interpreter [[person:Niels Christian
Petersen]] died aboard the ship. Nares broke off the expedition a year early
and reported that the route by Smith Sound was impracticable. See
[[page:nares-1875-the-alert-at-floeberg-beach]] and
[[page:nares-1876-farthest-north-and-the-scurvy]].

## The Lady Franklin Bay Expedition, 1881-1884

Five years later the United States sent twenty-five men to the same coast, but
for a different reason. The first International Polar Year of 1882-83 was an
agreement among twelve nations to stop racing for the Pole and instead keep a
ring of stations making the same observations at the same hours. The American
station was [[place:Fort Conger]], built on the shore of Discovery Harbour two
hundred yards from where the Discovery had wintered
([[artifact:nares-greely-010]], [[artifact:nares-greely-011]]). For two years
[[person:Adolphus Washington Greely]]'s party kept hourly meteorological,
magnetic and tidal records at the northernmost inhabited place on earth. See
[[page:fort-conger-and-the-first-polar-year]].

They also went sledging. On 13 May 1882 [[person:James Booth Lockwood]],
[[person:David Legge Brainard]] and the Greenlandic driver [[person:Thorlip
Frederik Christiansen]] reached 83 deg 24 min N off the north coast of
Greenland — four miles past Markham, and the first time in three centuries the
farthest north was not held by Britain.

Then the plan failed. A relief ship was to come in 1882; the Neptune was
stopped by ice. Another was to come in 1883; the [[artifact:nares-greely-017|
Proteus]] was crushed in Kane Basin on 23 July 1883 and sank. Under his orders
Greely abandoned Fort Conger on 9 August 1883 and took his twenty-five men
south, four hundred miles by boat and then a thirty-day drift on the floes.
They reached the shore near [[place:Cape Sabine]] on 29 September 1883 and
built [[place:Camp Clay]] — a stone wall roofed with an upturned whaleboat —
beside a cache that turned out to hold a fraction of what the record said.
See [[page:the-retreat-from-fort-conger]].

Nineteen of the twenty-five died there between January and June 1884, of
scurvy and starvation. Among them were both Greenlandic hunters:
[[person:Thorlip Frederik Christiansen]] on 5 April and [[person:Jens Edward]],
drowned in his kayak in a lead in [[place:Buchanan Bay]] on 29 April, hunting
for the party. On 6 June Greely ordered [[person:Charles B. Henry]] shot for
repeatedly stealing food. Near midnight on 22 June [[person:Winfield Scott
Schley]]'s Thetis and Bear found seven men alive; one died on the way home.
See [[page:the-winter-at-camp-clay]] and [[page:the-shooting-of-private-henry]].

## Why it still matters here

Three things carry forward from these four years to the ship this history is
written for.

The first is the ice. What defeated Nares and killed Greely's men is the
multi-year ice that drains out of the Arctic Ocean through Nares Strait — the
oldest and thickest ice in the northern hemisphere. The ice arches that form
across the strait each winter, and the years they fail to form, are still an
active question for the Amundsen and every other ship that works these waters.
Nares's word for the ice, palaeocrystic, was a nineteenth-century guess at the
same fact.

The second is the science. Fort Conger's two years of hourly observations were
the first long instrumental record from above 81 degrees north, and the
International Polar Year that produced them is the direct ancestor of the
Polar Years of 1932-33 and 1957-58 and of the coordinated Arctic observing
that goes on today. [[place:CFS Alert]] stands a few kilometres from Floeberg
Beach and keeps one of the longest atmospheric baseline records in the Arctic
([[artifact:nares-greely-020]]). See [[topic:science-stations]].

The third is that both expeditions depended on people from Greenland and would
have got nowhere without them. [[person:Hans Hendrik]] of Fiskenaesset was on
his fourth expedition when he sailed in the Discovery, and it was his dog team
that brought Beaumont's scurvy-ridden party in off the Greenland ice
([[artifact:nares-greely-007]]). Jens Edward and Frederik Christiansen were
hired at Proven (Kangersuatsiaq) in July 1881, drove the sledges that made the
farthest north, hunted for the party at Camp Clay, and died with it. The
Inughuit connection continued at Fort Conger after Greely left it: Robert Peary
dismantled the house in 1900 and built three small huts in the Inughuit manner,
in which whole Greenlandic families lived and worked while he made his attempts
on the Pole. See [[page:fort-conger-after-greely]] and
[[topic:sverdrup-bernier-peary]].
