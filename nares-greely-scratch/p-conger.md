The Lady Franklin Bay Expedition is remembered for how it ended. It was
organised for something else entirely: to keep a set of instruments running at
a fixed point for two years.

## Weyprecht's idea

In 1875 an Austro-Hungarian naval lieutenant, Carl Weyprecht, came back from
Franz Josef Land arguing that the Arctic was being wasted on geography. Nations
were spending fortunes to plant flags a few miles further north than the last
man, and learning almost nothing. What was needed instead was a ring of
stations round the pole, all making the same observations, at the same hours,
by the same instruments, for a full year.

He put the case to the Academy of Sciences in Vienna, then to the International
Meteorological Conference in Rome in 1879, and international conferences at
Hamburg, Berne and St. Petersburg worked out the programme. Eleven countries
established fourteen stations for the first International Polar Year of
1882-83: twelve in the Arctic and two in the far south. Three were on what is
now Canadian territory — Fort Rae on Great Slave Lake (British), Kingua Fiord
on Baffin Island (German), and [[place:Fort Conger]] in
[[place:Lady Franklin Bay]] (American).

Every station recorded temperature, pressure, humidity, wind, cloud and
precipitation; magnetic declination, inclination and intensity; the aurora; and
astronomical positions. On the first and fifteenth of each month came a "term
day": readings every fifteen minutes for twenty-four hours, and for one hour of
that, readings every two minutes — a scheme Gauss had devised, with everything
timed to Gottingen mean time so that the whole ring of stations could be laid
side by side afterwards. See [[source:taylor1981first]] and
[[topic:science-stations]].

## Building the station

[[person:Adolphus Washington Greely]]'s party landed on the shore of
[[place:Discovery Harbour]] on 11 August 1881 ([[artifact:nares-greely-024]]),
two hundred yards from where HMS Discovery had wintered six years before —
Greely chose the site partly so that his records could be compared with
[[person:Henry Frederick Stephenson]]'s. He found Stephenson's cairn with a
record dated 11 August 1876, five years to the day.

The house they built was double-walled, tarred and black-roofed so that the
spring sun would warm it, banked with ice and snow in winter
([[artifact:nares-greely-010]], [[artifact:nares-greely-011]]). Inside were
three rooms: 17 by 15 feet for the four officers, a cook's corner and an entry,
and a long room of two-tiered bunks for everyone else. The plan Greely printed
in his book shows bunk 6 assigned to [[person:Thorlip Frederik Christiansen]]
and [[person:Jens Edward]]. A magnetic observatory stood two hundred yards to
the north-east, pegged together with wooden dowels rather than nails so that no
iron would disturb the readings, roofed with earth and packed with snow, and so
cold that a fireplace had to be added in the second year for the men who sat in
it all through a term day. Thermometers hung in a louvred shed forty yards
off; pendulum piers were set in the floor of the house. The station was named
for Senator Omar D. Conger of Michigan, who had pushed the appropriation
through.

[[person:Edward Israel]], a recent graduate of the University of Michigan at Ann Arbor, took the
magnetic and astronomical work. Sergeant Winfield Jewell ran the meteorology.
[[person:George W. Rice]] photographed ([[artifact:nares-greely-009]],
[[artifact:nares-greely-013]]). They added tides and sea temperatures to the
required programme. The result, published in 1888 as
[[source:greely1888report|the official report of the expedition]], is the first
long instrumental record from above 81 degrees north.

## And they went sledging anyway

Greely was as keen on the farthest north as anyone. In the spring of 1882
[[person:James Booth Lockwood]], [[person:David Legge Brainard]] and
Christiansen crossed Hall Basin to the Greenland shore and worked east along
the coast with a dog team, the supporting sledges dropping away one by one
([[artifact:nares-greely-023]]). On 13 May they camped at the north end of
[[place:Lockwood Island]], which observation put at 83 deg 23.8 min N.
Brainard's field notes:
[[artifact:nares-greely-028|We have reached a higher latitude than ever before
reached by mortal man]].

It beat [[person:Albert Hastings Markham]] by four miles and took the record
from Britain for the first time since Hudson in 1607. It stood until Nansen
passed it in 1895. Greely's own summary of the achievement names Lockwood and
Brainard; the plate in his book naming all three men — "Lieut. Lockwood,
Brainard, and Christiansen returning from 83 deg 24 min N" — is a fair
reminder that the Greenlandic driver who got them there was routinely left out
of the sentence ([[artifact:nares-greely-012]]).

## What the station was worth

Two years of hourly observations came home in the pockets and sledge boxes of a
party that had eaten its own boot leather. The magnetic and meteorological
series from Fort Conger were used for decades; the tidal record was the first
from that latitude; the natural history collections were left behind at the
station and lost.

The Polar Year itself worked. Of the fourteen stations, only the Dutch failed
to establish, their ship beset in the Kara Sea. The idea — simultaneous,
standardised, shared observation rather than competitive exploration — ran on
into the second International Polar Year of 1932-33, the International
Geophysical Year of 1957-58, and the fourth Polar Year of 2007-08, in which
Canadian ships including the Amundsen took part. The line from Weyprecht's
argument to a modern Arctic observing programme is direct.
