Twenty-five men moved into [[place:Camp Clay]] on [[place:Bedford Pim Island]]
in the third week of October 1883. The hut was a waist-high stone wall banked
with snow, roofed with their whaleboat turned over on sawn-off oars, with a
canvas passage and a commissary at the end. The only clear floor was a strip
down the middle where the cooking was done and where, on a bad day, a man could
take his exercise. Nineteen of the twenty-five died there or within reach of
it.

## The arithmetic

Greely had rations for about forty days at full issue and eight months of
winter. He cut the ration and cut it again — to seven ounces of bread and four
of meat, then to less — and set the party to getting food from the country. The
totals he eventually recorded for the winter give the shape of it: 150 pounds
of seal meat, 325 pounds of bear, about 90 pounds of fox, about 75 pounds of
dovekies and ptarmigan, 1,155 pounds of shrimps, 83 pounds of seaweed, and
"considerable quantities" of reindeer moss, saxifrage and lichens — for
twenty-five men over eight months.

The shrimps mattered most and were worth least. Brainard worked a net in the
tide cracks for hours a day, in the dark, for a few pounds at a time of tiny
crustaceans that had to be baited with sealskin the party could otherwise have
eaten. Toward the end they were boiling the oil-tanned cover of Greely's
sleeping bag, and their own boots, and scraping the lichen called tripe de
roche off the rocks.

Two of Garlington's promised items — a barrel of beef at Cape Sabine and the
bacon said to be in the wreck cache — never existed as far as the party could
find.

## Elison

In the first week of November Sergeant [[person:George W. Rice]] and Private
[[person:Julius Frederick]] went back to [[place:Cape Isabella]] for the rest
of the English meat, with Corporal [[person:Joseph Elison]] and Sergeant Linn.
Elison froze on the return. To thaw his limbs they cut up the English ice-boat
for fuel; Frederick wrote that when his face, feet and hands began to thaw
"his sufferings were such that it was enough to bring the strongest to tears."
They could not get him over the divide between Baird Inlet and Rosse Bay. Rice
went for help in the dark across newly formed ice, twenty-five miles in sixteen
hours, and woke Greely at midnight with "Elison is dying in Rosse Bay!"
Brainard and Christiansen went out at half past four in the morning at 28
below.

Elison lived. Both hands and both feet came off by natural amputation in
January, and for the next seven and a half months he was fed, turned and
dressed by men who were starving — [[person:Henry Biederbick]] doing his wounds
on alternate days to the end. Greely wrote afterwards that no one, in all that
time, ever suggested to him that this man was a burden.

## The dying

Sergeant [[person:William H. Cross]], the launch engineer, died on 18 January
1884, the first. The rest came in a rush from April.

[[person:Thorlip Frederik Christiansen]] died at nine in the morning on 5
April. Dr. Pavy examined him and found only faint signs of scurvy: the cause
was simply not enough food. Greely's own note on it is the flattest sentence in
his book — "We dreaded to use or hear the word starvation, but that was the
plain meaning of it." [[artifact:nares-greely-031|Lockwood wrote of him]] that
he was a good man, that he had worked hard, and that he never spared himself on
any sledge trip.

Sergeant Linn died on 6 April. On 9 April Rice died on the floe at
[[place:Baird Inlet]], in Frederick's arms, trying to reach a cache of meat
that the ice had carried away; Frederick, alone and half frozen, walked ten or
twelve miles over the floe to bury him before coming back. The same day
[[person:James Booth Lockwood]] died in the hut. Sergeant Jewell followed on
the 12th.

On 29 April [[person:Jens Edward]] took his kayak into a lead in
[[place:Buchanan Bay]] after a bearded seal. Long, watching from the fast ice,
saw him suddenly paddle hard and the stern lift; the kayak had probably been
cut through by new ice. He drowned at half past eleven in the morning, and the
party's only reliable rifle went down with him.
[[artifact:nares-greely-030|Greely's diary entry]] is worth reading whole.

Then Ellis on 19 May, Ralston on the 23rd, Whisler on the 24th,
[[person:Edward Israel]] on the 27th; [[person:Frederick Fowler Kislingbury]]
on 1 June, singing the Doxology and asking for water; Salor on the 3rd;
[[person:Charles B. Henry]] shot on the 6th (see
[[page:the-shooting-of-private-henry]]); Bender and [[person:Octave Pavy]] the
same evening; Gardiner on the 12th, who had spent part of an eight-pound
baggage allowance carrying the party's only bible; Schneider on the 18th.

## The rescue

[[artifact:nares-greely-032|Greely's journal stops on 21 June]] with Connell's
legs paralysed and a south gale flattening the tent onto the men in their
sleeping bags. Nobody had eaten anything but a few square inches of soaked
sealskin and some water for forty-two hours.

[[person:Winfield Scott Schley]] had driven the Thetis and the Bear through
Melville Bay earlier in the season than any relief squadron had dared
([[artifact:nares-greely-018]]). Near midnight on 22 June Greely heard the
Thetis's whistle recalling her boats and could not believe it. Brainard went
out to the brow of the hill fifty yards off and came back saying there was
nothing to see. Then, in Greely's words,
[[artifact:nares-greely-033|strange voices were heard calling me]].

Seven were alive: Greely, Brainard, [[person:Francis Long]],
[[person:Julius Frederick]], [[person:Henry Biederbick]],
[[person:Maurice Connell]] and Elison. Elison died at Godhavn
([[place:Qeqertarsuaq]]) on 8 July after a surgical amputation. Six came home
([[artifact:nares-greely-019]]).
