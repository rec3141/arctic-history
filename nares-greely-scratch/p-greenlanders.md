Neither expedition could have moved without Greenlanders, and the way the two
records treat them is worth setting side by side.

## On the Alert and the Discovery, 1875-76

The two British ships stopped at Proven (Kangersuatsiaq) from 19 to 21 July
1875 to engage dog drivers. Three men went north with them, and all three are
in the printed crew lists.

[[person:Hans Hendrik]] of Fiskenaesset joined the Discovery. He had already
served [[person:Elisha Kent Kane]] on the Second Grinnell Expedition,
[[person:Isaac Israel Hayes]] on the United States, and
[[person:Charles Francis Hall]] on the Polaris — he had been on the ice floe
that drifted 1,500 miles with the Polaris party — and he had written his own
book, [[source:hendrik1878memoirs|Memoirs of Hans Hendrik, the Arctic
Traveller]], one of the very few nineteenth-century Arctic narratives written
by an Inuk. Thomas Mitchell photographed him with two of his children on the
Discovery's upper deck at Proven ([[artifact:nares-greely-007]]). See
[[topic:kane-hall-polaris]].

The Alert carried [[person:Frederick the Greenlander]], entered in the crew
list under that name and no other, and [[person:Niels Christian Petersen]], a
Dane long resident in Greenland who had been with Penny, Kane and M'Clintock
and who came as interpreter.

Petersen was frostbitten on a March sledge journey, had both feet amputated,
and died aboard the Alert on 14 May 1876. Hans Hendrik's part in the disastrous
Greenland sledge season is stated plainly in Nares's own narrative: when
[[person:Lewis Anthony Beaumont]]'s party came in with every man scorbutic and
two dying, the recovery of the rest was credited to Dr. Coppinger's treatment
and to "the invaluable exertions of Hans Hendrich in obtaining fresh" meat, and
of the last stage Beaumont wrote that "it is mainly due to Hans' clever
management of the dogs, and his skill as a driver, that we were enabled to
advance so rapidly with such a heavy load." Sick men were on that sledge.

On the way home the Alert put Hans and Frederick ashore at Lievely (Godhavn)
with the surviving dogs. Frederick was going home; Hans was to wait at Disco
until the ice let him travel north in the spring to rejoin his family at
Proven.

## On the Lady Franklin Bay Expedition, 1881-84

Six years later [[person:James Booth Lockwood]] took the steam launch fifty
miles down from Upernavik to the same village and came back on 28 July 1881
with two Kalaallit hunters and their kayaks: [[person:Thorlip Frederik
Christiansen]], aged thirty-five, and [[person:Jens Edward]], aged thirty-eight.
They contracted the same day. Greely's judgement on them, written after both
were dead, was that "they ever proved themselves faithful, industrious, honest,
and truthful."

They were the expedition's drivers and, at the end, its hunters. Christiansen
drove for Lockwood's northern journeys ([[artifact:nares-greely-012]]) and was
the third man at 83 deg 23.8 min N on 13 May 1882 — the highest latitude then
reached by anyone. Greely's summary sentence about that record names Lockwood
and Brainard; the plate in his own book names all three. Jens Edward drove
sledges, hunted seal, walrus and bear, and at
[[place:Camp Clay]] took his kayak out into the leads because he was the only
man who could.

At Fort Conger the two of them shared bunk 6, the only two-man bunk in the
house; every other bunk held three or four. It is a small detail from Greely's
own floor plan and it says something about where they stood in the party — with
it, and slightly apart from it.

Both died in the same month. Christiansen was delirious through the night of 4
April 1884 and died at nine the next morning; Pavy could find only faint traces
of scurvy and put the death down to lack of food.
[[artifact:nares-greely-031|Lockwood's obituary of him]] is four sentences
long and Lockwood himself was dead four days later. Jens Edward drowned on 29
April, his kayak apparently cut through by new ice in a lead in
[[place:Buchanan Bay]], while trying to reach a bearded seal for the party.
[[artifact:nares-greely-030|Greely's entry]] on it is the warmest thing in his
book, and [[artifact:nares-greely-037|Brainard's]] is plainer and just as
final: "Our poor Jens! He had grown very dear to us."

Christiansen's body was carried out of Camp Clay by the relief squadron.
[[source:brainard1929outpost|Brainard records]] that at Disco
([[place:Qeqertarsuaq]]) the rescuers and survivors stopped for a few days to
bury the man they called Eskimo Fred and to hold a memorial service jointly for
him and for Jens Edward, whose body was never recovered. It is the only funeral
either of them got.

## Reading the record

The pattern in both expeditions is the same and it is not subtle. Greenlandic
men were hired as skilled specialists, did work no one else in the party could
do, were praised in the narratives in strong terms, and appear in the
achievement sentences only sometimes. Their family names are recorded
inconsistently or not at all — the Alert's man is "Frederick, Greenlander" and
nothing more. Both of the Lady Franklin Bay drivers died of the same starvation
as the soldiers, and both are counted among the nineteen.

What changed after Greely, at [[place:Fort Conger]] itself, was scale rather
than principle: Robert Peary moved whole Inughuit families to the site from
1898 and built his shelters on Inughuit lines, and the consequences for those
families were serious. See [[page:fort-conger-after-greely]],
[[topic:greenland-inughuit]] and [[topic:sverdrup-bernier-peary]].
