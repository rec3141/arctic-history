The Admiralty sent [[person:George Strong Nares]] north in 1875 with a plain
instruction: reach the North Pole, by way of Smith Sound, and do the science on
the way. The geographers who had pressed for the expedition — Clements Markham
above all — believed that a ship that forced the channels above Baffin Bay
would break out into an open polar sea. Nares had commanded HMS Challenger on
her circumnavigation and was recalled from Hong Kong to take the job.

HMS Alert and HMS Discovery left [[place:Portsmouth]] at four in the afternoon
of 29 May 1875 ([[artifact:nares-greely-001]]), with a telegram from Balmoral
wishing them every success. They watered at Godhavn, and from 19 to 21 July
they lay at Proven (Kangersuatsiaq) in north-west Greenland engaging dog
drivers. One of them was [[person:Hans Hendrik]] of Fiskenaesset, who had
already served [[person:Elisha Kent Kane]], [[person:Isaac Israel Hayes]] and
[[person:Charles Francis Hall]] and who came aboard the Discovery with his
family ([[artifact:nares-greely-007]]). Another was the Dane
[[person:Niels Christian Petersen]], a veteran of the Franklin search.

## Through the channels

They rounded [[place:Cape Sabine]] on 4 August and spent the next three weeks
fighting north through the ice of [[place:Kane Basin]] and
[[place:Kennedy Channel]]. On 25 August the Discovery went into winter quarters
at [[place:Discovery Harbour]] in [[place:Lady Franklin Bay]] under
[[person:Henry Frederick Stephenson]]. The Alert went on alone into
[[place:Robeson Channel]].

On 1 September a south-west gale drove the pack three miles off the Ellesmere
shore, and the Alert made ten knots north past [[place:Cape Sheridan]] with the
lookout calling down "Water, plenty of water ahead, and no ice in sight."
[[person:Albert Hastings Markham]], her commander, wrote that the day
[[artifact:nares-greely-027|must always be regarded as a red-letter day]] in
the annals of naval enterprise. At noon they hoisted the colours in 82 deg 27
min N. No ship had ever been so far north, and none would winter so far north
again for thirty years.

## The floebergs

The gale that had opened the lane closed it again. The Alert stopped at an open
beach that Nares named [[place:Floeberg Beach]], after the grounded masses of
ice along it ([[artifact:nares-greely-002]]). These were not ordinary bergs.
They were fragments of a floe so old and so thick — twenty-five to fifty feet
through — that Nares coined a word for it: palaeocrystic, ancient ice. It is
the same multi-year ice that still drains out of the Arctic Ocean through
[[place:Nares Strait]] today, and it settled the expedition's fate before a
sledge was loaded. There was no open polar sea beyond. There was a floor of
rubble.

The ship lay against that beach for eleven months. The sun left them in
October and did not come back until the end of February: about 142 days of
night, longer than any expedition had yet endured, though Nares noted that
the low angle of the sun's path gave them unusually long twilights. Snow was banked against the hull, the
upper deck was housed over, the crew ran a school, printed a newspaper, put on
plays. Photographs were taken with wet plates in temperatures that froze the
collodion: Thomas Mitchell of the Discovery and George White of the Alert made
the first photographs of a ship beset above 81 degrees
([[artifact:nares-greely-003]]).

[[person:Henry Wemyss Feilden]] ([[artifact:nares-greely-006]]) collected
through it all — the birds, the musk-oxen, the fossil wood that showed these
coasts had once carried forest. [[person:Edward Lawton Moss]], the surgeon,
drew, and his chromolithographs in Shores of the Polar Sea (1878) are among the
first colour images of the coast above 82 degrees.

## What was already wrong

Something else was in the ship, and nobody recognised it. The Royal Navy had
carried lime juice as an antiscorbutic for seventy years, and by 1875 the
service had convinced itself that fresh air and cleanliness mattered more.
Lime juice was issued on board but not carried on the sledges: it froze, and
the weight was thought better spent on food. The lime juice of the 1870s, made
from West Indian limes and heated in copper, had in any case lost most of its
vitamin C.

The sledge crews would go out in April 1876 hauling about 240 pounds a man
— Markham's own figure for the most that should ever be asked of a sledge crew —
on a diet of pemmican, biscuit, bacon and rum, with no fresh food at all. See
[[page:nares-1876-farthest-north-and-the-scurvy]].
