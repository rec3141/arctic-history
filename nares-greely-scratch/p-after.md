[[person:Adolphus Washington Greely]] walked out of [[place:Fort Conger]] on 9
August 1883 leaving the dishes on the table. Fifteen and a half years later
someone opened the door.

## Peary, 6 January 1899

Robert Peary sledged up the Ellesmere coast from Cape D'Urville in the darkness
of December 1898 with the surgeon T.S. Dedrick, Matthew Henson, and four
Inughuit men — Uutaaq, Iggiannguaq, Ukkujaaq and Sigluk — using shelters they
had built a day's dog-run apart along the whole route. They reached Fort Conger
on 6 January 1899. Peary's own account of the interior:

> Forcing an entrance and lighting our oil stove, [I] found the interior
> presenting the utmost confusion. Floor of both officers' and men's quarters
> and kitchen blocked and littered with boxes, packed and empty, pieces of fur,
> cast-off clothing, rubbish of all descriptions... In the men's quarters
> dishes remained on the table just as left after lunch or dinner on the day
> the fort was deserted.

Peary froze most of his toes on that journey and had them amputated in the
Greely house. He used the station as his forward base until 1909.

## The three huts

When his supply ship Windward could not get through in 1900, Peary pulled
Greely's house down. It was a prefabricated building designed in the United
States, and Bertulli, Dick and Dawson's Parks Canada study is blunt about it:
"entirely unsuited to the rigours of an Arctic climate." From its lumber, and
scavenged wood, his party built three small semi-subterranean huts in August
1900 — double wooden walls packed with silt and gravel, six layers including
tar paper and asbestos paper and unused star charts and meteorological record
sheets from Greely's own stores, banked with earth and turf and then with
drifted snow, connected by excavated tunnels roofed with canvas, musk-ox skins
and snow-block domes.

That is Inughuit building practice applied with American lumber: small,
grouped, semi-subterranean, sharing entrances so that heat is not thrown away.
What was not Inughuit was how the space was ranked. Peary's own quarters
connected to Dedrick's; Dedrick passed orders to Henson and to the Inughuit,
who were housed in the huts beyond. The three huts are still standing, and are
Classified Federal Heritage Buildings; the roofless north-west one was the
Inughuit hut ([[source:bertulli2013fort]]).

Peary's 1898-1902 expedition was the first on which he moved whole Inughuit
families north to support his attempts on the Pole. Lyle Dick's work, cited in
the same study, connects the severe psychological distress among the Inughuit
that Peary's people labelled pibloktoq to what was actually being done to them:
long separations from family, dangerous sledging, sexual exploitation and abuse
of women, compulsory work routines and rationing. See
[[topic:sverdrup-bernier-peary]] and [[topic:greenland-inughuit]].

## Everyone who came after

Fort Conger stayed useful for another half-century as a depot and a landmark.
W.E. Ekblaw of MacMillan's Crocker Land Expedition and his Inughuit companions
Esayoo and E-took-a-shoo slept there in May 1915 and cooked on the Army Range
still standing in Greely's kitchen. In April 1920 Godfred Hansen of the Royal
Danish Navy, on the Third Thule Expedition, left about 300 pounds of food,
kerosene, ammunition and a report for Roald Amundsen in Henson's hut, a depot
still called Amundsen's Cache. Lauge Koch stayed in April 1921 with Inughuit
companions, one of whom had helped build the huts twenty-one years before, and
wrote that his guides sang drum songs in them long into the night. The last
expedition to use the place was Edward Shackleton's Oxford University Ellesmere
Land Expedition: in April and May 1935 RCMP Sergeant Henry Stallworthy, A.W.
Moore and the Inughuit Nukappiannguaq and Inuutiq stopped there on their way to
and from Lake Hazen, burned Fort Conger's coal, ate its pemmican and its
half-mouldy cigars, and fed the last of Amundsen's cache to their dogs.

## The site now

Fort Conger is a national historic site of Canada inside Quttinirpaaq National
Park, and it holds three layers at once. There are Nares expedition relics
including the post-office cairn built of gravel-filled meat tins and topped
with a globe of barrel hoops, and the boards commemorating J.J. Hand and C.
Paul, who died of scurvy in June 1876. There are Greely's remains — the house
foundation, the louvres of the thermometer shelter, the brick instrument
pedestal, tent rings, a portable forge, metal bedsteads. And there are Peary's
three huts.

Everything is degrading: wood weathering, the bank eroding, visitors, and
inorganic contamination in the soil from a century of tins and fuel. Parks
Canada has recorded the standing structures with 3D laser scanning so that
there will be a record when the buildings go.

Two hundred and fifty kilometres to the north-east, at the other end of the
same shore, [[place:CFS Alert]] sits a few kilometres from
[[place:Floeberg Beach]] and takes its name from Nares's ship
([[artifact:nares-greely-020]]). It is a weather station, a signals station and
an atmospheric baseline observatory, and it is the direct institutional
descendant of what Greely's men were doing in a peg-jointed shed in 1882. See
[[topic:science-stations]].
