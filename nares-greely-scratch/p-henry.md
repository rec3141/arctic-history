Private Charles B. Henry was the biggest and strongest man in the party and he
stole food. On 6 June 1884, on [[person:Adolphus Washington Greely]]'s written
order, three sergeants shot him. It is the only military execution in the
history of Arctic exploration, and it is the part of the Lady Franklin Bay
Expedition that has been argued about ever since.

## Who he was

He enlisted in the Fifth Cavalry as Charles B. Henry, and his company
commander's recommendation was strong enough that Greely, who knew the man
personally, made a place for him. He also arranged to write for the Chicago
Times from the Arctic.

His real name was Charles Henry Buck, born in Hanover in 1856. He had worked in
a print shop in Moberly, Missouri, joined the Seventh Cavalry in 1876, been
convicted in 1878 of forging his company commander's signature on orders for
whisky, served a year, and turned up in Deadwood, where he killed a Chinese man
in a gambling brawl. He then enlisted under the alias. Officers of his old
regiment recognised his face in a group photograph of the expedition printed in
Harper's Weekly and reported him to the War Department, but by then he was in
the Arctic. This account is [[source:stein2011arctic|Glenn Stein's]], drawn
from army and newspaper records.

At [[place:Fort Conger]] Henry put on twenty-five pounds. At
[[place:Camp Clay]] he was, by every diary, the boldest of the thieves — and he
was not the only one. Greely's own record names Bender, Schneider and Dr. Pavy
as taking sealskin and thongs, and Whisler as forcing the storehouse door.
Hunger did that to almost everyone. What set Henry apart was that he kept
doing it after warnings, admitted it without contrition, and was physically
strong enough that nobody could stop him.

## The orders

Greely had held an informal trial in March and let the matter go. On 5 June
1884 he wrote to Sergeants [[person:David Legge Brainard]],
[[person:Julius Frederick]] and [[person:Francis Long]]:

> Private Henry having been repeatedly guilty of stealing the provisions of
> this party, which is now perishing slowly by starvation, has so far been
> condoned and pardoned. It is, however, imperatively ordered that if this man
> be detected either eating food of any kind not issued him regularly, or
> making caches or appropriating any article of provisions, you will at once
> shoot him and report the matter to me. Any other course would be a fatal
> leniency, the man being able to overpower any two of our present force.

The next day Frederick caught him taking shrimps from the mess pot, and Greely
stopped him coming back from the old winter quarters with sealskin. Greely
wrote the second order: [[artifact:nares-greely-029|Private Henry will be shot
to-day]], the manner of death to be decided by two ball and one blank
cartridge.

The party had one serviceable rifle left, so the two-ball-and-a-blank could not
be done. According to an account the journalist Frank Copley took from
Brainard, Long and Biederbick at an Arctic Club dinner in 1906 and published in
1910-11, the three sergeants drew lots for who would fire and swore never to
say which of them it was. They never did. Copley's version has Henry taken down
to the old hut on a pretext, told to his face what was about to happen, making
a lunge for an axe on the ground, and shot twice — in the chest and then the
head. Shots were heard from the tent about two in the afternoon.

Greely wrote in his diary that evening that "every one, without exception,
acknowledged that Henry's fate was merited," and that twelve pounds of hidden
sealskin, a pair of Greely's own boots and a chronograph stolen from Fort
Conger were found among his effects. Two more men, Bender and
[[person:Octave Pavy]], died within four hours.

## Afterwards

Greely reported the execution verbally to his superiors within days of landing
and in writing to the Adjutant General from Portsmouth on 11 August 1884,
asking for a court of inquiry or a court martial. On 14 November 1884 Adjutant
General R.C. Drum replied that, having read the diaries of the several members
of the expedition and Henry's own, the Secretary of War "entertains no doubt of
the necessity, and the entire propriety" of the order, and that no inquiry was
required. Henry was buried with full military honours at Cypress Hills National
Cemetery in Brooklyn; the cemetery record gives his cause of death as
starvation. A woman claiming to be his sister asked for an exhumation and
autopsy. It never happened.

Brainard, asked about it decades later, said twice in almost the same words
that he would have shot a man who stole from the starving as gladly as he would
have shot a wolf.

## The cutting of the dead

There is a second thing, and it is not in Greely's book at all.

When Lieutenant Colwell of the Bear walked down to the ice foot at Cape Sabine
on the night of the rescue he found a body that was afterwards identified from
a bullet hole as Henry's. The seaman keeping a journal aboard the Bear wrote
that they "found the bones of a large man about 75 yds from hut, no head."
Greely's people had been too weak to dig graves; the dead had been laid in the
ice foot or in cracks.

The relief squadron brought the bodies home. Through August 1884, as they were
released to families, it became public that flesh had been cut from several of
them, and the American press carried the story — the New York Times of 9 August
1884 ran it under the heading "Greely's dead comrades." Greely stated that he
had not known of it at the time, and that no man's death had been hastened by
it. He never wrote about it afterwards: [[artifact:nares-greely-035|the second
volume of Three Years of Arctic Service]], which prints the execution orders in
full, is silent on the cutting.

Modern historians do not dispute that some of the survivors ate the flesh of
the dead at Camp Clay; [[source:guttridge2000ghosts|Leonard Guttridge's
account]], built from the men's private diaries, is the fullest. What is not
established, and probably cannot be, is how many bodies were cut, by whom, or
whether Greely knew. It is worth keeping the two questions separate. The
execution was ordered in writing by a commander who then demanded to be tried
for it. The cannibalism was concealed, denied, and reported by newspapers
rather than by any of the men who came home.

Both belong to the same fact, which is that a national scientific expedition
was left on a beach for two years because two relief ships failed and nobody
ashore thought hard enough about what would happen if they did.
