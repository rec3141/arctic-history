Greely's orders were explicit. If no ship reached him in 1882 he was to stay
another year. If none reached him in 1883, he was to abandon
[[place:Fort Conger]] not later than 1 September 1883 and retreat south along
the east coast of Ellesmere Island to [[place:Cape Sabine]], where a depot
would be waiting. He followed those orders exactly, and they nearly killed
everyone.

## Two failed reliefs

In 1882 the steamer Neptune under Lieutenant [[person:William M. Beebe]] was
stopped by ice in Smith Sound. She landed small caches at
[[place:Cape Sabine]] and Littleton Island and went home. Nothing reached Fort
Conger. Greely's men had food and coal enough and a second Polar Year winter
to work through, so this was a disappointment rather than a crisis.

In 1883 it became a crisis. Lieutenant [[person:Ernest A. Garlington]] took the
Proteus — the same sealer that had carried the expedition north in 1881 — into
[[place:Kane Basin]] in July. On 23 July she was nipped between floes and sank
([[artifact:nares-greely-017]]). Most of the stores went down with her. The
party retreated south in boats and reached Upernavik. Before leaving, they
cached what they had saved at Cape Sabine and left a written record. The record
was hopeful about what was there. It was wrong, and Greely believed it for
weeks.

## Out of Fort Conger

On the morning of 9 August 1883 Greely watched the ice reports, and at ten
o'clock ordered the formal abandonment of the station at one in the afternoon.
Sergeant Jewell took the last scientific observation at one o'clock. Brainard
opened barrels of blubber, pork, beef and bread so that the dogs, who could not
be taken, might live for some months; Greely would not have them shot in case
the party had to come back. Three tons of coal, the natural history
collections and a year's rough rations were left in the house.

At two in the afternoon the steam launch Lady Greely
([[artifact:nares-greely-013]]) took three boats in tow and started across
Archer Fiord.

## The drift

What followed is one of the harder retreats in the record
([[artifact:nares-greely-025]]). They worked south through
[[place:Kennedy Channel]] in the boats when there was water and hauled when
there was not. On 17 August they were ice-bound in 80 deg 44 min N. On 25
August they were near Cape Frazer in 79 deg 45 min N. On 26 August they reached
Cape Hawks at a quarter past two and left again at twenty-five past four.

On 27 August, in 79 deg 22 min N and 75 deg W, the ice closed round them for
good. They abandoned the launch, got onto a floe with the boats and the stores,
and drifted. For thirty days the pack carried them south and east across
[[place:Kane Basin]] — 79 deg 19 min on 1 September, 79 deg 0.6 min on 6
September off Victoria Head, 78 deg 50.9 min on 12 September — while they
watched the Ellesmere shore go past out of reach. Greely's daily latitudes are
the only fix on the drift; he could rarely get a longitude.

On 29 September the floe brought them within three miles of the land. Brainard
went ahead at half past four in the morning and found open water in the way;
they brought up a boat, ferried two lanes, struck fast ice and walked in.
Lockwood's party touched shore at twenty past five in the evening. Greely was
the last across. The point, he wrote, "consists of nothing but immense boulders
of granitic rock, piled one upon another; but it is solid land, and all are
exceedingly thankful to have it under foot once more."

The retreat had covered over four hundred miles by boat and a hundred more by
sledge and boat. Not one man had been lost.

## Camp Clay

Now they looked for the depot. On 15 October Greely walked down to within a
mile of Cape Sabine and opened the wreck cache from the Proteus. Instead of the
five hundred rations of meat Garlington's record had promised, there were
barely a hundred. Sergeant Rice came back from [[place:Cape Isabella]] with the
news that the English depot Nares had left in 1875 held 144 pounds of meat and
no boat.

Between the wreck cache, the Beebe cache of 1882 and the Cape Isabella meat,
Greely had perhaps forty days' full rations for twenty-five men, and eight
months of winter in front of him. He chose the best ground on
[[place:Bedford Pim Island]], beside a small lake inland from the wreck cache,
and the party built [[place:Camp Clay]]: a stone wall about waist high, banked
outside with snow, roofed with the overturned whaleboat carried on sawn-off
oars, with a canvas-covered passage and a commissary at one end. Twenty-five
men, their sleeping bags and a cooking space in the middle.

They moved in during the third week of October 1883. See
[[page:the-winter-at-camp-clay]].
