An oak carpenter's plane, from a Thule winter house on [[place:Skraeling Island]], excavated by Peter Schledermann and Karen McCullough. It is the most domestic object in the whole Ellesmere assemblage and, for that reason, the hardest to explain away. Chain mail can be a curiosity passed hand to hand; a boat rivet can be scrap iron; a plane is a woodworker's tool, made of oak, from a tree that grows no nearer than southern Scandinavia or the British Isles, and it is the sort of thing a ship's carpenter carries and does not casually give away. It belongs to the list Hayeur Smith, Smith and Nilsen give of the Norse material from the site: chain mail, an oak carpenter's plane, boat rivets, and two pieces of woven woollen cloth, all from an early Thule, Ruin Island phase settlement they date to 1200-1300 AD and describe as occupied in the late thirteenth or early fourteenth century. The plane came out of the seasons after Schledermann's 1980 preliminary report, and its full publication is McCullough's 1989 monograph The Ruin Islanders (Mercury Series, Archaeological Survey of Canada paper 141), where the house number, the dimensions and the wood identification are given.
