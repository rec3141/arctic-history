A small wooden carving found in the summer of 1977 in House 8 at the Okivilialuk site on the north side of McKellar Bay, near Kimmirut (Lake Harbour) on the south coast of Qikiqtaaluk. Deborah Sabo and George Sabo III give it as 5.4 cm long, about 2 cm wide and 1 cm thick; the Canadian Museum of History's record measures it at 53.8 by 18.7 by 9.4 mm. Photograph: [[artifact:norse-helluland-036]]. Its face is featureless and its appendages attenuated, both normal in Thule carving; what is not normal is a long hooded robe falling nearly to the feet, slit or pleated in front from below the waist to the hem, the line of a yoke or cape running across the shoulders, and an incised cross on the chest. It is the single most-reproduced image in this whole subject and it is usually captioned as a Norseman or even a bishop.

The find context is known in full from Sabo and Sabo's 1978 report. Along the inner edge of the gravel sleeping platform were three upstanding slabs forming a box-like niche; the figurine lay in it on a thin piece of cut mica resting directly on the slab floor, with a small oval wooden disk, a wooden wick trimmer and a small wooden toggle beside it. A single slab had fallen into the niche and wedged there, and the deposit was further sealed by the later growth of sod over the house interior. On and between the floor slabs were a small wooden gull hook shank and two antler spoons, and directly under one slab of the niche a Thule type 2 harpoon head of antler with a prominent angled-spur base, an open socket and a self-bladed rounded tip. Sabo and Sabo note the absence of historic items in the lower soil deposit; the upper deposit, above the buried sod, held nails and other material obviously introduced by Euro-Canadians. Six to eight kilometres away at Talaguak on the same bay, the same 1977 season found a small iron-bladed ulu on an undisturbed early Thule occupation floor.

IT HAS NEVER BEEN RADIOCARBON DATED. Sabo and Sabo date the lower component of House 8 to the 12th or 13th century on harpoon typology alone (Ford 1959: 95-96), and write that accurate radiocarbon dating of these assemblages is highly desirable, which is to say that none had been attempted; their summary sentence is that Thule Eskimos met with Vikings on the south coast of Baffin Island 'sometime around A.D. 1200'. Birgitta Wallace read the hood as 11th or 12th century because it lacks the trailing liripipe that appears from the 13th century on. The Canadian Museum of History catalogues the object as circa AD 1250-1300, and labels it 'about 1350' in the Canadian History Hall: three different dates for one undated object.

Guy Mary-Rousseliere in 1984 accepted a European reading of the dress but rejected the bishop interpretation outright: long garments were ordinary lay wear in the 13th and early 14th centuries, and medieval ecclesiastical costume rarely carried a cross; he suggested a knight of a military order instead. In 2018 Michele Hayeur Smith and colleagues AMS-dated two textiles from Okivilialuk to the 15th to 17th centuries and pointed out that the site is 240 km by coastal sailing from Kodlunarn Island, where Frobisher mined in 1576-78; they asked whether the figure might represent a member of Frobisher's crew, a priest attached to his expedition, or simply an Inuit person in such garments. On the stratigraphy the two results need not be in conflict: the 1978 report places the figurine in a sealed sub-sod deposit with no historic material in it, and never assigns any textile to that deposit. Cloth appears in the report exactly once, as an unfinished line of work - 'identification of cloth fragments from Thule contexts at Okivilialuk' - with no house, level or feature given. So Sutherland's reply, that the excavation records put the carving at a lower level than the textiles, is consistent with the published record, though the published record does not itself say where the textiles came from. What it comes to is that the figurine's own date is still typological, and the redated textiles neither confirm nor refute it.
