The finding that settles the yarn argument. Michele Hayeur Smith, Kevin Smith and Gorill Nilsen ran nine new AMS dates on spun fibre from three sites - Nanook and Nunguvik on Qikiqtaaluk and Willows Island 4 in Frobisher Bay - and their conclusion is that Dorset communities had been making spun and plied yarn for at least a thousand years before the Norse arrived. The fibres are musk ox and arctic hare, wild local animals: wool was present only in the woven textiles from Skraeling Island and Okivilialuk. One Nanook sample is spun and plied sinew, and it is the oldest date in the treated set, which suggests the whole tradition grew out of an older indigenous practice of spinning and plying sinew rather than out of anyone's instruction. Technologically the two traditions are also unlike: Z2S-plied yarn accounts for 98.1 per cent of 105 plied pieces from the three sites, uniform and well combed, and there is no evidence the Dorset ever wove their plied yarn into cloth, whereas weaving was what the Norse did with yarn and plied yarn was never used for making cloth in Greenland. The authors' conclusion is flat: 'no dates yet document the production of these fibers at any times during which Dorset/Norse interaction was possible.'
