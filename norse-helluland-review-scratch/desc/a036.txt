The best available photograph of the Okivilialuk figurine, from the Canadian Museum of History's own collections record (artifact 482525, negative CD1996-0117-053, related image S94-6299). The carving is shown lying on its back on the stone it was photographed against; the original slide scan is 3072 by 2048 pixels. Measurements from the museum record: length 53.8 mm, width 18.7 mm, thickness 9.4 mm; material, wood. What the photograph shows and does not show is worth stating plainly. The featureless head, the stumpy arms and the tapered legs are all ordinary Thule carving. The garment reaching nearly to the feet, the horizontal line across the shoulders that may be the edge of a hooded capelet, and the vertical split down the front are what the argument rests on. The incised cross on the chest is at the limit of what this photograph resolves; Deborah and George Sabo examined it microscopically and concluded that the vertical arm is a natural split in the grain that probably opened along the length of an incised line, and that the thin incisions were probably cut with a quartz crystal blade rather than a metal implement. The museum dates the object circa AD 1250-1300, which is later than the excavators' 12th to 13th century estimate and earlier than the about 1350 given in the Canadian History Hall label for the same object.
