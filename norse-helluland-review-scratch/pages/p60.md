# The other side of the meeting, and the end of Norse Greenland

Everything on the other pages is European evidence: sagas written in Iceland,
annals kept in Iceland, objects excavated by Canadians and Danes. This page
gathers what the people the Norse called *skrælingar* had to say, and what
happened to the Norse.

## What the Norse called them

*Skræling* is not a neutral word. It has been derived from *skrá*, dried skin,
and from a root meaning to shriek or to be feeble; nobody is certain, and every
proposed etymology is uncomplimentary. It is applied indiscriminately in the
Norse sources to the Dorset, to the Thule, and to the peoples of Vinland, who
were unrelated to each other. It is a word for "not us".

Norse writing about them is thin and almost entirely secondhand. The thirteenth-
century *[[artifact:norse-helluland-014|Historia Norwegiæ]]* reports only that
hunters north of the Greenlanders came across "certain small people whom they
call Skrælings". The sagas describe trade in red cloth and milk for furs, and
then fighting. The clerics who sailed north about 1267 found
[[artifact:norse-helluland-005|"ancient dwelling-places of Skrælings"]] and were
interested in how far the people extended and where they came from — an
ethnographic question, from a party that seems not to have met anyone.

## What the Greenlanders remembered

[[person:Hinrich Rink]] spent, by his own account, sixteen winters and
twenty-two summers on the shores of Davis Strait, from the southernmost point of
Greenland up to 73° north, "first as a scientific explorer, afterwards as Royal
Inspector or Governor of the Southern Danish establishments in Greenland". The
tales he collected were written down in part by Greenlanders themselves. He
introduces the four *kavdlunait* stories with a caution worth quoting because it
is so exact: they are known only to the west Greenlanders, especially the
southernmost, and represent "the only trace of intelligence left concerning the
ancient Scandinavian settlers which the author has been able to discover by
inquiries made in the country."

Four hundred years after the last Norseman, that is what remained. Two stories,
localised to the two districts where the ruins stand.

[[artifact:norse-helluland-035|The first meeting]] is friendly. A boat's crew
comes up Kangersuneq, finds a large house with people in it who are not
Kaladlit, and is treated "kindly and civilly"; they are frightened anyway and
leave. Word spreads, more boats come, trade begins, the foreigners start
learning the language. At Kapisilik a Norseman and a Greenlander become
inseparable friends and compete at archery until the Norseman proposes a
lethal wager, insists on it against his friend's refusal — and misses. His own
countrymen think it served him right. The mountain is called Pisigsarfik, the
shooting-place, to this day.

[[artifact:norse-helluland-013|The Ungortok story]] is the other kind, and it
begins with a Norseman inviting his own death. A Norseman gathering shells on
the beach at Kakortok calls out to a passing kayaker from Arpatsivik, "Let us
see whether thou canst hit me with thy lance." The kayaker refuses, and refuses
again; then the chief of the place, Ungortok, says "Since he seems so very
anxious about it, take good aim at him", and the kayaker throws in earnest and
kills him. Ungortok does not reproach him. Three winters later the same man
kills a second Norseman for no reason at all. Then the Norse come at night with
axes and destroy a whole household, and the rest of the tale is Kaisape's long
revenge, ending with Ungortok shot beside a lake at Aluk on the southeast coast.

Two things are worth noticing.

First, these are not stories of innocent victims. The first killing is done by a
Greenlander, at the victim's own repeated invitation and with the Norse chief's
consent; the second is unprovoked, and it is what brings the axes. Nobody in the
tale comes out of it well. That is not the shape a legend takes when its job is
to justify — it is the shape a story takes when it has been told for a long time
by people who were there.

Second, the archaeology does not corroborate the war. There is no burn layer at
Hvalsey. Rink himself, an honest editor, notes that the two stories come from
six manuscripts with their contents partly mixed up, that the same events have
been localised separately to the Julianehaab and Godthaab districts, and that
the Godthaab narrators had adapted the southern story to their own fiords of
Kapisilik, Pisigsarfik and Ameralik and inserted another tale to explain how the
fighting began. These are traditions about ruins as much as traditions from the
events.

## What actually ended the colony

Nobody recorded the end of Norse Greenland. There is no last letter, no
evacuation, no massacre in any document. There is a
[[artifact:norse-helluland-017|wedding at Hvalsey in September 1408]], and then
nothing.

What the evidence supports:

**The Western Settlement went first.** Ívar Bárðarson, sent out from Bergen in
1341 to run the diocese at [[place:Gardar]], sailed north to the Western
Settlement at some point before 1360 and found the farms empty. Not burnt;
empty. Archaeoentomology at the farm called Gården under Sandet suggests a
final very poor season, the colonists eating their dogs and livestock, and
starvation over a winter.

**The climate turned.** Reconstructions of North Atlantic seasonal temperature
show maximum summer temperatures falling from the late thirteenth century,
and the coldest winters of the last two thousand years in the late fourteenth
and early fifteenth. Norse Greenland was a marginal farming society in a good
climate. It was not one in a bad climate.

**The market collapsed.** This may matter more than the weather.
[[source:star2018ancient|Ancient DNA from walrus rostra]] in medieval European
workshops shows Greenland holding something close to a monopoly on European
ivory for two hundred years — and then
[[source:barrett2020ecological|later specimens are smaller, more often female,
and from more northerly stocks]], the signature of a hunt working further and
further up the coast as the near grounds emptied. Meanwhile elephant ivory came
back into Europe. A single-commodity economy, at the end of a very long supply
line, whose commodity was both depleting and losing its price.

[[artifact:norse-helluland-034|Genomic sourcing published in 2024]] puts the
Norse hunting grounds in the North Water Polynya — Pikialasorsuaq — between
Ellesmere Island and northwest Greenland. To keep the trade going they were
sailing to roughly 77°N. That is what
[[artifact:norse-helluland-003|three men at 73°N in about 1300]] and
[[artifact:norse-helluland-015|"Skegge the Stately... they were lost, and never
found"]] look like from the demand side.

**And the Inuit did better.** The Thule arrived in Greenland in the thirteenth
century with a marine hunting technology — the toggling harpoon, the kayak, the
umiaq, the dog sled, the snow house, sealskin clothing — adapted to exactly the
conditions that were killing the Norse farms. They are still there. The Norse,
who kept cattle in Greenland for four hundred years and buried their dead in
consecrated ground in a place with no wood for coffins, are not.

That is the comparison that makes this history worth having on a ship in these
waters. Two societies met in the same environment; one was equipped for it and
one was not, and the one that was is the one that is still here. The people of
[[topic:thule-dorset]] and [[topic:greenland-inughuit]], and the communities of
[[place:Mittimatalik]], [[place:Kimmirut]] and the rest of Qikiqtaaluk, are the
continuous thread. The Norse are the episode.

## What the sagas got right

One last thing, in fairness to a much-abused body of literature. The sagas said
there were three lands to the west: barren rock, then forest, then somewhere
warm enough for grapes. They said people were living there. They said the Norse
traded with them and then fought them and left.

Every one of those statements is true. L'Anse aux Meadows was identified in 1960
where a saga said to look. The problem has never been that the sagas are
worthless. It is that they are not precise enough to be checked, and that a
century and a half of people have wanted them to say more than they do.
