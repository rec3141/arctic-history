# The Helluland Archaeology Project and its critics

The claim that Norse voyagers were present on Baffin Island over a long period,
and traded there with the Dorset, is the most interesting thing anyone has said
about this subject in fifty years. It is also, as things stand, unproven, and
some of the specific evidence it rested on has since been dated and found to
belong to somebody else entirely.

This page tries to be fair to both sides. The controversy is unusually tangled,
because a scientific argument and an employment dispute happened at the same
time and in public.

## The claim

[[person:Patricia Sutherland]], then Curator of Arctic Archaeology at the
Canadian Museum of Civilization, noticed in the late 1990s that spun yarn in
the museum's collections from Dorset sites on Baffin Island resembled Norse
yarn from Greenland. Dorset people were not thought to have spun thread. From
1999 she pursued the implication through the collections and then into the
field, as the Helluland Archaeology Project.

The evidence she assembled, over some fifteen years, was:

- **Spun yarn and cordage** from four sites facing Greenland:
  [[place:Nunguvik]] (PgHb-1) near Mittimatalik, the Nanook site in
  [[place:Tanfield Valley]] (KdDq-9), Willows Island 4 in Frobisher Bay, and
  Avayalik in northern Labrador.
- **Whetstones** — dozens across four sites — carrying wear-traces of copper
  alloy, brass and smelted iron: tools for sharpening metal blades, in places
  with no metallurgy.
- **Wooden tally sticks** of a kind used in Norse Greenland, and carved faces
  read as European.
- A **turf-and-stone structure** at Tanfield Valley, first dug by Moreau Maxwell
  in the 1960s, who called it "very difficult to interpret".
- An **Old World rat pelt** at Nanook. This one has been reported in press
  coverage only; it has never been published in a peer-reviewed journal, and it
  should be treated as a claim rather than a finding.
- [[artifact:norse-helluland-030|A small stone crucible]] from the same site,
  published with Peter Thompson and Patricia Hunt in *Geoarchaeology* in 2015,
  whose interior carries copper–tin alloy traces and high-temperature glass
  spherules.

Taken together, that is a picture of Europeans wintering on Baffin Island,
sharpening tools, working bronze, and trading with the people already there.
James Tuck of Memorial University, quoted by *National Geographic* in 2012,
said the evidence had gone from compelling to convincing.

## What has been tested since

**The yarn.** This is where the argument has been decided, and it was decided by
better dating rather than by opinion.

Dating organic material in the Arctic is hard because marine mammal oil soaks
into everything and carries old carbon. Sutherland knew this: she reported
offsets of up to 2,000 years on these samples and concluded in 2009 that it was
"not considered useful to publish the dates obtained." In 2018,
[[source:hayeursmith2018dorset|Michèle Hayeur Smith, Kevin Smith and Gørill
Nilsen]] published a protocol that removes the oil entirely, validated it on
material of known age, and applied it.

The results:

- [[artifact:norse-helluland-031|The two Nunguvik yarns]] date to
  **AD 602–674** and **AD 664–770**. Seventh and eighth century — two to four
  centuries before there was a Norse settlement in Greenland.
- [[artifact:norse-helluland-033|Nine new dates from three sites]] — Nanook,
  Nunguvik and Willows Island 4 — show that Dorset communities "had been making
  spun and plied yarn for at least 1000 years before the Norse arrived". The
  oldest date in the treated set is on spun and plied **sinew**, which suggests
  the whole tradition grew out of an older indigenous practice.
- The fibres are **musk ox and arctic hare**, not sheep. Sheep's wool appears
  only in the woven cloth from [[place:Skraeling Island]] and
  [[place:Okivilialuk]].
- The two traditions are technologically unlike. Z2S-plied yarn accounts for
  98.1% of 105 plied pieces from the three sites, uniform and well-combed, and
  there is no evidence the Dorset ever wove their plied yarn into cloth.
  Weaving was the Norse use for yarn, and plied yarn was never used for making
  cloth in Greenland.

And crucially: at Nunguvik, paired dates on the same objects with and without
the oil removed came out statistically indistinguishable. Those early dates
were not a contamination artefact. They were right. The protocol was not
window-dressing either — one Nanook sample came out about 3,500 years older
untreated than treated — which is exactly why the paired dating had to be done
before anything could be concluded.

The authors' sentence is flat: "no dates yet document the production of these
fibers at any times during which Dorset/Norse interaction was possible."

There is a further sting. Sutherland's argument had been that spinning was a
skill the Dorset must have learned. Hayeur Smith's team suggest the traffic may
have gone the other way — that arctic hare fur in Norse Greenlandic textiles may
have been "the result of Inuit women instructing the Norse how best to use local
resources, rather than the Norse teaching Arctic North Americans to spin".

**The figurine.** [[artifact:norse-helluland-029|The Okivilialuk carving]] is
not Sutherland's find — Deborah and George Sabo published it in 1978 — but it
is the image the whole idea travels on, and
[[artifact:norse-helluland-036|the museum's photograph of it]] is one of the
most reproduced pictures in Arctic archaeology. It has **never been radiocarbon
dated**. Its site chronology rests on harpoon typology alone.

The Sabos' report is careful and specific about where the carving lay. It was in
a box-like niche of three upstanding slabs at the inner edge of the gravel
sleeping platform of House 8, resting on a thin piece of cut mica directly on
the slab floor, with a small oval wooden disk, a wick trimmer and a toggle
beside it. A slab had fallen into the niche and wedged there; sod grew over the
house interior afterwards and sealed it. **They note the absence of historic
items in the lower soil deposit.** The upper deposit, above the buried sod,
contained nails and other Euro-Canadian metal. The date of the lower component —
twelfth or thirteenth century — comes from a classic Thule type 2 harpoon head
found directly under one of the floor slabs, and the Sabos close by writing that
accurate radiocarbon dating of these assemblages is highly desirable, which is
their way of saying that none had been run.

So when Hayeur Smith and colleagues in 2018 AMS-dated two textiles from
Okivilialuk to the **fifteenth to seventeenth centuries** — in a place 240 km by
coastal sailing from Kodlunarn Island, where Frobisher mined in 1576–78 — and
asked whether the figure might be a member of Frobisher's crew, a priest
attached to his expedition, or simply an Inuit person in such garments, the two
results do not actually collide. The 1978 report never assigns any textile to
House 8's sealed lower deposit; it mentions cloth exactly once, as an unfinished
line of work, "identification of cloth fragments from Thule contexts at
Okivilialuk", with no house, level or feature named. Sutherland's reply, that
the excavation records put the carving at a lower level than the textiles, is
therefore consistent with the published record — though the published record
does not itself say where the textiles came from, and her reply exists only as a
self-posted preprint that has never been peer reviewed.

What survives all of this is a narrower and duller statement than either side
would like. The figurine is an undated object in a typologically dated deposit.
The textiles are dated objects of unrecorded provenience. Neither dates the
other. And the Canadian Museum of History, which holds it, gives three
different dates for it in three different places: circa AD 1250–1300 in the
catalogue record, "about 1350" on the Canadian History Hall label, and the
excavators' twelfth-to-thirteenth century in the literature it cites.

**The crucible.** Here the sceptics have *not* done the work. No critique,
comment, reanalysis or rebuttal of the 2015 *Geoarchaeology* paper has appeared
in the peer-reviewed literature. The published objections are journalism, and
they are the obvious ones: the vessel was recovered in the 1960s from a site
occupied and abandoned repeatedly, and its radiocarbon bracket runs from 754 BC
to AD 1367 — a span of more than two thousand years. The analysis of what is
*in* the vessel looks sound. What is unestablished is *when*, and by whom.

**The wider frame.** Robert Park's 2008 review in *Antiquity* argues the Dorset
owed nothing to the Norse and probably never met them. And Patrick Jolicoeur's
2021 work in *American Antiquity* shows Late Dorset using metal at least as
often as stone for some tasks, moved thousands of kilometres through
**indigenous** exchange networks. Metal in the Arctic does not require a Norse
explanation. Neither, it turns out, does yarn.

## The other dispute

Sutherland was terminated by the museum in April 2012, after about thirty years,
and the Helluland project stopped. The museum said nothing for over two years.
Then, in December 2014, after a CBC interview in which Sutherland agreed that
she might have been fired because her research was out of step with government
views of Canadian history, the museum broke silence and stated that she had been
"terminated for harassment following an 18-month investigation done by an
independent third party". Sutherland categorically denies the allegation. No
outcome of any grievance or legal action has been reported.

Two things follow, and both should be said.

The dismissal is not evidence about the archaeology. A researcher can be right
about Baffin Island and wrong about how they treat colleagues, or the reverse;
neither settles the other.

But the dismissal *did* affect the archaeology, in a way that matters. The
project volume was suspended. Sutherland lost access to the collections and, as
far as has been reported, had not regained it. The crucible and the soil
samples are held in trust by the museum pending a museum facility in Nunavut.
Much of the excavated material from Tanfield Valley has never been published in
full. That means the strongest version of her case has never been laid out
where it can be tested — which is bad for her, and bad for everyone else too.

The museum's own public position, given in 2018: "L'Anse aux Meadows is the only
recognized Viking site in North America," while conceding that the research
"suggests, but does not prove conclusively, longer-term contact."

## Where this leaves Helluland

A fair summary in 2026:

1. Norse material **certainly** reached Inuit hands in the eastern High Arctic
   in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries. That is
   [[page:norse-things-in-inuit-houses|the Ellesmere evidence]], and it is not
   in dispute.
2. Norse voyagers **certainly** worked the waters between Greenland and Arctic
   Canada — [[artifact:norse-helluland-034|the walrus genomics]] put the ivory
   trade's hunting grounds in the North Water Polynya.
3. Norse people **plausibly** made landfall on
   [[place:Qikiqtaaluk]] more than once over four centuries. Given the 1347
   [[artifact:norse-helluland-007|Markland ship]] and the traffic north, it
   would be surprising if they never did.
4. That there was a sustained Norse presence or a trading relationship on Baffin
   Island is **not established**. The yarn evidence has been withdrawn by the
   dating. The crucible is undated. The structure is ambiguous. And there is no
   Norse midden, no Norse burial, no Norse house that anyone agrees is Norse.

Sutherland may still be right. Archaeology has overturned firmer consensuses
than this one, and the material she dug has not been fully published. But the
case as it currently stands in print does not carry the weight that has been put
on it, and the honest thing for a wiki to say is that
[[place:Helluland]] remains a name in a saga.
