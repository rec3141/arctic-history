# Helluland and the sagas: what the texts actually say

Two Icelandic sagas describe voyages from Greenland to lands in the west. They
are the whole of the written evidence for those voyages, and they are not
records. Before using them it is worth being precise about what they are.

## The manuscripts

[[source:gathornehardy1921norse|Grœnlendinga saga]], the Saga of the
Greenlanders, survives in one place only: Flateyjarbók, a large illuminated
Icelandic codex written in the 1380s, roughly four hundred years after the
events it narrates.

[[source:sephton1880eirik|Eiríks saga rauða]], the Saga of Erik the Red,
survives in two fourteenth-century manuscripts, Hauksbók and AM 557 4to,
which differ from one another.

Both were composed in Iceland, by people who had never seen North America, for
audiences interested in the ancestry of prominent Icelandic families. They are
good literature and they may well preserve real memory. They are not logs, and
nobody aboard either voyage wrote anything down.

## What the two sagas agree on

Very little, but the little is the important part.

Sailing west and then south from the Greenland settlements you come first to a
barren land of flat rock, then to a low wooded land with white sand beaches,
then further south to a land warm enough for grapes and self-sown wheat. The
Norse called them Helluland, Markland and Vinland. Wherever these lands are,
the sequence — bare, wooded, warm, going south — is the same in both texts and
it matches the actual North American coast.

Both also agree that people were already living in these lands, that they were
called *skrælingar*, that there was trade, and that the trade ended in killing.

## What they disagree on

Nearly everything else, and the disagreements are not small.

**Who found the lands.** Grœnlendinga saga gives the first sighting to
[[person:Bjarni Herjolfsson]], a trader blown off course between Iceland and
Greenland who sighted three unknown coasts and would not land, and was
criticised at the Norwegian court for his incuriosity. Eiríks saga rauða has
never heard of Bjarni. It gives the discovery to
[[person:Leif Eiriksson]], blown off course on the way back from Norway.

**Who named Helluland.** In Grœnlendinga saga,
[[artifact:norse-helluland-001|Leif names it]]: "all the intermediate land from
the sea to the glaciers was like one flat rock, and the country seemed to them
destitute of value." In Eiríks saga rauða the naming happens on
[[person:Thorfinn Karlsefni]]'s much larger expedition years later, and the
description is different —
[[artifact:norse-helluland-002|flat stones "so great that two men might well lie
on them stretched on their backs with heel to heel"]], and arctic foxes in
abundance.

**How many voyages, in what order, and who died on which.** The two accounts
cannot be harmonised without deciding that one of them is wrong. Every published
reconstruction of the Vinland voyages is a choice about which saga to believe.

## The two descriptions of Helluland

They are not the same place, or at least not obviously.

Grœnlendinga saga's Helluland is glaciated: no grass, great glaciers behind, the
foreground one sheet of rock. That describes the fiorded east coast of
[[place:Qikiqtaaluk]] very well, or the Torngats.

Eiríks saga's is not glaciated at all. It has flagstones big enough to lie on
and it has arctic foxes, and Karlsefni's party reach it in two *dœgr* from the
Bear Isles. It reads like a lower, flatter coast.

Both can be Baffin Island, which is big. That is the point: the descriptions are
loose enough that they can be almost anywhere on a two-thousand-kilometre coast.

## The identification with Baffin Island

Most scholars now place Helluland on Qikiqtaaluk, and the reasons are decent
ones. It is the first land you meet sailing west from the Greenland settlements.
Its eastern coast is exactly bare glaciated rock. The direction and the sailing
times fit. And the *Icelandic geography*, a fourteenth-century treatise,
places it in the same relative position:
[[artifact:norse-helluland-006|"South from Greenland is Helluland, next to it is
Markland, thence it is not far to Wineland the Good."]]

But this is an inference from texts, not a finding on the ground, and it has
been argued the other way by serious people:

- **G. M. Gathorne-Hardy** (1921), whose translation is the one used here,
  rejected the Labrador identification of Helluland outright and argued for
  Newfoundland, on the grounds of the sailing directions between the lands.
- **H. P. Steensby** (1918) put Karlsefni's Helluland landfall at Resolution
  Island, in the mouth of Hudson Strait.
- **Sir Wilfred Grenfell** (1934) placed it at Kangalaksiorvik Bay in northern
  Labrador, where he had seen "endless acres of flat stones".
- **Gustav Storm** put Helluland in Labrador, Markland in Newfoundland.

*Hella* means flat stone. There is a great deal of flat stone in the eastern
Arctic. The name is a description, not an address.

## Helluland's uninhabited regions

There is a second, stranger body of Helluland material that has nothing to do
with the Vinland voyages. In the *fornaldarsögur*, the legendary sagas written
in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, "Helluland's *óbygðir*" — its
uninhabited regions — is a standard setting for monsters. In *Örvar-Odds saga*
an enemy hides in Skuggifjörðr in Helluland's óbygðir; in *Hálfdanar saga
Eysteinssonar* a hero "brought Helluland's óbygðir under his sway and destroyed
all the giants there".

This tells us something real, though not what it appears to. By the time these
were written, Helluland was no longer a place anyone was going. It had become
what the far north always becomes in literature: the place where the story puts
the giants. Any claim built on these texts is a claim about Icelandic
storytelling, not about Baffin Island.

## What follows

The sagas cannot be checked, and nothing found on Baffin Island so far confirms
them. The medieval **record** — annals, letters, law — is a different and better
kind of evidence, and it survives in fragments: see
[[page:the-northern-hunting-grounds]]. What is in the ground is treated in
[[page:norse-things-in-inuit-houses]] and
