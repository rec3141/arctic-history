# The Norse in the eastern Arctic

Between about 985 and some time in the fifteenth century there were European
farmers living on the west coast of Greenland, within sight of the Davis
Strait. They were the only Europeans in the Arctic for four hundred years, and
they are the reason a saga name — *Helluland*, the land of flat stone — is
attached on modern maps to [[place:Qikiqtaaluk]] (Baffin Island).

What follows separates three kinds of thing that are often run together, because
the difference between them is the whole difficulty of the subject.

**Saga** is literature. [[source:gathornehardy1921norse|Grœnlendinga saga]] and
Eiríks saga rauða were written down in Iceland two to four centuries after the
events they describe, in a culture that valued a good story and traced family
descent through it. They are our only account of the voyages west, they
contradict each other on basic points, and they cannot be checked.

**Record** is the annals, letters and church documents: dated entries made close
to the event by people with no story to tell. There is very little of it, and
almost all of it is about Greenland rather than about the lands west.

**Archaeology** is objects in the ground. There is more of it than there used to
be, and it says something the sagas do not: that Norse things reached Inuit
hands in the Canadian Arctic, hundreds of kilometres from any Norse settlement.

## The base: Greenland

Everything Norse in this region radiates from two clusters of farms in southwest
Greenland. The [[place:Brattahlid|Eastern Settlement]], in the fjords around
modern Qaqortoq and Narsaq, was the larger, with the bishop's seat at
[[place:Gardar]] (Igaliku) from 1126. The Western Settlement, inland of modern
Nuuk, held perhaps a thousand people on some ninety-five farms. Sheep and cattle
in a place where the growing season is eight weeks: it worked, and then it did
not.

The colony's export was walrus ivory, and the walrus were not at home. They were
north, in the [[place:Nordrsetur]] — Old Norse for "the northern stations" — the
uninhabited coast beyond the Western Settlement where Greenlanders went every
summer to hunt walrus and seal and to collect driftwood. This is the fact that
matters most for anyone reading a chart of Baffin Bay: the Norse Greenlanders
were not a farming people who once made a famous voyage. They were a people
whose economy required them to sail six hundred kilometres north every year,
into the ice, for four centuries. See [[page:the-northern-hunting-grounds]].

## The northern datum

On a small island in the Upernavik Archipelago at about 73°N, three cairns were
found before 1824, and in one of them a stone bearing the names of three men:
[[artifact:norse-helluland-003|Erlingr Sighvatsson, Bjarni Thordarson and
Eindridi Oddsson]], who "erected these cairns and cleared" — as
[[source:nansen1911northern|Nansen]] translates it — on the Sunday before
*gagndagr*, Rogation Day, 25 April. The stone gives no year, and six runes at
the end of the inscription have never been securely read. On linguistic grounds
it is of about 1300 or the fourteenth century.

That is the hard northern limit of Norse presence in the Arctic — not a
theory, not a saga, a stone with names on it, in a museum in Copenhagen. Every
claim about Norsemen further north than [[place:Kingittorsuaq Island]] rests on
something softer.

## The voyages west

Both sagas agree on a sequence: sailing west and then south from Greenland you
meet first a barren land of flat rock, then a wooded land, then a land with
grapes. They named them Helluland, Markland and Vinland. They disagree on almost
everything else — [[source:gathornehardy1921norse|Grœnlendinga saga]] gives the
first sighting to [[person:Bjarni Herjolfsson]] and the naming to
[[person:Leif Eiriksson]]; [[source:sephton1880eirik|Eiríks saga rauða]] has
never heard of Bjarni and gives the naming to
[[person:Thorfinn Karlsefni]]'s expedition.

The identification of Helluland with Baffin Island is now standard, and it is
reasonable: the coast is right, the direction is right, and the arctic foxes
of [[artifact:norse-helluland-002|Eiríks saga's account]] are there. It is
also only an identification. Gathorne-Hardy, translating the sagas in 1921,
rejected Storm's Labrador and put Helluland in Newfoundland and Labrador taken
as one country, or perhaps Newfoundland alone; Hovgaard placed the landfall at
Resolution Island; Steensby put both Helluland and Markland in Labrador. See
[[page:helluland-and-the-sagas]].

The one confirmed Norse site in North America outside Greenland remains
L'Anse aux Meadows in Newfoundland, identified in 1960. Nothing comparable has
been confirmed on Baffin Island.

## After the sagas

The annals are thin and they are worth more than the sagas. In 1121 Bishop
[[artifact:norse-helluland-008|Eiríkr Gnúpsson sailed for Vinland]] and was
never heard of again. In 1285 the Annals record a New Land west of Iceland — the
manuscripts disagree whether what was sighted was land west of Iceland, the Down
Islands, or the uninhabited parts of Greenland — and the King of Norway
commissioned a man called Land-Rolf to organise an expedition that never sailed.
And in 1347 a small ship came into an Icelandic harbour, anchor gone, with
seventeen or eighteen men aboard
[[artifact:norse-helluland-007|who had been to Markland]] and were blown off
course going home to Greenland.

That last entry is the most useful sentence in the medieval record. It survives
only by accident — the ship happened to fetch up in Iceland where someone kept
annals — and it shows the Greenlanders were still crossing to North America for
timber three and a half centuries after Leif. How often, nobody knows.

## The meeting

The sagas call the people the Norse met *skrælingar*, and describe trade in red
cloth for furs that turns to killing. In Helluland this would have been the
Dorset, whose culture was ending in these centuries; further south and later,
the Thule, the ancestors of today's Inuit. What the Greenlanders themselves
remembered of the Norse, four hundred years on, is on
[[page:skraelingar-and-the-end-of-norse-greenland]] — along with what the
evidence says actually killed the colony.

The archaeology is better evidence than the sagas here, and it is genuinely
surprising. Norse chain mail, boat rivets, worked oak, iron and woollen cloth
have been excavated from thirteenth-century Inuit winter houses on
[[place:Skraeling Island]] in Alexandra Fiord, eastern Ellesmere, nearly a
thousand kilometres from the nearest Norse farm — along with
[[artifact:norse-helluland-037|an oak carpenter's plane]], which is harder to
pass off as a curiosity than a rivet is. They were found in Inuit houses, not
Norse ones, and the houses belonged to people who had themselves only recently
arrived from Alaska. See [[page:norse-things-in-inuit-houses]] for the objects
and [[page:off-alexandra-fiord]] for the sites on the ground, which are within
sight of the ship when she is in [[place:Buchanan Bay]].

Whether anything more than objects crossed — whether Norsemen wintered on
Baffin Island and traded there for generations, as
[[person:Patricia Sutherland]] has argued from the sites at
[[place:Tanfield Valley]] and Nunguvik — is the live controversy in this
field, and it is treated on its own page:
[[page:the-helluland-project-and-its-critics]].

## Why it matters here

Three reasons a scientist aboard might care.

First, the Norse were the first outsiders in this region to leave a written
description of it, and their four-century occupation of southwest Greenland
brackets the Medieval Climate Anomaly and the onset of the Little Ice Age. Their
farms are one of the better-dated proxy records of North Atlantic climate
change, and their abandonment of the Western Settlement is one of the
better-studied cases of a European society failing in an Arctic environment.

Second, the walrus. [[source:star2018ancient|Ancient DNA from ivory in medieval
European workshops]] traces most of it to Greenland animals, and
[[source:barrett2020ecological|later specimens are smaller, more often female
and from more northerly stocks]] — a palaeoecological signal of serial depletion
read out of medieval church art. Genomic sourcing published in 2024 puts
[[artifact:norse-helluland-034|the Norse hunting grounds in the North Water
Polynya, Pikialasorsuaq]]. The Norðrsetur was an industrial hunting ground, in
waters this ship works.

Third, the framing. This is a history in which Europeans arrive, meet people
already living here, take what they came for, and leave or die; and in which the
people who were already here are the continuous population, then and now. The
Dorset and Thule material discussed on these pages belongs to
[[topic:thule-dorset]]; the northwest Greenland communities whose ancestors
handled the Norse iron found on [[place:Ruin Island]] are the subject of
[[topic:greenland-inughuit]]. The Norse are an episode in their history, not the
other way round.
