# The Norðrsetur: the Greenlanders' northern hunting grounds

Strip away the sagas and there is still a Norse Arctic, and it is better
documented than the voyages west. For something like four centuries the Norse
Greenlanders sailed north every summer, past the last farm, into the ice.

They called that country the **Norðrsetur** — "the northern stations", from
*seta*, a place of residence. It was not settled. It was worked.

## Why they went

Greenland's export was ivory. Walrus tusk was the raw material of medieval
European luxury — chess pieces, croziers, reliquaries — and the walrus herds
were north of the settlements, on the banks off Disko Bay and beyond. With the
ivory came walrus hide rope, seal oil, bear skins, narwhal tusk, and the live
gyrfalcons that were worth a king's ransom. And driftwood: Greenland has no
trees to speak of, and the Norðrsetur coasts catch the drift.

The seventeenth-century Icelandic compiler Björn Jónsson, working from texts now
lost, preserved the best description of the operation:

> [[artifact:norse-helluland-004|All the great franklins of Greenland had large
> ships and vessels built to send to the Norðrsetur for seal-hunting, with all
> kinds of sealing gear and cut-up wood ... melted seal-fat was poured into
> sacks of hide and hung up against the wind on boards, till it thickened.]]

That is an industry, not an adventure: purpose-built ships, standing camps, a
processing method for the product. The hunters kept "booths or houses" at two
named places, **Greipar** and **Króksfjarðarheiðr**.

## Where it was

Nobody knows exactly, and this matters, because the answer decides whether the
Norse were routinely crossing to the Canadian side.

[[person:Fridtjof Nansen]], writing in 1911, put the Norðrsetur on the Greenland
coast around and south of Disko Bay, identifying Króksfjörðr with the Vaigat —
the channel between Disko Island and the Nuussuaq peninsula, at about 70½°N —
and Greipar somewhere near 67°N. His argument against the more expansive
readings is worth having, because it is the argument from seamanship:

- Norse ships were not built for the ice of Baffin Bay and Melville Bay, and a
  fleet crossing and recrossing that every year would have left casualties in
  the record.
- Driftwood, one of the objects of the hunt, comes ashore on the Greenland side.
  You do not cross an ice-choked gulf to collect wood that is already at your
  feet.
- The walrus were on the Greenland banks.

Later writers have put Króksfjarðarheiðr further south still; a minority have
argued for Jones Sound, on the Canadian side. Nansen calls that an absurdity —
"separating these two places by the whole of Baffin's Bay". The honest position
is that the two names cannot be fixed, and that the routine hunt was almost
certainly a Greenland-coast operation.

## That it was a legal jurisdiction

The most solid piece of evidence for the Norðrsetur is not a story at all. When
the Greenlanders submitted to the Norwegian crown around 1261, the terms
included a clause about killings:

> [[artifact:norse-helluland-016|for all manslaughter, whether of Norsemen or
> Greenlanders, and whether they were killed in the settlements or in
> Norðrsetur, and in all the district to the north under the star, the king
> should have his weregild.]]

Legal language does not romance. It tells us that the Norðrsetur were a defined
area everyone could name, that people were killed there often enough for the
liability to need settling in advance, and that royal authority was understood
to run as far north as anyone went.

## The northern limit

At about 73°N, on [[place:Kingittorsuaq Island]] in the Upernavik Archipelago,
three cairns stood on the summit of a small island. In one of them, found before
1824, was a stone bearing
[[artifact:norse-helluland-003|the names of three men]]:
[[person:Erlingr Sighvatsson]], [[person:Bjarni Thordarson]] and
[[person:Eindridi Oddsson]], who raised the cairns "the Saturday before Rogation
Day". No year. Dated by its language to about 1300.

This is the northernmost Norse inscription in the world and the hardest fact in
this topic. Nansen worried at it: why three cairns? Why in April, when that sea
is ice? He concluded the men most likely wintered there — in which case, he
adds, they were probably starving, and the cairns were meant to draw a future
traveller's eye to their bodies. Alternatively they came north over the ice that
spring, by dog sledge, in which case they had learnt the technique from Inuit,
"whom we know to have lived on Disco Bay ... at that time."

Either reading has the same implication. Three Norsemen at 73° north in about
1300 were operating at or past the edge of what their own technology allowed,
and if they managed it they probably did so with Inuit methods.

## The voyage further north

Once, at least, they went deliberately looking. About 1267 the priests in
Greenland
[[artifact:norse-helluland-005|sent a ship north from Króksfjarðarheiðr]] to
find out what lay beyond the furthest point already reached. Driven north by a
gale, they came into open water and saw islands, seals, whales, and "a great
number of bears", and found old Dorset dwelling sites they could not land at.
They were back at Króksfjarðarheiðr on St James's day, 25 July.

Nansen works their furthest north out from the sailing times at about 74°N, near
Melville Bay. He does not trust the details — the account says they could not
land for the bears yet describes the ruins ashore — but he does not doubt the
voyage happened, and neither should we: it reached us as a letter from a priest
named Halldór to another priest, which is the shape real news takes.

Its purpose is the interesting part. The year before, men had come back from the
Norðrsetur having gone further north than anyone had heard of, and had reported
seeing no Skræling dwellings except at Króksfjarðarheiðr. The clerics' voyage
looks like a follow-up: an attempt to find out how far north these people
extended, and where they came from. In other words, the one Norse voyage of
exploration we can reconstruct in Baffin Bay was launched to answer a question
about Inuit — which is also, several centuries later, a fair description of
[[topic:thule-dorset]] and [[topic:greenland-inughuit]].

## What became of it

The last dated evidence of the western trade is
[[artifact:norse-helluland-007|the small ship that came into Iceland in 1347]]
with seventeen or eighteen men who had been to Markland. After that, silence:
the Western Settlement empty by about 1360, a wedding at
[[place:Hvalsey]] in 1408, and nothing.

Modern isotope and ancient-DNA work on walrus ivory in medieval European
collections has traced the great majority of it to Greenland animals, and shown
the hunters working further north and taking smaller and more often female
animals as the centuries pass. Read that alongside the Norðrsetur texts and it
describes a familiar Arctic story: a distant market, a single-commodity economy,
a resource retreating northward, and a settlement that could not follow it.
