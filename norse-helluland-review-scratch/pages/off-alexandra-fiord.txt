# Off Alexandra Fiord: what is on the ground, and who dug it

If the ship is anywhere in Buchanan Bay, the archaeology is within sight. This
page is meant to be usable from the bridge: where the sites are, what is in
them, and whose work you are looking at.

## Where things are

[[place:Skraeling Island]] lies at the mouth of [[place:Alexandra Fiord]], about
5 km northeast of the abandoned RCMP station on the fiord's south shore. It is a
small island, roughly 2 by 1.4 km, ridged with raised gravel beaches. The
archaeological site — SfFk-4 in the Borden system — is on the island's southern
extension: twenty-three Thule winter house ruins, seventeen of them in five
clusters and six scattered, with tent rings, kayak and umiaq supports and food
caches among them. All twenty-three have been at least partly excavated. Higher
up the island, at 29 to 30 m above sea level, is SfFk-6, an Arctic Small Tool
tradition site four thousand years older, where a partly buried walrus tusk
dated 5390 plus or minus 380 BP, about 3440 BC, though the excavator says its
association with the stone tools there can only be inferred.

North and west of the fiord, [[place:Bache Peninsula]] and
[[place:Knud Peninsula]] carry more. On Knud Peninsula there is a Late Dorset
longhouse 45 m long and 5 m wide, with lines of joined stone hearths beside it —
one unbroken run of them 32 m long, of eighteen units — where whole communities
seem to have cooked together and eaten inside the enclosure. There is the Thule
winter site of Eskimobyen. Further west, in the Flagler Bay area, is
[[place:Haa Island]]. Across Smith Sound on the Greenland side is
[[place:Ruin Island]], which gave the whole cultural phase its name.

The scale is easy to underestimate. A season of helicopter reconnaissance in
1978, from Wade Point on Smith Sound to Norman Lockyer Island in Princess Marie
Bay, located 125 new sites. The count of Thule winter dwellings alone in the
Bache Peninsula region passed two hundred.

## Why here

Polynyas. Skraeling Island sits on the western edge of the North Water, just
south of the Flagler Bay polynyas, near several smaller and less reliable
openings. Another polynya forms between Knud and Bache Peninsulas and, on
satellite imagery, begins to open in late April or early May. When
[[person:Peter Schledermann]]'s party arrived on Knud Peninsula in early July
1978 they counted about three hundred walrus distributed across eighteen ice
floes in it.

That is the whole explanation for the density of sites. Ringed and bearded seal
and polar bear are there year round; beluga, narwhal, bowhead and walrus come
in summer; the North Water carries the largest concentration of seabirds in the
Arctic. This coast is one of the few places in the High Arctic where a family
could live through a winter without moving.

## Who dug it

- **1875.** [[person:George Strong Nares]]'s expedition notes seasonal camp
  remains in passing.
- **1899-1900.** [[person:Otto Sverdrup]]'s men observe and name the winter
  house ruins on Knud Peninsula, and dig into one or two. The site is still
  called Eskimobyen because of them. See [[topic:sverdrup-bernier-peary]].
- **1924.** The RCMP establish a detachment on Bache Peninsula; its members
  record further sites.
- **1939.** T. C. Lethbridge spends a few days excavating and mapping
  Eskimopolis, the site Sverdrup had first noted.
- **1930s-40s.** [[person:Erik Holtved]] digs the Thule winter houses on the
  northwest Greenland coast, including Ruin Island, and names the Ruin Island
  phase — the earliest known Inuit occupation of the region.
- **1977 onward.** Peter Schledermann of the Arctic Institute of North America,
  with [[person:Karen M. McCullough]], surveys and then excavates the whole
  region, in seasons that ran into the 1990s. W. Blake Jr. of the Geological Survey of Canada runs the radiocarbon
  programme, deliberately dating several different materials from single
  structures to measure how far they disagree.
- **2013.** [[person:Lesley Howse]] returns to the animal bone from
  [[artifact:norse-helluland-042|House 15]] and reads it against Inuit oral
  history and ethnography.

## Who the Ruin Islanders were

They were newcomers, and their belongings say where from. The house plan puts a
separate kitchen at the end of its own tunnel running parallel to the entrance
passage — a Bering Sea arrangement.
[[artifact:norse-helluland-040|Clay pottery]] from the site carries the stamped
curvilinear paddle marks of Barrow Curvilinear Paddled ware, made on the Bering
Sea coast. There is [[artifact:norse-helluland-041|an ivory brow band]] of a
kind common at Cape Denbigh on Norton Sound.
[[artifact:norse-helluland-038|The decorated ivory needle case]] found within a
few centimetres of the chain mail in House 6 matches specimens from Nunagiak and
from a Tigara burial at Point Hope, and lacks the flaring wings of the eastern
Arctic style entirely.

[[source:schledermann1980western|Schledermann and McCullough argued in 1980]]
that these people had come the whole way from Alaska, and that they arrived
about AD 1050 to 1100. The first half of that has held up. The second half has
not: the redating of the Thule migration since the 2000s puts the arrival
around AD 1200 or later, and the Ruin Island phase is now normally given as
1200 to 1300. Their own evidence pointed that way, had they trusted it — the
Sicco harpoon heads they used to argue for an early arrival had no radiocarbon
date earlier than the twelfth century.

The people themselves, and the Dorset who were here before them, belong to
[[topic:thule-dorset]]; their descendants across Smith Sound are
[[topic:greenland-inughuit]].

## The Norse material

That is the point of this topic, and it has its own page:
[[page:norse-things-in-inuit-houses]]. In short: chain mail, boat rivets, an oak
carpenter's plane, iron knife blades hafted in wood and musk-ox horn, copper,
two pieces of woven woollen cloth, barrel bottoms and a piece of an oak box —
all of it out of Inuit houses, none of it out of a Norse structure, because
there is no Norse structure here.
