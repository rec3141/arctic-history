# Norse things in Inuit houses: the Ellesmere archaeology
[page · norse-helluland · draft]

# Norse things in Inuit houses

The best evidence that the Norse and the people of the eastern Arctic met is not
in the sagas. It is in a set of Thule winter houses on a small island off
Ellesmere, nearly a thousand kilometres from the nearest Norse farm, and it was
dug up from 1978 onward. For the sites themselves — where they are, why they are
there, and who worked on them — see [[page:off-alexandra-fiord]].

## What was found

[[person:Peter Schledermann]] of the Arctic Institute of North America worked
the Bache Peninsula region of eastern Ellesmere Island, and published his first
account of the European material in
[[source:schledermann1980notes|*Arctic* in 1980]]. On
[[place:Skraeling Island]], in Alexandra Fiord, he recovered from Thule house
ruins:

- [[artifact:norse-helluland-023|A section of iron chain mail]], 25–30 cm below
  the top sods of house 6, on the gravel floor by the passage to a kitchen room,
  beside a stone-lined meat pit. Oak charcoal from that kitchen: GX 6069,
  670 ± 110 BP, about AD 1280. Two further single mail links, from house 10
  (willow from the floor: GSC 2938, 760 ± 60 BP, about AD 1190) and house 2.
- [[artifact:norse-helluland-024|Iron boat rivets]] — clinch nails from a
  clinker-built hull — in houses 6, 10 and 4, identified by the square central
  holes their radiographs show.
- [[artifact:norse-helluland-044|Iron knife blades]], one set in a wooden
  handle and one in a handle of musk-ox horn, each still hafted the way an Inuit
  knife is hafted.
- Copper strip and band, analysed at the Canadian Conservation Institute as
  copper with silver and traces of iron and antimony.
- [[artifact:norse-helluland-025|Woven woollen cloth]], 700 ± 50 BP, two pieces,
  from [[artifact:norse-helluland-042|house 15]].
- [[artifact:norse-helluland-037|An oak carpenter's plane]] — the hardest object
  in the assemblage to explain away, and the one this topic knows least about,
  because it was found after the 1980 report and published in a monograph no
  digital copy of exists.

The 1980 season, reported in a three-line addendum added while that paper was in
press, added [[artifact:norse-helluland-043|more rivets, a small woven woollen
bag, an iron spike, a knife blade, many iron pieces and a copper end blade]].
[[artifact:norse-helluland-039|House 6 itself]] is worth looking at as a plan:
gravel floor, entrance passage, and a separate kitchen reached by its own tunnel
running parallel to the entrance, with the mail on the floor beside it and a
boat rivet near the bottom of the meat pit.

At the Eskimobyen site on the Knud Peninsula,
[[artifact:norse-helluland-026|two barrel bottoms joined together and decorated
with incised concentric circles]], and an oak box fragment with wooden dowels
dated about AD 1390. On Haa Island,
[[artifact:norse-helluland-027|a 27 mm ivory figurine]] said to represent a
Norseman.

Similar material has turned up elsewhere in the High Arctic, and the contrast in
quantity is the point. [[person:Erik Holtved]] found chain mail and woollen
cloth on [[place:Ruin Island]] in northwest Greenland in the 1930s — the cloth
dated 680 ± 100 BP, remarkably close to the Skraeling Island piece. Everywhere
else it is ones and twos:
[[artifact:norse-helluland-046|a section of a small folding balance from the
west coast of Ellesmere, and bronze fragments from Thule houses on Bathurst,
Devon and Cornwallis Islands]]. And far to the northeast, in Peary Land, Eigil
Knuth found [[artifact:norse-helluland-045|an umiaq frame with one piece of oak
in its gunwale]], which he read as a meeting with Europeans within the previous
three hundred years and which radiocarbon later moved back to about AD 1220.

Norse goods reached the central High Arctic in scraps. They reached Smith Sound
in quantity.

## The three things this material does not say

**It does not say Norsemen lived there.** Every object was found in an Inuit
house, in an Inuit assemblage, at an Inuit site. There is no Norse structure, no
Norse midden, no Norse burial anywhere in Arctic Canada. What was found is
foreign material inside somebody else's household — and the household was itself
new to the country. The people of the Ruin Island phase had arrived from Alaska,
probably within a generation or two: their houses have the Bering Sea kitchen
offshoot, their pots are [[artifact:norse-helluland-040|Barrow Curvilinear
Paddled ware]], they carried [[artifact:norse-helluland-041|ivory brow bands]] of
a Norton Sound kind, and [[artifact:norse-helluland-038|the ivory needle case
lying a few centimetres from the chain mail in house 6]] is as Alaskan as an
object can be. Whatever happened in Smith Sound in the thirteenth century
happened between Norse Greenlanders and recent immigrants from the Bering Sea,
each about as far from home as the other.

**It does not say the Norse brought metal to the Arctic.** This is the finding
that ought to have changed the conversation and largely has not.
[[artifact:norse-helluland-028|Microprobe analysis of the Skraeling Island
iron]] found two populations: terrestrial iron at 0.06–0.07% nickel, and two
pieces at 6.6% and 7.0% — meteoric iron from the Cape York meteorites of
northwest Greenland, which Inughuit had been quarrying and cold-hammering for
centuries before any Norseman saw Greenland. The Norse iron went into houses
that already had a metal industry. In house 1, an iron point had been made by
reworking a ship rivet, and the radiograph still shows the rivet's square hole.
That is not a people receiving technology; that is a people using a raw material
that turned up.

**It does not say how the objects got there.** Schledermann puts the question
plainly and refuses to answer it beyond the evidence: did the Norse material
arrive through direct trade between resident Inuit and Norse voyagers, or
through trade between Inuit groups along the west Greenland coast and across
Smith Sound? "Based on the present evidence it seems possible that both events
took place, although more supportive evidence must be obtained in order to
confirm the first contention." Forty-five years later that is still where the
evidence stands.

A wrecked or abandoned ship is a fourth possibility that would produce exactly
this signature: rivets, mail, cloth, barrel staves, oak — the contents and
fabric of a vessel, dispersed and reused. Nobody has found the ship.

## Dating things carefully

Schledermann's report is worth reading for its handling of dates alone, because
this is where claims about Norse contact usually go wrong.

A radiocarbon date on woollen cloth dates the shearing of the sheep. A date on
oak dates the felling of the tree. Neither dates the arrival of the object in
the Arctic, and the gap can be long. Willow from a house floor dates older than
heather from its sleeping platform in the same house, because people gathered
old dead willow for the hearth and fresh heather for bedding. Schledermann had
W. Blake Jr. of the Geological Survey of Canada run a comparison of different
dated materials within single structures precisely to measure this.

The Haa Island figurine is the case in point. It looks like the perfect find: a
carving of a Norseman, in the High Arctic. Its house dates to about AD 1680 —
almost three centuries after the Norse Greenland colony ended, and squarely in
the period when European whalers and traders were back in Davis Strait.
Schledermann says so himself, in the paper that publishes it.

## What it adds up to

Between roughly 1200 and 1400 — the same centuries as the
[[page:the-northern-hunting-grounds|Norðrsetur hunt]], the
[[artifact:norse-helluland-005|clerics' voyage of about 1267]] and the
[[artifact:norse-helluland-003|Kingittorsuaq runestone]] — European iron,
cloth, wood and copper were moving through Inuit hands in the Smith Sound
region. The medieval Norse record says the Greenlanders were going north and
meeting people up there. The archaeology says objects crossed. Neither says how
often, or on whose initiative, or whether anyone stayed.

That is a real and interesting result, and it is smaller than the headlines it
usually generates. For the claim that goes considerably further — that Norse
voyagers were present on Baffin Island over a long period and traded there with
the Dorset — see [[page:the-helluland-project-and-its-critics]].

The people whose houses these were are the subject of [[topic:thule-dorset]];
their descendants in northwest Greenland, whose ancestors worked the Cape York
meteorite iron, are [[topic:greenland-inughuit]].
