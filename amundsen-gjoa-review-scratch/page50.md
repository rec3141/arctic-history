The [[vessel:Gjoa|*Gjøa*]] spent nearly two years at [[place:Uqsuqtuuq]] and
thirteen days completing the Northwest Passage. The thirteen days were the worst
of the voyage. [[person:Roald Amundsen]] could not eat through most of them.

## Leaving, 13 August 1905

"Precisely at 3 a.m. on August 13th, 1905, the windlass played a lively tune on
the deck of the 'Gjøa'." The weather was thick fog and a light contrary breeze.
[[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-072|The ship had lain here all summer]].

Amundsen wanted a Netsilingmiut aboard for the rest of the voyage.
[[person:Talurnakto]] had been offered
the eighth place and could not, in the end, face leaving his country, so
[[person:Tonnich]] applied for it and got it; on coming aboard he was scrubbed
on deck under [[person:Gustav Juel Wiik]]'s supervision, clipped, dusted with
insect powder and dressed in a brand-new frieze suit made in a Norwegian prison,
and Amundsen says no one was ever prouder of their clothes.

At two the next morning the ship was abreast of Douglas Bay, and she put in to
the camp at Kamiglu, where
[[person:Manni]] — Maniracha, not over seventeen — was standing outside a tent
crying, because he had asked months before to come and had just learned that
Tonnich had the place. Amundsen took him aboard "for the present", then put the
question to Tonnich, who by now had lived a few days like a prince and answered
with "surprising frankness" that no, he had no wish to go at all. His
foster-father [[person:Umiktuallu]] came aboard and wanted paying for Manni, and
was satisfied with a file and an old knife — a transaction Amundsen records with
amusement and which reads very differently now. They then took "a last cordial
farewell" of [[person:Ugpi]], the Owl, and their other friends among the
Netsilingmiut, and set off.

## Navigating without a compass

The problem in these waters is the thing the expedition had come to study. This
close to the North Magnetic Pole a magnetic compass is useless, and the charts
were either blank or wrong. So the *Gjøa* worked west the way a small ship works
an uncharted coast: two lookouts aloft, a man on the lead without a pause, and
the captain or [[person:Godfred Hansen]] at the helm where they could see
furthest.

> "The man attending to the soundings had his full share of work; the lead flew
> up and down so rapidly that it was almost a wonder it did not melt. Ten
> fathoms and a clay bottom, was the report; then again, eight fathoms, stone;
> ten fathoms and clay." (vol. II, p. 107)

[[person:Anton Lund]] and [[person:Helmer Hanssen]] took the crow's nest, being
the best qualified; [[person:Peder Ristvedt]] and Wiik had the engine. Watches
ran eighteen hours a day.

## Eta Strait

[[place:Simpson Strait]] divides around Eta Island. Godfred Hansen and Helmer
Hanssen had established by sledge, the previous spring, that the northern
channel was impassable for the ship, so everything depended on the southern one:
not more than three-quarters of a mile wide, and known to be foul. "We had long
talked about this passage and shuddered at the thought of it."

They took it on a flat calm, broiling summer day. The lead was used continually
and the helm went from board to board the whole time, "just as if we were in
thick ice". The shallowest water they found was three fathoms, and the *Gjøa*
drew ten feet loaded. "I can vouch for it, as it was my turn at the helm, that
the passage did not, by any means, resemble a bee-line." Amundsen does not name the
date, but he counts the nights: the ship was abreast of Douglas Bay at two in
the morning after leaving, the fog lifted at seven, and the strait was the
morning watch of 14 August.

## The Royal Geographical Society Islands, 15 August

At daybreak on 15 August a group of islands stood across the whole horizon north
to south, with ice packed all round them. This was the group John Rae had
sighted in Victoria Strait half a century before, which Godfred Hansen and
Ristvedt had worked over, charted and named the
[[place:Royal Geographical Society Islands]] only three months earlier on their
sledge survey out of Gjøahavn — a chart, Amundsen says, that proved of great
importance to the ship's navigation now. Knowing where the islands were did not
get him past them. There was no way round to north or south, so they picked the
weakest point in the ice belt, went full speed ahead with everybody holding on,
and took the thumps.

West of the islands the bottom turned ragged and stony and the soundings jumped
from seventeen fathoms to five and back. "We bungled through zigzag, as if we
were drunk," with the lookout who "jumped about in the crow's nest like a
maniac, throwing his arms about for starboard and port". Amundsen anchored on
17 August at five in the morning on the west side of Cape Colborne, and
afterwards wrote that his hours of rest in this stretch were spent brooding, and
that at every meal he felt a devouring hunger and could not swallow.

Then the water opened, the watches went back to six hours, and they made fair
westing.

## Nelson Head, 26 August 1905

At four in the afternoon on 26 August a high land came up to windward through
mist. By dead reckoning they should have been off Cape Parry on the mainland, so
Amundsen took it for Cape Parry. When the air cleared in the early morning it
was [[place:Nelson Head]], the southern cape of Banks Island, and he had been
wrong by a long way. He is candid about it, and adds the explanation American
whalers later gave him: there is probably a lot of iron in these mountains, the
compass becomes "utterly distracted", and strong currents finish the job.

His watch ended at eight and he turned in. Some time later he half woke to
running feet overhead and was annoyed, assuming a bear or a seal.

> [[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-029|"Lieutenant Hansen came rushing down into the
> cabin and called out the ever memorable words: 'Vessel in sight, sir!' … The
> North West Passage had been accomplished — my dream from childhood. This very
> moment it was fulfilled. I had a peculiar sensation in my throat … I could
> feel tears coming to my eyes."]]

The ship was the San Francisco whaling schooner
[[vessel:Charles Hanson|*Charles Hanson*]], Captain James McKenna, working east
out of the Pacific. A vessel coming from the west was the proof: there was
continuous navigable water from the Atlantic to the Pacific, and the *Gjøa* had
sailed it. [[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-066|The meeting is the frontispiece of
volume II]].

> [[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-030|"'Are you Captain Amundsen?' was his first
> remark. … 'Is this the first vessel you have met?' the old man asked. And when
> I admitted it was so, his countenance brightened up. We shook hands long and
> heartily. 'I am exceedingly pleased to be the first one to welcome you on
> getting through the North West Passage.'"]]

**A note on the date.** The events are entered here on 26 August 1905, the date
Amundsen and his crew kept and the one the literature uses. Read closely, his
own narrative puts the sighting of Nelson Head at 4 p.m. on the 26th and the
meeting after 8 a.m. the *following* morning — which would make it the 27th.
This history follows his date and flags the discrepancy rather than silently
correcting a man's account of the most important morning of his life.

## What "first" means

Others had established that the passage existed. [[topic:franklin-1845|Franklin]]
proved the last link at the cost of his ships and 129 men;
[[topic:mcclure-investigator|McClure]] crossed from west to east in 1850–54 but
abandoned the *Investigator* and finished by sledge and in another ship;
[[topic:franklin-search|the searchers]] filled in the coasts. What the *Gjøa*
did that nobody had done was carry one hull the whole way. She could do it
because she was small enough to use three fathoms of water and shallow channels
no naval ship could enter — and because her crew had spent two winters learning
how to live there.

The passage was made on 26 August 1905. The *Gjøa* herself did not enter the
Pacific for another year, and Amundsen marked that second moment too, off Cape
Prince of Wales in August 1906, with a toast and no flag, because the wind would
have torn it to tatters: [[page:the-third-winter-at-king-point]].
