The [[vessel:Gjoa|*Gjøa*]] expedition is remembered for the passage. What it
actually carried away from [[place:Uqsuqtuuq]] was an education, given over two
winters by the Netsilingmiut — the Nattilingmiut, the people of the place where
there is seal — and it is the reason a Norwegian party reached the South Pole in
December 1911 and came back, while a British party did not.

[[person:Roald Amundsen]] says as much himself, in the most direct way
available: he wore Netsilik dress continuously for twenty months, only
discarding it in the middle of July 1906, and gave Part II of volume II of
[[source:amundsen1908northwest]] to the people who made it.

## Clothing

The expedition came north with skin clothing made in Norway and Siberia, and
Amundsen writes that they would have been "crippled in a single day" if they had
worn it with the seams next to the skin, so thick and coarse were they. Netsilik
women sewed the skins edge to edge rather than overlapping, which leaves no seam
ridge at all. Two anoraks, one with the hair in and one with the hair out; two
pairs of trousers the same way; both loose so the air can move. In the hardest
cold he took to a broad belt of reindeer or wolf skin round the abdomen, as the
Netsilingmiut did.

> [[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-032|"My experience is that the Eskimo dress in winter
> in these regions is far superior to our European clothes. But one must either
> wear it all or not at all; any mixture is bad."]]

That rule — all or nothing, no wool against the skin, nothing that traps sweat —
is a direct transfer from [[person:Nalungia]], [[person:Navya]],
[[person:Kabloka]] and the other women who cut and sewed, to the Antarctic
plateau seven years later. What it looked like in wear is on the plates:
[[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-052|Talurnakto standing full length in caribou skin]],
and [[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-069|a deck full of people in summer, 1904]].

The clothing lesson came with a second lesson about how to learn. The crew
thought the fringes on their new anoraks looked absurd on grown men and cut them
off.

> [[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-034|"But I had my scruples about this, as I had
> already had evidence that nothing either in the Eskimo's clothing or other
> arrangements was, in fact, without meaning or purpose, so I kept my fringe and
> put up with the ridicule."]]

The trimmed anoraks curled up "like neckties" and the fringes went back on. The
fringe breaks up airflow at the hem and keeps the skin from rolling. Amundsen's
working assumption — that a design he does not understand is one he has not
understood *yet* — is worth more than any single technique he took away.

There is a wry footnote in his account: a Netsilik woman sewing for her own
family sews fine and small, but sewing to order for a *kabluna* — his spelling
for a white man — she used "long coarse stitches that will not hold for a day.
Consequently, when we want serviceable clothes, we buy them second hand from the
wearer."

## The snow house

Amundsen's fullest technical description of anything Netsilik is of
[[person:Atikleura]] building a snow house, and it reads like an engineering
report because that is what it is. Site first: sheltered north, east and west by
the ridge behind, open south to the sun, a pond close by for water, and the
right snow. Then the shovel — a wooden board, bartered up from the south,
because there is no wood at all in Nattilik and no driftwood reaches it —
reinforced at the edge with caribou bone. Then the long snow knife.

Blocks about eighteen inches by twenty-four by four, cut from inside the ring so
that the floor drops as the walls rise and the site yields its own material.

> [[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-033|"Just a cut here and there, then a kick, and the
> thin neat block stands separated from the mass of snow. All the blocks from
> Atikleura's hand are so exactly equal in size that they look as if they had
> been accurately measured."]]

The wall is a continuous spiral, each course leaning further in, so that the
last blocks appear to hang unsupported; the small triangular keystone has to be
passed out through the hole it will fill and set from outside. Amundsen tried
it: "Many a snow-block did I get on my head when I essayed this work."

It is a two-person build. [[person:Nalungia]] works the loose shovelled snow
until it is "as fine as grated sugar" and throws it against each block as it is
set, chinking every crevice from outside while Atikleura sets from within. When
his wife wanted more light he went down to the frozen lake, cut an oblong slab
of ice and set it over the door — "the most elegant window imaginable". The two
of them stand together outside their skin tent in
[[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-060|one of the few plates in the book that names its
sitters]]; a whole camp of the finished houses is
[[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-056|photographed by moonlight]], and another group
stands [[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-058|in the valley behind the harbour]].
[[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-050|Inside one of them]], lamps lit, is a family at
home.

The *Gjøa* expedition put that straight to work. In the second winter at
Uqsuqtuuq, Amundsen's own second magnetic observatory was
[[page:two-winters-at-uqsuqtuuq|a large igloo]] with ice windows in every wall
and the 66 lb instrument standing on a snow socket that did not shift by a
measurable amount all spring. Netsilik building technique, holding a
geophysical instrument steady.

## Dogs, meat and the seal hole

The dogs came from Greenland, but the driving came from Uqsuqtuuq.
[[person:Helmer Hanssen]] learned it there, and was Amundsen's lead driver at
the South Pole — one of the two men, with Amundsen himself, who both went
through the Northwest Passage and stood at the pole.

Sealing was the other technique, and Amundsen describes it minutely: the harpoon
with its toggling head, the plaited sinew line, the *ilia* set in the breathing
hole to signal the seal's approach, the two small wooden rests for the spear,
and the trick of pulling the left arm out of its sleeve and holding both hands
inside the coat while waiting, motionless, over the hole.
[[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-061|The tools themselves]] were collected and went to
the museum in Oslo.

And the food. [[person:Adolf Henrik Lindstrom]] cooked fresh reindeer, seal,
ptarmigan and fish through three Arctic winters, with bread baked all winter,
and the *Gjøa* did not have a single case of scurvy. Amundsen had stood on
[[place:Beechey Island]] in August 1903 looking at Franklin's graves and writing
that there "the spectre of scurvy showed itself for the first time" in
[[topic:franklin-1845|Franklin's]] expedition. The contrast was not lost on him.
Fresh meat, in quantity, all winter, was Netsilik and Inuit practice long before
it was understood as vitamin C, and it is the single largest difference between
the expeditions that came home and the ones that did not. See also
[[topic:inuit-oral-history]].

## The people, and what the exchange cost

Amundsen's narrative and index preserve the names of nearly twenty
Netsilingmiut, and the ones the expedition knew best are the ones the
photographs show.
[[person:Ugpi]] — Uglen, the Owl — worked on the magnetic and surveying journeys
from June 1904 and became, Amundsen says, an exceptionally clever hunter;
[[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-051|he is the archer on the plate]], about thirty years
old and married to [[person:Kabloka]], who was seventeen.
[[person:Talurnakto]] kept returning to the ship after being told he was not
needed until they took him on; among his own people, Amundsen writes, "he was
regarded as somewhat of a fool, but in reality was the cleverest of them all",
and his other name was Takichya, he who walks heavily.
[[person:Umiktuallu]] was Ugpi's elder brother and head of the family, a clever
seal catcher married to [[person:Onallu]];
[[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-055|he, Onallu, Kabloka and Ugpi's mother Anana are
lettered by name on the plate of a sledge visit to the ship]].
[[person:Teraiu]] and [[person:Kachkochnelli]] were the seal hunters Amundsen
travelled with in April 1904. [[person:Praederik]] was an angakkuq, and Amundsen
describes his work as conjuring and sorcery because that is what a European in
1908 made of it — [[source:rasmussen1931netsilik|Rasmussen's 1931 Netsilik
volume]] is the fuller record of the same practice, and
[[person:Knud Rasmussen]] had met Amundsen aboard the *Gjøa* in Greenland in
1903. [[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-067|Praederik and his wife sit in their snow
house]] by lamplight.

The exchange was not one-sided and not free of friction, and Amundsen records
both. He traded rifles, knives, sewing needles and tins; a Boothia depot of his
was stripped in May 1904 and a sledge taken, and returned a year later; two
Ogluli men who took a tin each from the ship were banned from it permanently,
and Amundsen notes that none of the Netsilingmiut had taken anything. He fed
families through a winter and slept thirteen guests on his cabin floor.
[[person:Manni]] — Maniracha, not over seventeen — joined the ship as a member
of the expedition in August 1905 after his foster-father Umiktuallu was paid a
file and an old knife for him, a transaction that reads very differently now.
[[person:Godfred Hansen]] taught him to read and to tell the time through the
third winter, a lesson at a time, and Manni drowned shooting ducks at
[[place:Qikiqtaruk]] in the summer of 1906.

Amundsen also published, as
[[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-053|a captioned plate of a scene in a snow house]],
his account of Umiktuallu killing an earlier foster-son. That is his telling,
made by a visitor in 1904 and printed in London in 1908; it is not the
community's own account of what happened, and it should be read as what it is.

Amundsen's closing verdict on the people who taught him is admiring and
paternalistic in the same breath:

> [[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-035|"My sincerest wish for our friends the Nechilli
> Eskimo is, that civilisation may never reach them."]]

He wrote it a page after calling them "children of Nature up at the Pole", and
after arguing that colonial governments had a duty to protect Inuit from the
harms that came with contact. He was right about the harms and wrong about the
people: the Netsilingmiut were not a museum specimen to be sealed off, and the
century that followed — the whaling stations, the trading posts, the
[[topic:posts-rcmp-missions|police posts and missions]], the tuberculosis
evacuations and the [[topic:relocations-qtc|relocations]] documented by the
Qikiqtani Truth Commission — happened to a people who kept adapting, and who
still live at [[place:Uqsuqtuuq]]. The
[[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-025|Nattilik Heritage Centre]], opened in the hamlet in
2013, holds their side of this history.

And the knowledge kept flowing outward. It was Inuit oral testimony — gathered
by Rae in 1854, by Hall and Schwatka, by Amundsen from [[person:Uchyuneiu]] in
1905, and above all by Louie Kamookak of Gjoa Haven over a lifetime — that put
searchers in the right water and found both
[[topic:wrecks-disasters|Franklin wrecks]] in 2014 and 2016. Two expeditions
came to King William Island. One would not take instruction and left 129 dead.
The other took instruction, lost one man to illness on the Yukon coast, and won
the South Pole with what it had been taught here.
