On Saturday 12 September 1903 the north wind dropped enough for the
[[vessel:Gjoa|*Gjøa*]] to work in under her motor, and at half past eight in the
evening she anchored in a small, almost landlocked harbour on the south-east
coast of King William Island. [[place:Simpson Strait]] lay open to the west and
the Northwest Passage was, that afternoon, there for the taking.
[[person:Roald Amundsen]] stayed for one month short of two years.

The place is [[place:Uqsuqtuuq]] — "lots of fat", for the seal oil — and the
Netsilingmiut had been coming to it long before the *Gjøa*. Amundsen named the
anchorage Gjøahavn after his ship, and the hamlet is Gjoa Haven in English to
this day. [[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-046|The ship lay here through the open water
of 1904]] and [[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-072|through the summer of 1905]].

## Why he stopped

The expedition's stated object was not the passage. It was to re-observe the
North Magnetic Pole, which [[topic:ross-victory|James Clark Ross]] had fixed at
Cape Adelaide on the west coast of [[place:Boothia Peninsula]] on 1 June 1831,
and to find out how far and how fast it had shifted in seventy years. That
question needed a fixed station running for months, not a ship dashing past. The
pole was some ninety miles off; the harbour was sheltered, had fresh water, and
sat on ground where reindeer and fish could be got. Amundsen chose it and let
the passage wait.

## The station

The buildings went up fast. Amundsen and [[person:Godfred Hansen]] dug the
trenches for the magnetic variation house themselves — "more than once we
stretched our aching backs, unaccustomed to this kind of work, and wished
trench-digging to the deuce" — and the observatory was ready on 26 September
1903. On the 29th they began a house for [[person:Peder Ristvedt]] and
[[person:Gustav Juel Wiik]] to live in, 250 feet away on the same ridge; it took
sixty packing cases and was finished on 17 October, whereupon it was christened
the Villa Magnet. Last came *Uranienborg*, the astronomical observatory: Hansen,
the astronomer, wanted a rotunda, so the crew built him a snow house, and an
empty barrel served as the instrument pier.
[[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-057|It stands here in the winter dark]].

Not a scrap of iron went into the magnetic house. The self-registering variation
instruments began recording on 2 November 1903 and ran for nineteen months
without a break. Wiik, who had trained at the Magnetic Observatory in Potsdam,
attended them — in Amundsen's words — quite single-handed, changing the paper on
the registering drum every noon, often ploughing through driving snow a yard
deep to do it. Amundsen and Wiik took the absolute observations between them,
and when spring came and the load outgrew two men, Hansen was taught the
instruments and brought in. Ristvedt ran the meteorology, three readings in
twenty-four hours with recording instruments going day and night. None of it was
reduced in the field. It went home in the ship and was worked up in Norway
afterwards, which is exactly how a modern research cruise handles its data.

By the second winter Amundsen was running two magnetic observatories, and the
second one is the best single illustration of what these two winters were really
about. One was Wiik's, built of old sails and tarpaulins in summer and covered
over with snow when the cold came. The other, about a hundred yards north,
Amundsen had built as a large igloo, with thick ice windows let into all the
walls for daylight, and the instrument standing on a snow socket. It weighed
66 lbs, and Amundsen recorded that no movement of it was observed during the
whole of the spring. A European geophysicist had asked Netsilik builders for a
laboratory and got a better instrument pier than a barrel. The two huts were
within calling distance of one another so that simultaneous readings could be
taken. See [[page:what-the-netsilingmiut-taught-them]].

## Towards the pole, April–May 1904

On 6 April 1904 Amundsen and Ristvedt set out by sledge up the east coast of
Boothia, travelling in company with [[person:Teraiu]] and
[[person:Kachkochnelli]] and their families, who were going to Abva (Mount
Matheson) to catch seals. Teraiu's wife [[person:Kayaggolo]] — "the old Eagle" —
went two hours ahead, lost her way on the ice, and arrived exhausted at the same
time as everyone else, after which the ever-polite Ristvedt gave her a seat on
his sledge. It was 22° below zero and the first day of the year they felt any
warmth in the sun. [[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-049|The first sledge party out of
Gjøahavn]] had gone a month earlier, in March.

They got as far as a station a little south of the
[[place:Tasmania Islands]] and turned back on 7 May. Amundsen believed they had
"probably passed over" both magnetic poles, the 1831 position and the current
one, without being able to fix either. He meant to go on to Victoria Harbour and
take a comparison series where the two Rosses had wintered with the *Victory* in
the 1830s — "possibly even more interesting than at the Pole itself" — and did
not, because his left foot gave out and laid him up from 12 to 18 May.

Then, on 21 May, they reached their depot on the Boothia coast and found it
stripped: eleven pounds of pemmican scattered on the ground, and a sledge gone.
Amundsen names [[person:Kaumallo]] and [[person:Kalakchie]]. Ten cakes of
pemmican, a couple of packets of chocolate and a little bread had to carry two
men and their dogs back to the ship, which they reached on the evening of
27 May. "So our trip was not a brilliant success," he wrote. A year later the
sledge came back — left out on the ice near the Netsilik camp rather than handed
over, which Amundsen read as fear of what he might do. [[person:Anton Lund]]
repaired it and pronounced it better than new.

He is honest, on the whole, about theft going both ways and about his own
severity. When two men each took a tin from the ship he had them brought before
him, spoke severely, and forbade them ever to come aboard again; they "slunk
away very sheepishly". He names them: [[person:Teraiu]], whom he calls his great
wizard, and Teraiu's brother Tamoktuktu. He also records, carefully, that the
offenders were all Ogluli — the neighbouring group he distinguishes from the
Netsilingmiut — and that none of the Netsilingmiut had taken anything.

## The rest of the two years

The routine that made this possible was food. [[person:Adolf Henrik Lindstrom]]
cooked fresh reindeer, seal and fish and baked bread all winter, and the *Gjøa*
came through three Arctic winters without a case of scurvy —
[[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-011|the cook with a catch of fish]] and
[[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-054|the cook with his specimens]] are not incidental
photographs. Lund and [[person:Helmer Hanssen]] were the professional hunters;
one boat trip in September 1903 brought back twenty reindeer carcasses from
ground near Booth Point, and on the same trip Lund and Godfred Hansen sailed far
enough south-west to establish that the land south of the station was an island
after all — not charted by M'Clintock, who, Amundsen supposed, had passed it in
a fog. Through the following spring Godfred Hansen and Helmer Hanssen built
cairns on every summit and knoll and charted a good deal of the Neumayer
Peninsula from the permanent base.

There was [[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-048|the first Christmas Eve on board in
1903]] and a long winter of visits:
[[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-068|Netsilingmiut came aboard constantly]], and
Amundsen and Hansen took as many as thirteen guests to sleep on the cabin floor
on cold nights, the crew of the fore-cabin declining on grounds of smell. In the
summer of 1904 [[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-069|the deck was a meeting place]].
Beside the ship, [[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-058|snow houses stood in the valley
the crew named after Lindstrom]], and
[[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-056|a camp on the sea ice was photographed by
moonlight]].

In April 1905 Godfred Hansen and Ristvedt left on the expedition's largest piece
of original survey: the unmapped east coast of Victoria Island, north to Cape
Nansen, where they built a cairn and left a document on 26 May 1905. They were
out eighty-four days, from 2 April to 25 June, on stores for fifty, and covered
800 miles. On the way back they worked over the land John Rae had sighted in
Victoria Strait and found it to be one group in a mass of small islands, which
they charted and named the [[place:Royal Geographical Society Islands]] — a
chart that mattered to the ship four months later. Amundsen printed Hansen's own
account as the supplement to volume II of [[source:amundsen1908northwest]].

The ice did not let the ship out in the summer of 1904. It did in 1905: the
windlass turned at three in the morning on 13 August. What happened next is
[[page:the-passage-west-august-1905]].
