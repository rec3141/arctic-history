The Northwest Passage was made on 26 August 1905. The [[vessel:Gjoa|*Gjøa*]]
anchored off Nome on 31 August 1906. In between lies the third winter, which
cost the expedition its only Norwegian life.

## Stopped by the ice

The ship worked west from [[place:Nelson Head]] and on 3 September 1905 came up
to [[place:King Point]] on the Yukon coast at noon, to find, as
[[person:Roald Amundsen]] had been warned an hour earlier, the ice lying close
in and no way past it for the present. They made fast to the solid ice outside a
wreck. "Little did we dream then that King Point was to be our residence for the
next ten months."

The wreck was the San Francisco schooner [[vessel:Bonanza|*Bonanza*]], run
ashore days before to stop her sinking, and her second mate, the Norwegian
[[person:Christian Sten]], had rowed out to meet them. Building began at once
and the expedition's house was roofed in by 15 September;
[[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-074|the wreck, the Gjøa and the house stand together in
one plate]], each lettered with its name. Sten's own house, fifty yards west,
was built of planks and boards off the *Bonanza* and looked, Amundsen said, "for
all the world like a villa residence": two rooms, the inner one for Sten, his
wife [[person:Kataksina]] and their small daughter Anni, the outer one a shared
kitchen where the harpooner [[person:Jimmy]] and his wife [[person:Kappa]] had a
bed by the stove and [[person:Neiu]] and his wife were berthed on a trapdoor
directly above it — a warm berth on baking days. [[person:Kunak]] built his house
alongside Sten's and covered it with turf.

So the third winter was spent as a mixed Norwegian, Inuvialuit and American
settlement: "we were twenty souls all told encamped together here for ten
months". [[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-075|The masts and huts along the shore]] and
[[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-076|Kunak with his family outside their tent]] are what
it looked like. The pattern of the first two winters held. Kataksina sewed
Amundsen's boots and travelling clothes. Neiu guided
[[person:Godfred Hansen]] and [[person:Peder Ristvedt]] on a two-month hunting
expedition to lay in fresh meat. The magnetic and meteorological series went on
uninterrupted, in a variation house Wiik and Ristvedt built two hundred yards
from the dwelling, with the road to it marked by poles against snowstorms.

## The ride to Eagle

Amundsen could not wait a year to tell the world. At six in the morning on
21 October 1905 he set out for the nearest telegraph — Fort Egbert, at
[[place:Eagle]] on the Yukon River in Alaska, roughly 800 km away over the
Richardson Mountains and up the river — and the
[[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-002|route he took]] is on the map. He went first to
[[place:Qikiqtaruk]] (Herschel Island), where
[[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-077|the American whaling fleet lay five ships abreast]]
and several hundred people were wintering; Sten, Neiu and
[[person:Manni]] came that far with him, and Manni, whose legs hurt after the
trip, was given a seat on a sledge going back to King Point. From Herschel
Amundsen went on with Captain Mogg of the wrecked *Bonanza*, with Jimmy and
Kappa as far as Fort Yukon, and with the winter mail carriers, on ski, snowshoe
and dog sledge, at temperatures that eventually brought the telegraph wires
down.

He came in off the river ice in early December, drove into the town, and sent
his message from the telegraph office inside
[[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-082|Fort Egbert]], held by two companies of the US 3rd
Infantry. "I despatched my precious telegram, which was only just in time, as
shortly afterwards the wires broke down, owing to the intense cold."

Then he waited two months for the answer, as the guest of Frank N. Smith of the
Northern Commercial Company, whose household he remembered with unqualified
warmth. He started north again on 3 February 1906 and was back aboard at six in
the evening on 12 March, bringing newspapers and letters for everyone; he found
Godfred Hansen had kept the meteorology and Wiik the magnetic work going without
interruption in his absence. It is 1,600 km of winter travel to send one
message — and the point of the *Gjøa* expedition's whole method is that by 1905
Amundsen could do it as a matter of routine.

## Wiik

[[person:Gustav Juel Wiik]] was born at Horten in 1878 and had trained at the
Magnetic Observatory at Potsdam. He had attended the self-registering
instruments at [[page:two-winters-at-uqsuqtuuq|Gjøahavn]] single-handed for
nineteen months, and he was still taking observations at King Point. He sits
with Godfred Hansen and [[person:Anton Lund]] in
[[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-070|one of the two named portraits of the crew]].

He fell ill on 26 March 1906 with acute pain in the right side. Amundsen took it
for pleurisy and treated him out of Uckermann's *Medical Guide* — there was no
doctor, and the nearest was at Qikiqtaruk. On 30 March Amundsen wrote in the log
that Wiik was making good progress towards recovery, with his appetite improving.
That night he had a rigor, and in the morning his pulse was irregular. Amundsen
went to Jimmy to send him for the doctor; a gale was blowing from the north-west
and Jimmy would not start into it. They settled that he would go the next
morning.

> [[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-031|"At about 5 p.m. … the bell rang very loudly, all
> of a sudden. There was something in the ring that spelt disaster, and I ran
> off at full speed. But I was only just in time to see our dear friend breathe
> his last. It was an inexpressibly sad moment. I closed the eyes of our late
> comrade, and we remained sitting there for a while in silence and sorrow."]]

Sten went to Herschel Island a few days later and came back with the news that
sending for the doctor would have been useless: he was working night and day on
a pair of poisoning cases.

Lund had the black-painted coffin ready on 3 April. It was set on two stools in
the outer room of the house, the lid screwed down and a flag laid over it, and
it stayed there five weeks while the crew moved into the ship and nailed the
house up, because the ground was frozen too hard to dig.

On 9 May 1906, at half past ten in the morning, with every flag at half mast,
the coffin was drawn on a sledge to the magnetic observatory Wiik had built with
his own hands and been fond of, on open ground facing the Arctic Sea. Amundsen
said his farewell and read the Lord's Prayer. The coffin was placed on two small
wooden stools and covered with a Norwegian flag; the room was filled with
driftwood and walled up. Later that summer they raised a high cross on the north
side, turfed the grave and covered it with flowers, and the American whalers
promised to tend it every year.

On 6 May, three days before the burial, Amundsen had handed the mail carriers
returning from Herschel Island an open telegram announcing the death, with a
covering letter to Mr Firth, the manager at Fort McPherson, asking him to send
it off at once and to advance the money. His reason was precise and awful: he
did not want Wiik's mother to hear from some other source that all was well with
the expedition before she heard the truth from him. The mail from the fort came
into Herschel Island in August with nothing in it from Fort McPherson. The
telegram never arrived.

## Getting away

The whaling fleet came east in the second week of July: three ships sighted in
the open water on the evening of 10 July, the *Alexander* and the *Jeannette*
working in on the 11th. The *Gjøa* left King Point a day or two later — the
narrative gives no day; she was at Herschel Island at half past two in the
morning of the 13th — waiting only on
[[person:Adolf Henrik Lindstrom]]'s last ovenful of bread. She sailed with three
more aboard than she had wintered with: two men engaged from the whalers, the
Norwegian Ole Foss and a young American named Beauvais, and Dr Wight from
Herschel Island, who had asked for a passage south. Kataksina came aboard the
wreck of the *Bonanza* with Anni on her back, and Kunak and Neiu with their
wives and sons, to see the ship off. Passing the grave, the crew lowered the
flag to half mast.

At Herschel Island the whaling skippers advised them to lie a fortnight before
going on. Amundsen ignored the advice, met ice, and had to come back and take
it. Manni — the young Netsilingmiut who had joined the ship at
[[place:Uqsuqtuuq]] the summer before, whom Godfred Hansen had taught to read
and to tell the time, and who had once left the ship to live with
[[person:Manichya]]'s family and come back hollow-cheeked within weeks —
drowned there, shooting ducks, in the summer of 1906.

## Nome

They rounded Point Barrow some time between 9 and 24 August 1906; Amundsen dates
neither. Off Cape Prince of Wales the expedition drank a simple toast to the
final accomplishment of the passage by ship, without hoisting a flag, because
the gale would have blown it to tatters — and then the gaff broke for the last
time. That, not a plan, brought the *Gjøa* in to [[place:Nome]] on the evening
of 31 August. A launch came out in the dark flying the Norwegian flag beside the
Stars and Stripes; as Amundsen and Godfred Hansen were brought ashore under an
electric searchlight, "a jubilant roar of welcome issued from a thousand
throats", and then *Ja, vi elsker dette landet*.
