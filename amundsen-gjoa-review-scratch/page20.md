The first leg of the [[vessel:Gjoa|*Gjøa*]]'s voyage, from [[place:Christiania]]
to [[place:Beechey Island]], took ten weeks and very nearly ended the expedition
twice inside three days. It is told in chapters II and III of
[[source:amundsen1908northwest]], volume I.

## A departure at night, in the rain

The *Gjøa* cast off on the night of 16 June 1903, in torrential rain, with only
the crew's nearest relations on the quay. There were seven men aboard —
[[person:Roald Amundsen]], [[person:Godfred Hansen]], [[person:Anton Lund]],
[[person:Helmer Hanssen]], [[person:Peder Ristvedt]],
[[person:Gustav Juel Wiik]] and [[person:Adolf Henrik Lindstrom]] — in a 47-ton
sloop with a 13-horsepower paraffin motor. They had sat for the camera before
they left: [[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-041|the seven of them, named in the plate]].

The night sailing is usually explained by Amundsen's debts: he had mortgaged
himself to fit the ship out, and a creditor was threatening to have her stopped.
Amundsen himself says only that the last week of enforced idleness had worn
everyone down and they were glad to go. Both can be true. What is certain is
that the expedition left with no fanfare at all, and that the contrast with the
send-off Franklin's ships got in 1845 is the whole story of the *Gjøa* in
miniature: a small ship, a small crew, no navy behind it.

## Greenland: dogs, skins and Knud Rasmussen

The *Gjøa* worked up the Greenland coast to
[[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-042|the harbour at Godhavn]],
[[place:Qeqertarsuaq]] on Disko Island, a town of 108 people in 1903, where she
took on dogs and sledges. Amundsen served out thick underclothing — Iceland
jerseys and "Nansen" clothing — to every man, and most of the crew bartered for
sealskin clothing on top of it. The expedition photographed
[[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-043|two Kalaallit women in full West Greenlandic dress]]
there; Amundsen's printed caption for them is "Eskimo beauties from Godhavn".
The ship sailed on 31 July with bunting on the public buildings and a salute
from the battery on the hill. The dogs came from two sources and did not agree
about it: Amundsen writes of "the old *Fram* dogs and the new ones from Godhavn"
settling their quarrels in a battle royal on the ice whenever the ship stopped.

North of Upernavik they crossed Melville Bay — the stretch Amundsen called the
most difficult in the whole Northwest Passage for a ship as small as his — and
got through without mishap in the first half of August. Then, on 15 August, off
Wolstenholme Island, the lookout called two kayaks ahead. The two Inughuit men
who came alongside could not make themselves understood until one of them
grinned and said a single word: "Mylius!" A rattle of gunfire went up from
behind an ice mass and six more kayaks shot out, one flying a Norwegian flag and
one a Danish one. It was the Danish Literary Expedition, and the men who climbed
aboard were [[person:Ludvig Mylius-Erichsen]] and [[person:Knud Rasmussen]] —
the same Rasmussen who, twenty-eight years later, would publish
[[source:rasmussen1931netsilik|the fullest ethnography of the Netsilingmiut]]
that exists. Mylius-Erichsen gave Amundsen four dogs, two grown and two puppies;
the puppies were named [[animal:Mylius]] and [[animal:Gjoa]], and *Gjoa* was,
Amundsen says, undoubtedly the best dog they had. See
[[topic:greenland-inughuit]].

## The depot on Dalrymple Rock

At [[place:Dalrymple Rock]], a bare cone of rock rising straight out of the sea,
the Scottish whaling captains Milne and Adams had landed a depot for the
expedition and walled it round with barbed wire. Mylius-Erichsen handed over
their letter wishing the *Gjøa* luck. There was no harbour, so the sloop lay
unprotected while a day and a half of loading went on — 105 cases, averaging two
and a half hundredweight each, all of it deck cargo.

She left at half past two in the morning of 17 August carrying 4,245 gallons of
petroleum, with the deck down to the waterline and cases piled almost to the
main boom, the dogs perched on top of them. This is worth holding on to:
everything that follows in the next fortnight — a fire and a grounding — is
about a very small, very overloaded ship full of fuel.

## Beechey Island, 22–24 August 1903

The *Gjøa* anchored in Erebus Bay at nine in the evening on 22 August. Amundsen
and Wiik began the magnetic observations the next morning, and they decided the
route: the dipping needle came to rest south-west, so the ship would go down
Peel Sound and not west towards Melville Island. The observations were finished
about noon on the 24th and the ship left at one o'clock. In between, Amundsen
walked up to [[topic:franklin-1845|Franklin's]] graves and to the marble slab
M'Clintock had set at the foot of the Belcher column, with Bellot's tablet in
the same column, on the beach where Franklin's ships had spent the winter of
1845–46. They found everything in order and re-erected the one gravestone that
had fallen.

> [[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-027|"I was sitting on one of the chain lockers
> looking towards the land with a deep, solemn feeling that I was on holy
> ground, Franklin's last safe winter harbour. My thought wandered back — far
> back."]]

He was about to sail, in a herring boat, past the place where those ships were
lost. See [[topic:franklin-search]] for what the searchers found here in 1850.

## The fire

A week out of Beechey Island, down Peel Sound and into the shoal water east of
King William Land, the ship was working in fog, with the charts wrong and the compass slowing as the
magnetic pole came near. On 31 August she touched a bank off the southernmost of
the Beaufort Islands at eleven in the morning and got off under sail and engine,
losing splinters of her false keel. Amundsen sat up that night writing the day
into his journal.

> "I heard a shriek — a terrific shriek — which thrilled me to the very marrow."

Flame and smoke were coming out of the engine-room skylight, in a compartment
holding 2,200 gallons of petroleum, and every man aboard knew what would happen
if the tanks heated. Wiik stayed at his post below. Two extinguishers and hand
pumping put it out; the fire had started in cleaning waste lying soaked in
petroleum on the tanks. [[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-044|The plate is captioned
simply "The Fire"]].

The margin was one order given hours earlier. Ristvedt had reported a full tank
leaking and Amundsen had told him to drain it into an empty one, at once. In the
fight with the fire the tap of that now-empty tank was wrenched clean off. Had
it still been full, over 100 gallons of petroleum would have poured into the
flames.

## The reef off Matty Island

The narrative gives no calendar date for what came next, but the mornings can be
counted from the journal entry of the 31st. At four the next morning,
1 September, they ran south along the coast and anchored at eleven under a low
island north of [[place:Matty Island]] to sit out a gale. They weighed again at
four on the morning of 2 September, and at six the *Gjøa* went hard aground on a
submerged reef — the third bank she had struck in two hours, and this one had
not a fathom of water over its shallowest part against a loaded draught of ten
feet two inches. The reef ran out of sight westward. A north gale was rising.
[[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-045|The plate shows her hard over with her sails set]].

They lightened her: twenty-five cases of dog pemmican of nearly four
hundredweight each went over the side, and the rest of the deck cargo was thrown
to one rail to heel her. She would not move. Through the following night the
gale rose and she began to pitch on the rocks, the false keel splintering and
floating up. At last Amundsen came down from the rigging and gave the order to
clear the boats and load them with provisions, rifles and ammunition. It was
Lund, standing nearest, who asked whether they might not make one more attempt
by throwing the remainder of the deck cargo overboard. Every man agreed at once,
and cases of four hundredweight went over the rail like trusses of hay.

> [[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-028|"She was lifted up high and flung bodily on to
> the bare rocks, bump, bump — with terrific force …. In my distress I sent up
> (I honestly confess it) an ardent prayer to the Almighty. Yet another thump,
> worse than ever, then one more, and we slid off."]]

Godfred Hansen was at the wheel when the rudder jammed and, a wave later, fell
back into its mountings. They anchored at noon under Cape Christian Frederik.
From that hour, Amundsen wrote, the *Gjøa* never made another quarter of a mile
of the Northwest Passage without one man aloft and another plying the lead.

Nine days later she anchored in the harbour at [[place:Uqsuqtuuq]] and stayed
nearly two years: [[page:two-winters-at-uqsuqtuuq]].
