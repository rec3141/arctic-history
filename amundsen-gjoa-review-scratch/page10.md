On the evening of 16 June 1903 a 47-ton herring sloop cast off from
[[place:Christiania]] in torrential rain, with only the crew's nearest relations
on the quay. She was the [[vessel:Gjoa|*Gjøa*]], built at Rosendal in 1872 — the
year [[person:Roald Amundsen]] was born — and she had spent twenty-eight years
fishing herring before he bought her. Seven men were aboard, and a 13-horsepower
paraffin motor. Amundsen was deep in debt, and the night departure has been read
ever since as a flight from his creditors; he wrote only that a week of enforced
idleness had tired everyone out. What she was is set out exactly in the
expedition's own drawing of her:
[[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-040|sail plan above, deck plan below, 47 tons]].

Three years and two and a half months later, on 31 August 1906, the same sloop
anchored off the open beach at [[place:Nome]] in Alaska. She had done what
[[topic:franklin-1845|Franklin]], [[topic:mcclure-investigator|McClure]] and a
long line of naval expeditions in far larger ships had not: sailed the Northwest
Passage from the Atlantic to the Pacific in a single hull. The whole route, as
Amundsen's own dates give it, is
[[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-001|the track on the map]].

## What the voyage was actually for

Amundsen was insistent that the passage was the by-product. The stated object
was magnetic: to re-observe the North Magnetic Pole, which
[[topic:ross-victory|James Clark Ross]] had fixed on the Boothia coast in 1831,
and to find out how far it had moved. That is why the *Gjøa* stopped where she
did. On 12 September 1903 she came into a small harbour on the south-east coast
of King William Island — [[place:Uqsuqtuuq]] (Gjoa Haven) — with Simpson Strait
open to the west and the passage lying clear ahead of her. Amundsen anchored
anyway. The magnetic pole was ninety miles off, and he wanted a fixed station,
not a dash. The expedition's own plan of the harbour,
[[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-047|with the observation stations marked]], shows where
the work was done.

The self-registering instruments started recording on 2 November 1903, in a hut
built without a scrap of iron in it. [[person:Gustav Juel Wiik]] attended them,
in Amundsen's words, quite single-handed, changing the paper on the registering
drum every noon for nineteen months without a break; he and Amundsen took the
absolute observations between them, and
[[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-057|the observatory on the ridge]] stood beside the
ship. That series is the reason one winter became two. It is also, for anyone
aboard this ship, the oldest piece of continuous geophysical observation in
these waters that was done on the modern plan: instruments left running, an
unbroken record, the analysis deferred to a laboratory at home.

## The three winters

Two winters at Uqsuqtuuq, from September 1903 to three in the morning of
13 August 1905. Then thirteen days west, and on 26 August 1905, off
[[place:Nelson Head]], the lookout's shout that
[[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-029|"Vessel in sight, sir!"]] — the San Francisco
whaler [[vessel:Charles Hanson|*Charles Hanson*]], working east.
[[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-066|The meeting is the frontispiece of volume II]]. A
ship from the west meant the passage had been sailed.
[[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-030|Captain McKenna]] shook Amundsen's hand and said he
was exceedingly pleased to be the first to welcome him through.

The *Gjøa* did not reach the Pacific that year. Ice stopped her at
[[place:King Point]] on the Yukon coast on 3 September 1905, and she wintered a
third time beside [[vessel:Bonanza|the wreck of the whaler *Bonanza*]] —
[[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-074|the two ships and the expedition's house]] stand
together in one plate. Amundsen could not wait to tell the world, so he
[[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-002|rode 800 km to the telegraph]] at
[[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-082|Fort Egbert]] in [[place:Eagle]], Alaska, and
800 km back. It was at King Point, on 31 March 1906, that Wiik died — the
expedition's only death among the Norwegians, though [[person:Manni]], the young
Netsilingmiut who had joined the ship at Uqsuqtuuq, drowned shooting ducks at
[[place:Qikiqtaruk]] (Herschel Island) within weeks of the end.

## The part that mattered most

The magnetic observations were the object. The passage was the fame. But the
thing the *Gjøa* expedition actually carried away from the Arctic, and the thing
that decided a race at the other end of the earth, was an education — and the
teachers were the [[page:what-the-netsilingmiut-taught-them|Netsilingmiut]].
Over two winters at Uqsuqtuuq the crew learned to drive dogs, to cut and set the
blocks of a snow house, to wear caribou skin sewn by Netsilik women rather than
wool, and to eat fresh meat all winter. Every one of those is standard equipment
at Framheim in 1911. [[person:Helmer Hanssen]], who learned dog driving at
Uqsuqtuuq, was Amundsen's lead driver at the South Pole: he and Amundsen are the
only two men who went through the Northwest Passage and then stood at the pole.

## Why this ship carries the name

[[vessel:CCGS Amundsen]] is named for him. She was launched in 1978 as the
*Sir John Franklin*, laid up in 2000, and refitted and recommissioned in 2003 as
Canada's dedicated Arctic research icebreaker under Amundsen's name — a renaming
that swapped a man whose expedition of 129 left no survivors for a man who
brought all his own countrymen home but one, and who got through by taking Inuit
instruction seriously. That is worth saying plainly on a vessel doing Arctic
science with northern communities today. See
[[page:gjoa-afterwards-and-ccgs-amundsen]].

Uqsuqtuuq is not a historical site. It is
[[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-024|a hamlet of about 1,350 people]], with the
[[artifact:amundsen-gjoa-025|Nattilik Heritage Centre]] holding the Netsilik
side of this history, and it was Inuit oral history — carried by
[[person:Uchyuneiu]] to Amundsen in 1905, and by Louie Kamookak and Sammy Kogvik
of Gjoa Haven to the searchers a century later — that finally located both
[[topic:wrecks-disasters|Franklin wrecks]].
